About this guide

What this is, what it is not, where the material came from, and how you may use it. Worth two minutes before you rely on it for an exam.

What this is

An independent study companion for ISACA Advanced in AI Security Management (AAISM), organized around the public exam content outline, checked on 20 October 2026. The three domain weights are 31%, 31% and 38%. The coverage map connects all 13 subtopics and 22 supporting tasks to chapters, worked evidence and practice questions. This is author-created instruction, not a reproduction of the official manual or a guarantee of exam readiness.

How to read this book

This is not a summary of the AAISM syllabus. Summaries are useful once you already understand something; they are almost useless before that. This book explains the ideas.

The certification sits on top of a CISM or CISSP, which means ISACA assumes you already think in terms of governance, risk appetite, control design and program management. What it does not assume — and what most candidates are genuinely missing — is a working mental model of how machine learning systems are built, where they break, and why the security properties you rely on elsewhere quietly stop holding.

So the book front-loads that. Part II spends six chapters on foundations before touching a single exam domain, because almost every difficult question on this exam is difficult for the same reason: it describes a situation where an ordinary security instinct gives the wrong answer, and only a real understanding of the machinery tells you why.

After that, the parts follow the domains in ISACA's own order and weighting. Read them in sequence the first time. The material genuinely builds: you cannot reason about AI incident response until you understand what a model is, and you cannot design AI controls until you understand the lifecycle they attach to.

Chapter One

What the exam actually tests

After this chapter: Use the public outline and a diagnostic to plan study without converting scaled scores into percentages.

Ninety multiple-choice questions in two and a half hours, scored on a scale from 200 to 800, with 450 as the pass mark. None of those numbers means quite what it appears to.

Start with the scale, because candidates routinely misread it. A scaled score is not a percentage of questions answered correctly. ISACA uses scaling so that scores remain comparable across different versions of the exam, which contain different questions of differing difficulty. Sit an easier form and you need more raw marks to reach 450; sit a harder one and you need fewer. The consequence for you is practical: there is no fixed number of questions you must get right, so there is no point trying to compute one. Aim to answer everything competently rather than to clear an imagined threshold.

Ninety questions in 150 minutes averages 100 seconds each, before a review reserve. Practise allocating roughly 135 minutes to a first pass and 15 to revisiting flagged items. Read the requested decision and its qualifier, then identify the scenario facts that change the answer. The public outline does not specify a fixed stem length or a universal answer-selection algorithm.

What the three domains are for

Domain 1, AI Governance and Program Management (31%), is about advising the organisation: policy, data governance, program structure, and incident response. Chapter Two

Thinking like the examiner

After this chapter: Choose the next action from harm, authority, obligations and completed work.

Scenario questions may offer several plausible actions. Choose the one that answers the stated question, fits the known facts, respects the role’s authority and meets mandatory obligations. The rules below are reasoning aids, not inside knowledge of exam marking.

Distinguish identifying a useful action from identifying the next justified action. Record what is already known, which controls already exist and whether harm is active; these facts can reverse an otherwise sensible choice.

Understand before you act

When the situation is uncertain and there is no identified immediate harm, establish purpose, data, exposure and impact before selecting a treatment. When a credential is actively exfiltrating data or a safety limit has been crossed, use authorized containment and escalation while gathering evidence. Assessment and action can run together.

Worked through

A business unit starts using an AI assistant that quietly appeared inside an already-approved SaaS platform. Security was never consulted. The options offered are: block it at the perimeter, determine what data it processes and why the business is using it, escalate the policy breach, or mandate awareness training.

Assume this example has no known active disclosure or immediate safety issue. Determining purpose and data is then the best-supported next step. If monitoring instead showed ongoing confidential-data leakage, restricting the feature under the response plan would be justified immediately; the investigation would continue in parallel.

Governance precedes technology

Policy and stakeholder decisions establish objectives and authority. They do not displace a required operational action when governance is already established or harm is ongoing. Choose the missing prerequisite, not a governance option merely because it sounds senior.

Risk belongs to the business

Residual risk is accepted by a named owner with delegated authority, within appetite and the law. Security advises and may also operate controls or hold explicit stop/acceptance authority for defined risks. A job title alone does not determine authority. Neither a business executive nor a committee can waive a legal prohibition.

Where people go wrong

For an opaque high-impact decision, identify the required explanation, applicable legal limits and approval authority. Options may include an interpretable model, redesigned workflow, reduced scope or rejection. Escalating uncertainty is sensible where the use is lawful and the decision is delegated; knowingly unlawful processing cannot be approved as ordinary residual risk.

Match the control to the stage

Identify when the attack acts and when a defence must be implemented. These are different axes: adversarial training prepares a model to resist inference-time evasion; training-time differential privacy can reduce later membership-inference exposure. Runtime restrictions can contain damage while a compromised dataset or model is repaired.

Choose proportionate treatment, including avoidance

Avoidance is a legitimate treatment for a particular use that is unlawful, unjustified or beyond tolerance. A blanket ban can also impose costs and encourage undiscovered use, so compare feasible alternatives. The objective is an authorized, evidenced decision about value and harm, not minimum risk regardless of business purpose.

What comes next

Chapter 4 sorts the vocabulary the office model will carry.

Every explanation in this book runs through one picture. It is worth ten minutes now, because it will save you a great deal of memorisation later.

The difficulty with AI security is not that the ideas are hard. It is that the vocabulary arrived faster than the intuition. Embeddings, retrieval, agents, tool calling, the context window — each term gets defined in isolation, and the definitions sit in the reader's head as unconnected facts. Then an exam question describes a situation, and the facts do not assemble into a judgement.

So this book uses a single working model throughout. An AI system is an office. Not because the metaphor is cute, but because many LLM application security properties have useful office parallels, and the equivalents are load-bearing rather than decorative. When you can see where a component sits in the office, you can usually derive the control rather than recall it.

Why this particular model works

Because offices already solved most of these problems, badly at first, over about a century — and the solutions are the ones we are now rediscovering.

An office does not secure an employee by making them incapable of error. It secures the situation around the employee: what the badge opens, what the spending limit is, who countersigns, what the clerk checks before handing over a file. Nobody writes a handbook clause saying "do not be deceived" and considers the matter closed, because everybody understands that a sufficiently convincing forgery will fool a competent person on a busy afternoon.

That is exactly the posture AI security requires, and exactly the one organisations fail to adopt. They try to make the model incapable of error — better filtering, stricter prompts, more guardrails — and treat the surrounding authority as a detail. The office model makes the mistake visible: you have hired someone capable and persuadable, and you have handed them the company card without setting a limit.

Why this exists

The analogy joins three related ideas: prompt injection can cross an intended instruction boundary, agents need least privilege, and prompt instructions alone cannot enforce authorization. Models can distinguish roles and source markers to varying degrees; that learned distinction is not a reliable substitute for an external permission check.

The parts of the office

A short tour, in the order the rest of the book uses them.

The employee is the model. Competent within their training, confident by disposition, and — this is the property everything else follows from — unable to reliably distinguish a genuine instruction from a convincing forgery.

Training is the employee’s earlier learning and fine-tuning is further learning. Neither is an ordinary file store: deleting a source document does not undo a trained model’s influence. Retraining and specialized unlearning methods exist, but their effectiveness and cost need evaluation.

The filing cabinet is the retrieval corpus and the clerk is the retrieval service. The vector database is a catalogue that may also hold the text chunks, metadata and source permissions. Protect all of them at the sensitivity of the source, and check entitlements before context reaches the model.

The handbook is the system prompt. Employees usually follow it. It is not a lock.

The conversation and papers represent the context window. Applications can mark system instructions, user messages and retrieved content differently. The model may nevertheless follow an instruction from a lower-trust source, so retain those distinctions and enforce consequential boundaries outside the model.

A badge is identity and the doors it opens are authorisation. When you give the employee a badge, a phone and a company card, they become an agent, and their mistakes stop being memos and start being transactions.

Tool calling and connector protocols help explain how agents gain external capability. Protocol-specific implementation details are optional context; do not infer new official exam objectives from them. Their data, authorization and supply-chain issues map to the public architecture and control tasks.

Where the analogy is honest about its limits

Two places, and knowing them prevents over-reading the model.

An employee has continuity of self; a model does not. Each request is a new employee who has read the papers on the desk and remembers nothing else, unless you have deliberately given them a notebook — which is what persistent agent memory is, and why poisoning that notebook is a durable attack rather than a one-off.

An employee can be held accountable. A model cannot. This is the point at which the analogy must be dropped deliberately rather than extended, and Chapter 9 returns to it: treating the system as an agent in its own right is precisely how organisations end up with nobody answerable for what it did.

A situation

A European insurer receives a routine erasure request. A former customer wants their data deleted. The privacy team has handled hundreds of these: locate the records, delete them, confirm, close the ticket. Twenty minutes of work.

This time the data protection officer asks a question nobody has asked before. Eighteen months ago, the claims team fine-tuned a model on historical claims to predict settlement values. That customer's file was in the training set.

The engineer's first answer is that the record has been removed from the dataset. The DPO asks whether the model still knows it. Nobody in the room can say. Nobody, it turns out, can even say what "removing it from the model" would mean.

The assumption that just broke

For the whole history of business computing, data and logic have been separate things. A program is instructions; a database is records. You can point at a record, read it, change it, delete it, and prove afterwards that it is gone. Every data protection regime, every retention schedule, every backup policy and every deletion workflow your organisation owns is built on that separation. It is so fundamental that it usually goes unstated.

Machine learning couples learned behaviour with training-data influence. Parameters can encode patterns and sometimes specific information, without a simple per-record address as in a database. Deleting a dataset row does not undo that influence in an existing model. Remediation may involve clean retraining, specialized unlearning or a justified change in use, with evaluation of what remains.

In the office: you did not file the claims history in a cabinet. You spent eighteen months training an employee on it. The training worked. And now you are being asked to make that employee selectively forget one afternoon of it.

Why this exists

Training changes parameters and can encode information about examples. There is no generic database-style delete operation on arbitrary weights. Full retraining without the affected records and specialized machine-unlearning methods are possible; the latter have model-specific assumptions, utility costs and verification limits. Plan deletion before training rather than assuming either perfect forgetting or universal impossibility.

Four consequences, from one mechanism

This is the highest-leverage paragraph in the book, so it is worth reading slowly. From the single fact that training absorbs data into weights, all of the following follow without needing to be memorised separately.

Deleting the source is not enough. Trace affected datasets, models and derived stores. Assess full retraining, a suitable unlearning method or changes to use, with privacy/legal review and evidence of residual exposure. Output suppression can reduce disclosure but does not by itself establish legal erasure. Chapter 23 covers the wider data estate.

Minimise before training. Avoid learning information the task does not need. Later remediation can be expensive and incomplete; evaluated retraining or unlearning remains possible.

Poisoning may persist in model behaviour. Runtime restrictions can contain harmful outputs or actions, but do not prove the learned cause is removed. Repair the data/pipeline and validate a clean retrain or other model-specific remediation before restoring trust.

Sensitive training data can resurface in outputs. Not by retrieval, but by the same mechanism that produces everything else: the model generates what its training makes likely, and if a record was distinctive enough, reproducing it is likely.

Think like an attacker

Training information can be attacked through legitimate query interfaces: distinctive completions, membership signals or functionality-copying attempts. Authentication, behavioural monitoring, privacy testing and query controls can help detect or reduce exposure; conventional host intrusion monitoring alone may miss it.

The second broken assumption: determinism

Traditional software executes instructions. Given the same input and state, you get the same output, and when you do not, something is broken and you fix it. Testing works because behaviour is reproducible; incident response works because a defect can be identified and patched.

A model may make probabilistic predictions yet produce repeatable output under fixed inputs and decoding. Sampling can introduce variation. Evaluate statistical performance over representative cases and repeated runs where needed, while testing deterministic application invariants such as denied access or spend limits. Those metrics and invariants support clear pass/fail release criteria.

Define acceptance thresholds, test-data independence, uncertainty and documented sign-off. A successful demonstration is some evidence, but not enough to establish general performance. Define separately which harmful events or aggregate deviations trigger an incident response.

Reality check

Theory: hallucination is a defect that better models will eliminate.

Reality: fluent generation does not guarantee factual correctness. Training, retrieval and validation can improve accuracy, but residual errors need measured controls and appropriate oversight. Confabulation is a failure to evaluate and mitigate, not proof that improvement is impossible.

Where your data ends up: two architectures

Fine-tuning and retrieval are two common, complementary techniques. Fine-tuning adapts behaviour through parameter updates; RAG supplies selected context at inference. A system can use both, with different data-protection consequences.

Fine-tuning changes model parameters and may improve task behaviour. It can introduce memorization and leakage risks, so record data rights, model versions and evaluation evidence. Sensitive facts that need frequent updates or per-user document permissions often belong in a governed retrieval layer instead.

RAG retrieves context at query time. That need not update weights, but the retrieved content is still processed by the inference service and may persist in provider logs, caches, traces or agent memory. Control every processing location and propagate source deletion and permission changes to derived stores.

Embeddings are an index, not a disguise

Retrieval works by converting text into embeddings — long vectors of numbers placing semantically similar content near each other. Because they look like meaningless numbers, people treat them as though the sensitivity has been removed.

It has not. Embeddings can be partially inverted to recover approximations of the source, and in any case the entire purpose of the system is to find the original content and return it. The card catalogue is not the books. It is also not a security measure: it is a complete map of where every book is, which is why it is worth stealing.

Exam trap

If the stated threat is a user retrieving documents they are not authorized to read, enforce requester entitlements before retrieval context reaches the model. Encryption at rest addresses a different threat. Both can be required; choose the control that addresses the question’s specific failure.

What changes when the employee gets a badge

An agent is a model given tools, memory and some autonomy — able to call an API, send a message, write to a system or run code, often in a loop. This is the largest single shift in the risk profile, and the office makes why immediate.

A model that produces text can already cause harm if people trust misleading advice or publish it. Tools add direct execution capability and can amplify that harm. Bound both access to information and authority to act, and verify outcomes independently where the stakes warrant it.

Prerequisites

What comes next

Chapter 8 asks how much of this lifecycle you actually control — which for most organisations is very little.

A situation

Two Calder engineers build a model that predicts pump failures from vibration data. It works. Maintenance costs fall, the engineers are commended, and the model quietly becomes part of how the plant is run.

Fourteen months later, false alarms are climbing. A manager asks the obvious question — is it still working? — and discovers there is no answer. Nobody wrote down what "working" meant. There was no threshold, no held-out test set kept for comparison, no record of which data version produced this model. The engineers have moved teams.

Nothing was hacked. No control failed. There was simply never a gate.

The assumption that just broke

Conventional software has a release gate whose meaning everyone shares: the tests pass or they do not. That single fact organises the entire delivery process. Change management, sign-off, rollback, and post-incident review all reference it.

A model needs an explicit release gate combining statistical criteria and deterministic safeguards. Record what is measured, which data and model versions were tested, how uncertainty is reported and who approves promotion. A demonstration without these elements is insufficient evidence for a production decision.

Why this exists

The lifecycle is not project-management scaffolding. It exists because risk enters an AI system at specific, identifiable points, and the control that addresses a risk almost never sits at the same point as the symptom. Poisoning enters at stage two and shows up at stage six. Rights problems enter at stage one and become legal exposure at stage seven. Learn the stages and you gain a filing system that makes Chapters Sixteen and Twenty-one derivable rather than memorisable.

Seven stages, and what each one lets in

1 · Sourcing data. The questions here are legal and procedural rather than technical: do we have the right to use this for training? That is four separate questions — consent, contractual permission, licence and copyright, lawful basis — and they can have four different answers for one dataset. It is also the first point at which poisoning becomes possible, and the point at which provenance must begin. Provenance reconstructed later is not provenance.

2 · Preparing and labelling. Labelling is frequently outsourced to a distributed workforce, and it is a genuine supply chain exposure: whoever assigns the labels shapes what the employee learns. Corrupt the labels and you corrupt the model without touching a single sample. The controls are unglamorous — vetted vendors, inter-annotator agreement, review of edge cases — and are frequently the correct exam answer.

3 · Training or fine-tuning. Isolated environments, access control over data and compute, secrets management, pipeline integrity — plus one AI-specific requirement: reproducibility. Recording the dataset version, configuration and seeds is what later lets you prove which data produced which model, and rebuild it if something is found to be wrong.

4 · Validation and evaluation. Held-out test sets, adversarial and robustness testing, fairness tested across subgroups rather than in aggregate, safety evaluation, red teaming — and documented acceptance criteria agreed beforehand.

Think like an architect

Evaluation is the release gate, and a gate has three properties the Calder engineers never gave it: criteria set in advance, a result recorded against a named model version, and someone whose sign-off is required. Strip any one and it stops being a control. If a scenario describes a model reaching production without all three, that is the finding — regardless of how good the surrounding architecture looks.

5 · Deployment. Signed artefacts, a registry with version control, staged or canary release, authentication and rate limits at the endpoint, and a tested rollback path. Version pinning matters more than in conventional software, because behaviour can change substantially between model versions in ways no changelog captures.

6 · Operation and monitoring. Where the world begins to diverge from the training data. Two distinct phenomena get flattened into "drift": data drift, where the inputs change distribution, and concept drift, where the relationship the model learned stops holding. Fraud models suffer both — fraudsters change tactics, and the customer population changes.

Common misconception

Misconception: a degraded AI system will announce itself the way a failing service does.

Reality: it stays available, responds quickly, and returns confident answers that are increasingly wrong.

Correct mental model: a stopped clock is obviously broken and misleads nobody. A clock losing four minutes a day is consulted, trusted, and wrong — and stays that way until someone checks it against something else. Conventional IT failures are stopped clocks; model degradation is the slow clock. Detection has to be deliberate, and continuity planning needs a trigger for "available but no longer trustworthy."

Chapter Nine

Stakeholders and the governance structure

After this chapter: Define decision rights, accountable owners and proportionate independent challenge.

AI governance fails in a recognisable way: security builds a control framework, the business routes around it, and by the time anyone notices, the models are already making decisions. The structural fix is the subject of this chapter.

The reason AI governance cannot be run as a security project is that the decisions it needs to make are not security decisions. Whether to automate a hiring screen is a business and ethical judgement. Whether a training corpus may lawfully be used is a legal one. Whether an error rate is tolerable depends on the process owner. Security can convene, advise, assess and monitor — it cannot decide most of what needs deciding.

Four families of roles

A tidier way to hold the cast than a long list. Leadership and strategy — executives, any chief AI officer, the steering committee. Development and operations — those who build and run the solutions. Users and their support — including HR and customer service. Governance and oversight — the governance committee, risk, security, privacy and internal audit.

The governing body's own duty is distinctive: it sets goals beyond financial return to include culture, values and ethics, and it is accountable for everything the organisation does — accountability that cannot be handed off. It decides whether a use of AI fits the risk appetite, stays informed about both possibilities and dangers, and regularly reviews how AI is managed, supervised, evaluated and reported.

Where people go wrong

Anthropomorphising the system. Talking about a model as though it decides, intends or knows is convenient shorthand that quietly relocates responsibility away from people. Boards are specifically cautioned against it, because a body that half-believes the AI is an agent in its own right will not hold anyone accountable for what it does.

The charter and the steering committee

An enterprise AI governance charter establishes mandate, scope, decision rights, delegations, escalation and independent assurance. A project charter separately defines one initiative’s objectives, milestones, budget, risks and sign-off. The steering committee operates within its enterprise mandate rather than inventing authority for each project.

The AI steering committee is the cross-functional body that keeps initiatives aligned, makes the calls on budget, scope, risk and ethics, and reviews policy on AI use, data handling and bias. Typically chaired by a senior leader such as the CIO, CTO or chief data officer, with project sponsors, domain experts, technical experts and compliance, meeting monthly or quarterly.

Readiness and governance develop together

A question worth asking before any of the machinery below is whether the organisation is ready to run AI at all. An AI readiness assessment looks at five things: whether the data is fit for purpose and lawfully usable; whether the skills exist internally or must be bought; whether the infrastructure can support training or inference at the scale intended; whether governance structures and policies exist to hold it; and whether the culture will actually adopt what is built. Readiness gaps do not stop AI arriving — they determine which of the failures in this book you get first. An organisation strong on infrastructure and weak on governance produces shadow AI. One strong on governance and weak on data quality produces well-documented models that do not work.

A structured readiness self-assessment goes wider than those five. The governing body should be able to answer, for a proposed programme: is there clear value and return; is it aligned to business strategy; is the organisation ready to operate it; is risk management in place; are regulatory obligations understood; is the use ethical; are privacy and security handled; is bias mitigation planned; are legal and contractual obligations mapped; is it financed realistically; is the project planned; and are the people there. Each area is then rated on a simple scale — fully in place, largely, partially, not at all, or unknown.

Key idea

"Unknown" is the most valuable rating on that scale and the one organisations quietly convert into "partially" to avoid an awkward conversation. An honest unknown is a finding. It tells you where to look before deployment rather than after an incident.

The ten components of a working structure

A governance model that survives contact with an organisation has these parts. Learn them as a set, because questions frequently describe a structure with exactly one missing.

  1. A charter — mandate, scope, authority, decision rights and escalation, approved at executive level. Without it the committee is a discussion group.
  2. A cross-functional review board that approves use cases, sets thresholds and adjudicates exceptions.
  3. Named roles — model owner, data owner, system owner, risk owner, human overseer. Ownership must be an individual. "The AI team" is not an owner.
  4. An intake process that every proposed use enters, including AI features inside purchased software.
  5. Risk tiering that maps a use case to a control set and an approval level.
  6. Impact assessment for the higher tiers.
  7. An inventory — the single source of truth, covered in Chapter 11.
  8. Policies and standards, covered in Chapter 10.
  9. Metrics and reporting to the board.
  10. Time-bounded approval with defined re-review triggers. Nothing is approved forever.

Chapter Ten

Appetite, strategy and policy

After this chapter: Translate appetite into tolerances, operating thresholds and controlled exceptions.

Business strategy, legal duties and risk appetite inform policy, standards and procedures iteratively. Identify the missing decision in a scenario; do not repeat an already approved governance step. A policy may also need immediate revision when obligations or exposure change.

Appetite describes the broad kinds and amount of risk the organization is willing to pursue. Tolerance specifies acceptable variation against objectives, and operational thresholds translate those limits into monitored actions. Set the relevant decision authority and comply with mandatory requirements regardless of appetite.

What risk appetite looks like when it is done properly

Not a sentence about being risk-aware. A set of decisions, made in advance and signed off by people with authority:

  • Which uses are prohibited outright, and stated explicitly rather than left to inference
  • Which require board or committee approval, and which a business unit may self-approve
  • Quantitative thresholds where they can be set — acceptable error rate, maximum disparity between demographic groups, maximum level of autonomy, minimum proportion of decisions subject to human review, spend ceilings
  • The triggers that force reassessment

Thresholds make selected tolerances operational, while legal prohibitions and qualitative constraints also need verifiable checks. Record what is measured or reviewed, by whom, against which limit, and what happens when it fails.

Think of it this way

“Drive safely” is too vague to test alone. A speed limit gives a measurable threshold; a requirement to stop at a red light gives a categorical constraint. AI governance needs both: numeric limits where justified and explicit required/prohibited actions with evidence of enforcement.

What belongs inside a business case

Five elements at minimum: the problem the AI is meant to solve; how the solution solves it; the expected return, based on a cost-benefit analysis; the risks and how they will be handled; and a detailed implementation plan.

Costs fall into three groups that are easy to under-count: implementation (development, licensing, training staff), operational (maintenance, energy, new salaries), and risk and compliance (privacy protection, reputation management). Benefits recur in three forms: efficiency and automation, better decision-making from large volumes of data, and cost reduction through lower labour and error costs — each of which must be stated as something measurable.

Two expectations worth setting with leadership. Early returns often look poor because upfront costs land first and people are still learning the tool; a slow start is not a failed project. And human factors — employee satisfaction, customer experience — count as returns even though revenue and cost are easier to measure.

Key idea

Prefer a proven solution to an unproven, fast-moving one. An established approach comes with costs and benefits already understood from real experience; something brand-new arrives loaded with high expectations and no evidenced path to meeting them. This is a security argument as much as a delivery one, because immature technology is where the undocumented failure modes live.

Procedures: where policy becomes action

Policy states intent; procedures, manuals and playbooks are what turn it into consistent daily behaviour. Existing ones mostly still apply — the trap is assuming AI is just another system and changing nothing. Three things force revision. AI consumes data at volume, so procedures must cover how data is collected, handled, protected, cleaned and standardised for training and validation. AI carries ethical and human-rights dimensions that older procedures never anticipated. And AI procedures reach across the whole organisation, not just the technology function, which means they must align with the acceptable use policy rather than sit beside it.

Think of it this way

Organisations increasingly draft these procedures using AI itself, which is efficient and carries an obvious hazard: an unreviewed AI-generated procedure is a control that nobody actually designed. Scope the request properly, validate the draft against existing documents and standard practice, and circulate it for human review. Garbage in, garbage out applies with particular force when the output is the thing everyone will follow.

Strategy, and what it has to answer

An AI strategy exists at three levels: national, aligning AI with a country's values, laws and security; industry, promoting innovation and shared standards within a sector; and corporate, pursuing advantage while staying compliant. Yours sits at the third and is shaped by the first two.

The OECD AI Principles underpin much of the vocabulary that later appears in law, and come in two groups. Five values-based principles for trustworthy AI: inclusive growth and wellbeing; respect for human rights and the rule of law; transparency and explainability; robustness and safety; and accountability. Five recommendations to governments: invest in research; foster an inclusive AI ecosystem; provide sound governance; build human capacity; and cooperate internationally.

Value alignment means designing AI to behave consistently with human values — and those differ by organisation and country, so it is not a setting you inherit. Four elements shape it: community feedback gathered over time, ethical foundations respecting cultural traditions, legal compliance with regional law, and operational strategy through continuing stakeholder engagement.

On build versus buy, six questions decide it: how total costs compare; whether you have the data, skill and time to build something as good as you could buy; the regulatory risk of each path; which option fits your current operating model; the data-privacy challenge of each; and the danger of vendor lock-in. On-premises gives control at higher cost; cloud scales easily at the price of control and with lock-in risk.

What makes a policy actually work

Seven conditions separate a policy that changes behaviour from one that sits on an intranet. Senior management support, stated plainly inside the document rather than assumed. Strategic alignment with objectives, culture and regulation. Clarity and consistency of language. Feedback and consensus from the stakeholders who have to live with it. A real communication and training plan. Ongoing compliance monitoring. And periodic review, at least annually or after any major change.

The standard components: an introduction showing management backing and the link to corporate strategy; a glossary; a purpose statement; a clear scope; and a principles section — the operational rulebook covering authorised uses, prohibited uses, responsible use, transparency, fairness, third parties, governance, training, security, compliance monitoring, and the date of last review.

Eight steps before writing an acceptable use policy

The AUP is fundamentally a communication tool: its job is to remove guesswork about where the line is. The preparation matters more than the drafting. Understand the technology, including model types, data sources, biases, limits and public-versus-private deployment. Assess organisational needs and research legal and regulatory requirements for your industry and region. Conduct a risk assessment covering technical and ethical risks. Confirm purpose and scope so staff know which systems are covered. Examine existing technology and security policies for rules you can reuse. Engage stakeholders. Adopt a governance framework for ownership and assurance. And prepare communication for internal and external audiences — customers and regulators also want to understand your intended uses and safeguards.

Think of it this way

An acceptable use policy works like the posted rules at a public swimming pool. The water is there for everyone to use, and the rules about diving, lanes and supervision are what keep the pool open. A policy that reads as a prohibition list makes people avoid the water; one that makes the safe path obvious gets them swimming.

Classification needs a second dimension

Key idea

Traditional classification asks how sensitive data is. AI governance also has to ask what it may be used for. A dataset can be internal-only and still be prohibited from training a model whose outputs are externally visible — because the sensitivity level and the permitted use are different questions. Add a permitted-use dimension to your classification scheme: may this be used for training, for retrieval, for externally-facing output?

Where the lifecycle breaks traditional data governance

At sourcing, assess legal rights and permitted use. At training, information can persist after source deletion, so plan evaluated retraining or unlearning where relevant. Where people go wrong

Forgetting that prompt logs are a data store. Prompt and output logs need classification, retention rules and access control like any other repository.

Chapter Twelve

Running the program

After this chapter: Distinguish coverage, control operation and outcomes in program reporting.

An AI security program is an extension of the information security program, not a parallel one. Building it separately is itself a finding.

The components are the ones you would expect: a strategy tied to the organisation's AI ambitions and its appetite; defined scope across systems, jurisdictions and suppliers; resourcing and skills; integration points into the software lifecycle, procurement, change management, privacy, incident response, continuity and audit; a control framework; a maturity assessment and roadmap; metrics; awareness and role-based training; and a continuous improvement loop. What differs is what you measure.

Where this is heading

Board-level AI oversight is being formalised — added to audit or risk committee remits, with named executive accountability. Expect the metrics in this section to be asked for by people who are not technologists, which is an argument for choosing measures that survive being read out loud without explanation.

Trust but verify

The organising principle of an AI security programme, and a phrase worth carrying into the exam. Because AI systems are open to manipulation — jailbreaks that slip past a model's own rules, malicious content planted in a model, providers who change what they ship — every output that matters must be validated by something other than the model that produced it. That means real review and approval mechanisms for AI-generated work, not an assumption that the vendor has handled it.

Someone has to own this

Even where no senior AI role exists, a named AI lead should track how the organisation's relationship with AI is evolving and maintain the plan for it. The role is coordinating rather than technical, working across cybersecurity, privacy, legal, procurement, risk and audit, and keeping the documented history of what the organisation has adopted and why. Its real function is to stop AI governance from being everybody's concern and nobody's job.

Audits and traceability: three questions

An auditable evidence chain covers purpose, lawful use, data origin and transformations, model/configuration version, release evaluation, approval, control operation and outcomes. Provenance and bias evidence are necessary parts; they do not replace the rest.

Key idea

Adapt the security programme before investing heavily, not after. Three things specifically: intellectual property leakage prevention through access permissions, visibility tooling and application controls; disaster recovery, incident response and continuity planning extended to AI; and AI-specific threat intelligence. Retrofitting these to a live estate is materially harder than building them into the adoption plan.

Two directions, and both must be covered

A framing worth carrying into the exam because questions hinge on it. Security for AI is protecting the AI systems themselves — the models, their training data, their intended behaviour; defending against prompt injection is security for AI. AI for security is the reverse: using AI to strengthen defence, such as sifting network activity for the faint signal of an intrusion. A complete programme covers both directions, and a scenario that only addresses one has a gap by construction.

The question that comes first

Define measures from the organization’s objectives and duties, including harm to people, reliability, business value and operational risk. Report harms explicitly so healthy adoption or throughput numbers cannot conceal them.

Key performance indicators track operational health and value: compliance, data quality, accuracy, reliability, downtime, business impact, how often the model is retrained, adoption, return, and satisfaction. Key risk indicators are the smoke alarms, and three types stand out — disparity metrics measuring differences in outcomes across population groups; fairness metrics checking positive outcomes are distributed equitably; and frequency metrics counting how often users flag biased output against the expected rate.

Chapter Thirteen

AI risk in the enterprise picture

After this chapter: Aggregate AI dependencies into enterprise exposure without losing local ownership.

One of ISACA's supporting tasks asks you to participate in the AI risk lifecycle including its impact on enterprise risk. That last clause is doing real work, and it is the subject of this chapter.

A specialist AI risk register is useful if its owners, controls and reporting integrate with enterprise risk. Aggregate the exposure into operational resilience, legal compliance, privacy, safety and other enterprise objectives; parallel reporting becomes a problem when it prevents that view.

Think of it this way

A separate AI risk register is like a household keeping the electricity bill in a different drawer from the rest of the finances. Every individual entry is accurate, and nobody can answer the only question that matters: can we afford this?

Aggregation is the hard part

Individually, forty low-tier AI use cases may each be genuinely low risk. Four compliance considerations to run every use case past

A compact checklist that catches most of what follows, and that is easy to recall under exam pressure. Confidentiality — can users expose trade secrets or proprietary material through this system, deliberately or by habit? Privacy — is personal data protected by design, with an assessment completed before go-live rather than after? Intellectual property — what are the rights in the training data, and who owns what the system produces? Employee rights — where AI touches hiring, monitoring, evaluation or termination, does it stay within employment law and within what you could defend to a tribunal?

Legal exposure that does not fit the security categories

Three legal risks belong in the enterprise picture and are easy to miss because they sit outside the usual security taxonomy.

Rights in training data. Whether you may use a corpus is four separate questions — consent, contract, licence and copyright, and lawful basis — and they can have different answers for the same dataset. Litigation and regulatory action in this area is active and unsettled.

Ownership and originality of outputs. Whether AI-generated material can be owned, and by whom, varies by jurisdiction. If output is going into a product you intend to protect, this is a question for legal before it is a question for security.

Licence terms on models themselves. Many openly available models carry licences that restrict commercial use, restrict use in particular domains, or impose obligations on derivatives. "Open weights" is not "open licence," and a model pulled from a public hub can carry conditions your organisation is not willing to accept. Licence review belongs in model selection, alongside provenance.

In the wild

Public regulatory actions concerning scraped facial images, and litigation about training material, illustrate that data rights can create enterprise exposure. Outcomes and jurisdictional reach differ and may change. The hardest question in AI incident response

Define which harmful events trigger immediate action and which aggregate deviations require review. A single unauthorized disclosure or serious safety event can be an incident even when overall model performance remains within its statistical threshold. Classification depends on impact and obligations, not merely whether errors are expected.

Key idea

Defining that threshold in advance is what makes AI incident detection possible at all. Without it there is no line to cross, and the organisation oscillates between ignoring genuine failures as "just how AI behaves" and escalating ordinary error as a crisis. The threshold is a governance decision made before deployment, not a judgement call made at 2am.

Observability is what makes the threshold usable: defined metrics, a baseline of normal performance, and — because no single signal settles a borderline case — a human in the loop to judge the subjective ones.

Where people go wrong

Assuming malicious data is as visible as data loss. Traditional monitoring is tuned to detect information leaving. Poisoning is information arriving, through a legitimate pipeline, looking like every other record. Nothing in a conventional detection stack is watching for it, which is why change logs on the data supply chain are a security control rather than a data-engineering nicety.

Who is on the team, and how you know the plan works

An AI incident response team needs roles a conventional one does not. Alongside the incident manager, security responders, legal, privacy and communications, you need someone who can read the model's behaviour — a data scientist or ML engineer able to say whether an output is a bug, drift, poisoning or normal variance. You need the model owner, who can authorise a rollback. And for third-party models you need a named contact at the provider, established before you need it, because discovering the support path during an incident costs hours you do not have.

Tabletop exercises are how you find out whether any of this works, and AI scenarios expose gaps that generic exercises miss. Four worth running: the model produces a discriminatory decision that a customer has publicised; an agent takes an unauthorised action against a production system; confidential data appears in an output to the wrong party; and the provider silently changes the model, degrading a control you depend on. Each tests a different muscle — communications, containment, notification, and detection respectively.

Using AI in incident response, and its limits

The syllabus asks about AI-assisted response as well as response to AI incidents. The benefits are real: faster triage across high alert volumes, correlation across sources a human would not connect, drafting of timelines and summaries, and consistency at three in the morning. The limits are equally real: false positives at scale, explanations that cannot be evidenced to a regulator, dependence on a system that may itself be affected by the incident, and — the one that matters most — an incident-response agent holds standing privileged access, making it the highest-value target in your estate. Everything in the agents chapter applies to it with more force, not less.

Assessing: the questions, in order

Assessment establishes three things — timeline, scope and impact — as fast as possible without damaging the integrity of the investigation. The order of the questions matters more than people expect.

  1. Is harm active or imminent? Identify the authorized containment and safety action while preserving evidence and tracking reporting obligations. Do not defer a required notification or safety measure merely to complete a question sequence.
  2. Who was impacted, and what harm occurred?
  3. When was it discovered? This date carries legal weight — notification deadlines run from it. Establish and document it early.
  4. What actually happened, which AI systems and data were involved, and what tactics were used?
  5. What is still unknown, and what do we need to know to return safely to normal operation?

Good model documentation earns its keep here: it gives investigators a reference for what normal looked like before the incident. Without it, the team is trying to establish both what changed and what the baseline was, simultaneously, under time pressure.

Multiple clocks run at once

A single AI incident can trigger privacy breach notification on one deadline, sector regulator reporting on another, contractual customer notification on a third, and AI-specific serious incident reporting on a fourth. They have different scopes, different triggers and different timeframes. This is why the correct answer to "who must be notified" is usually to determine obligations across all applicable regimes, rather than to name one.

Where this is heading

AI-specific incident reporting regimes are arriving, and public incident registers already exist and are being cited by regulators and researchers. Two consequences: your incidents may become externally visible on someone else's timeline, and the taxonomy you use internally should map onto the ones being standardised.

The post-incident review, and where to point it

Improvement, not blame — and the unit of analysis is the whole system rather than the failure point. Six areas to examine: how the data was preprocessed, since pipeline problems are a frequent root cause; whether the security controls held; whether the adversarial testing was thorough enough, which is a different finding from a control failure; the controls on model inputs and outputs; the fairness of those outputs; and the AI provider's own control environment. That last one is routinely skipped, and skipping it leaves an entire layer of the system unexamined.

The overarching aim is a posture shift — from reacting to trouble toward readiness that limits the impact of the next one. An incident that produces a report and no change in investment has not been reviewed; it has been documented.

How AI-assisted response actually works

Where an organisation adopts AI-powered incident response, the pipeline is worth knowing because each stage carries its own failure mode. The system ingests and normalises data from many sources; applies pattern recognition to detect anomalies; correlates events across sources to reveal multi-stage attacks a single feed would hide; automates triage by severity; accelerates root-cause analysis; automates response actions such as isolating a host or blocking an address; and learns from each incident.

The benefits are genuine — faster detection and response, quicker root cause, fewer false alarms, scale without headcount, and lower cost. Four challenges balance them, and they are the examinable half: accuracy and trust, because the system is sometimes confidently wrong; human oversight, to keep automated response aligned with business objectives and ethics; an evolving threat landscape that demands continual model updates; and AI bias inherited from incomplete or skewed training data.

Good practice when automating: integrate with existing tooling rather than building a parallel stack, use customisable playbooks tailored to your environment, let the system prioritise dynamically by severity, give humans and the system proper collaboration tooling, build compliance checks into the workflow itself, and review performance after every incident. And remember that automated response is itself regulated activity — logging, transparency and reporting obligations apply to it as much as to anything else.

Chapter Fifteen

Why AI risk behaves oddly

After this chapter: Write a scoped risk scenario with inherent exposure, treatment and residual acceptance.

The risk process is the one you already know — identify, analyse, evaluate, treat, monitor, communicate. The inputs are what change, and they change in seven specific ways.

Predictions may be probabilistic and generation may be sampled, while many deployments are repeatable under fixed conditions. Risk assessment still uses specifications, deterministic safeguards, statistical performance, uncertainty and impact; match the evidence to the failure being assessed.

Behaviour is emergent. Capabilities and failure modes appear that nobody designed and nobody anticipated. You cannot enumerate the failure modes in advance the way you can for a rules engine, which is why continuous evaluation replaces one-time certification.

The system is opaque. You may be unable to explain why a particular decision was made. That is not merely inconvenient — in regulated contexts it is itself a compliance exposure, because the obligation to explain a decision to an affected person does not disappear because the mechanism is hard to interrogate.

Risk originates upstream and outside. The properties of a model are largely determined by data and training you did not perform, at an organisation you do not control, at a time before you were involved.

Errors replicate instantly at scale. A flawed human decision affects one case. A flawed automated decision affects every case until someone notices, which is a fundamentally different impact profile from most IT risk.

Non-security harms are in scope. Bias, safety, environmental impact and societal harm sit alongside confidentiality, integrity and availability in this syllabus. A risk assessment that only considers harm to the organisation is incomplete by the exam's standard.

Assessments go stale quickly, because the provider changes the model beneath you.

Key idea

Trust is the organising concept of this whole domain. It is also the currency of any deployed system: once lost, a tool gets abandoned however capable it is, and no amount of accuracy buys it back. That is why AI risk cannot be assessed on technical performance alone — a model can be accurate, secure and available, and still be untrusted because it is unfair, unexplainable or unaccountable. Each attribute of trustworthy AI is testable, evidenceable and challengeable, which is precisely what makes trust a risk-management object rather than a slogan.

Identifying risk: the five-step sequence

AI risk is always contextual — the same model carries different risk across a whole hiring process, one stage of a supply chain, or casual use in a productivity tool — and the context keeps moving as the technology and its attackers evolve. The sequence that makes it tractable: map the mission and objectives of the AI programme; determine which products and services deliver it; identify the vital assets, including technology, processes, vendors and data; establish an AI value chain linking them; and assess impact through concrete loss-event scenarios.

Loss-event scenarios are what turn abstract risk into something a business owner can weigh. "The model might be biased" invites a shrug; "if this model rejects qualified applicants from one group at twice the rate, here is the regulatory, legal and reputational exposure" invites a decision.

Appropriate and inappropriate risk

Acceptance must be lawful, within appetite/tolerance, assigned to an authorized owner and periodically reviewed. Cost-benefit analysis informs the decision but does not override mandatory controls or prohibited uses. Record the assumptions and conditions that would reopen acceptance.

Impact assessment is not risk assessment

Key idea

Risk assessment can include organizational and stakeholder harm. AI impact assessment makes the effects on individuals, groups and society explicit: who is affected, what may go wrong, whether oversight is meaningful and how an outcome can be contested. Coordinate these views rather than treating people’s harms as outside ordinary risk management.

Coordinate it with the privacy impact assessment rather than running two overlapping processes. They ask different questions but need much of the same evidence.

Treatment, with one important caveat

The four options are familiar. Avoid — do not use AI here; a deterministic rules engine may be the correct answer for a high-stakes decision. Mitigate — guardrails, human oversight, narrower scope, reduced autonomy, better data. Transfer — contractual indemnity and insurance. Accept — documented, time-bound, owned by the business, monitored.

Where people go wrong

Believing transfer works the way it does for financial loss. An indemnity moves money. It does not move regulatory accountability, and it does nothing at all for reputation. If a model your organisation deployed produces a discriminatory outcome, "our vendor is liable" is not an answer to the regulator or to the person affected. This principle — accountability cannot be outsourced — recurs constantly across Domain 2.

Think of it this way

An airline that outsources catering has not outsourced food poisoning. The caterer may pay the bill afterwards, and the passengers were still on your aircraft, the regulator still writes to you, and the headline still has your name in it. Indemnity settles the invoice. It does not move the event.

Fundamental rights impact assessment

Under Article 27, a FRIA applies to specified high-risk uses and deployers, including public-law bodies, private entities providing public services and the relevant creditworthiness and life/health-insurance uses. It includes exceptions, including the referenced critical-infrastructure category. Determine scope and the applicable date from the law before asserting a filing duty.

Its content is prescribed in substance if not in form: how and for what period the system will be used; the categories of people likely to be affected; the specific risks of harm to those groups; the human oversight arrangements; and what happens — governance, complaint routes, remedies — if the risks materialise. If you read that list and it sounds like the impact assessment from the last chapter, you are right. The difference is that one is good practice you scope yourself, and the other is a statutory instrument with a defined trigger, a defined owner and a filing obligation.

Conformity assessment

The conformity assessment is the compliance demonstration for a high-risk system: an examination of the provider's quality management system and technical documentation to show the system meets legal requirements before it is placed on the market. It can be conducted internally or by an independent body depending on the system, and it must be repeated after any substantial modification.

Key idea

This distinction matters when determining responsibility. Conformity assessment is the provider proving the product is compliant. FRIA is the deployer assessing what happens when that product is pointed at real people. Different parties, different questions, and a deployer who has been shown a conformity assessment has not thereby discharged its own obligation.

Privacy impact assessment

A privacy impact assessment is a broad practice; a data protection impact assessment (DPIA) is a specific legal instrument where its statutory high-risk trigger applies. Identify controller/processor roles, lawful basis, necessity, proportionality and safeguards. AI can introduce inferred sensitive attributes, biased outcomes and barriers to exercising rights.

Where people go wrong

Use shared intake and evidence, then produce only the assessments required by the risk and applicable law. Record separately each trigger, accountable party, scope, decision and review/notification duty. A supplier conformity assessment does not automatically discharge a deployer’s impact obligations.

Putting risk in money

Everything so far in this part has been qualitative: tiers, appetite, thresholds. That works until you are in front of a board choosing between an AI investment and something else, at which point qualitative risk loses every argument to a number.

FAIR analyzes probable loss frequency and magnitude with uncertainty, including relevant primary and secondary losses. The following is a generic decision workflow in which FAIR analysis can be used, rather than a claim that these are its canonical five process stages:

  1. Contextualise. What decision is this analysis for? An analysis with no decision attached will not be used.
  2. Scope. Which asset, which threat, which effect. Vague scope produces uninterpretable numbers.
  3. Quantify. Estimate probable loss for each scenario — as a range with stated confidence, not a single figure.
  4. Prioritise and treat. Rank by likelihood and financial impact, and select treatments.
  5. Decide. Invest where the risk reduction per unit of spend is greatest.
Think of it this way

Qualitative assessment can be useful when evidence is limited or decisions concern nonfinancial constraints. Quantification can improve comparison where assumptions and ranges are credible. Present the method, uncertainty, severe outcomes and applicable limits instead of claiming either a heat map or a monetary mean settles every decision.

Where this is heading

Quantified AI risk is likely to become more common rather than less, driven by two forces: boards asking for AI exposure in the same terms as everything else, and an emerging insurance market that has to price this risk to sell cover. Both push towards measurable control evidence and away from colour-coded matrices.

Who actually sets the limits

The governing body sets overall appetite and oversees material exposure; it is not synonymous with the organization’s owners. Management may set specific tolerances and approve residual risk within documented delegation. Legal duties remain constraints regardless of the estimated treatment cost.

Chapter Seventeen

The threat landscape

After this chapter: Map attack objectives and stages to preventive, detective and recovery controls.

This is the densest chapter in the book and the highest-yield. Learn each attack by mechanism, and learn the single organising principle that makes the control obvious.

Identify two axes: the stage and objective of the attack, and the stage at which each defence is implemented. Poisoning often enters data preparation; a compromised model may also arrive at deployment. Adversarial training is a build-time defence against inference-time evasion; differential privacy during training can reduce later membership-inference risk. Runtime controls contain effects but do not alone establish eradication.

Think of it this way

The flour-and-plate analogy distinguishes causes entering during preparation from attacks at service time. It does not imply that later inspection is useless: detection, restriction and safe fallback can contain harm. Remove the underlying cause and test recovery separately.

Training-time attacks

Data poisoning alters training or other ingested data to degrade performance or steer behaviour. Its effects may persist if contaminated sources are reused. Provenance, restricted writes, versioned integrity checks, representative evaluation and attack-specific analysis reduce risk; runtime restriction can contain consequences without proving removal.

Backdoors cause selected behaviour when a trigger appears while ordinary use may look normal. They can enter through poisoned training, modified weights or supplied artifacts. Assess provenance and relevant trigger/adversarial tests without assuming a clean normal benchmark proves absence.

Label flipping corrupts the annotations rather than the samples, which is why labelling vendors are a genuine supply chain concern and why label quality review is a security control rather than a quality one.

Supply chain compromise covers malicious models on public hubs, typosquatted packages, backdoored libraries and poisoned public datasets. A related and underrated one is the serialisation attack, where merely loading a model file in an unsafe format executes attacker code. Controls: signed artefacts, safe formats, registry allowlists, dependency pinning, scanning, and an AI bill of materials.

In the wild

In late 2022 a malicious package was uploaded to the public Python index under the name of a PyTorch dependency, and was installed by anyone pulling nightly builds until it was removed. In 2024, security researchers reported finding around a hundred models on a public model hub carrying malicious payloads that would execute when the model file was loaded. Neither attack required touching a model's behaviour at all — they compromised the machinery around it.

Inference-time attacks

Evasion, also called adversarial examples, crafts inputs that cause misclassification — perturbations often imperceptible to a person. Countered by adversarial training, input validation, ensembles, confidence thresholds and anomaly detection on inputs.

Model extraction aims to copy useful behaviour or parameters through queries or other access. Query requirements vary; some attacks are relatively efficient. Authentication, rate/budget limits and pattern monitoring reduce exposure but are not guarantees. Evaluate what output detail and model access the use genuinely needs.

Model inversion infers attributes or representative information about training data; recovery need not be an exact original record. Training-data extraction seeks specific memorized material. Membership inference tests whether a record was included. Depending on the threat, differential privacy, regularization, access limits and constrained confidence detail may reduce risk; evaluate their actual guarantees.

Where people go wrong

Distinguish model extraction, which targets functionality, from training-data extraction and inversion, which target information about the data. Some controls overlap. Rate limits constrain probing; training-time differential privacy limits the additional information contributed by a protected individual or record under its stated adjacency assumptions and privacy budget.

Think of it this way

Stay with the restaurant. Extraction is a rival eating every dish on the menu until they can reproduce your kitchen. Inversion is a chemist working backwards from the finished sauce to your recipe. Membership inference is narrower and sometimes worse: not the recipe at all, just whether your tomatoes went into it — which matters enormously if the dataset was "patients treated for a stigmatised condition."

Prompt injection attempts to redirect a language-model application through its inputs. Direct attacks arrive through the user interface; indirect attacks arrive in material read on someone else’s behalf, such as documents, email or tool results. Relative severity depends on the system’s data and action authority, not merely the entry channel.

Key idea

Prompt instructions, source separation, training and input/output inspection can reduce injection success, but do not provide a general guarantee. Enforce permissions, egress restrictions and consequential action approval independently; protect reads as well as writes. Treat retrieved content and model output as untrusted at the appropriate application boundary.

Think of it this way

Imagine an assistant reading an invoice with a forged instruction to transfer money. Source and role markers can help separate the instruction from legitimate authority, but can fail. Require an independent payment authorization and permitted destination rather than relying on the assistant’s interpretation alone.

Excessive agency is the consequence of ignoring that advice — an agent holding more permission, autonomy or functionality than its task requires. Controls are least privilege on tools, scoped and short-lived credentials, human approval gates for consequential or irreversible actions, sandboxing, egress restriction, and explicit blast-radius limits.

Insecure output handling is the mirror image: model output flowing unvalidated into something that executes or renders it — a database query, a shell, a browser, generated code. The rule is simple and absolute: model output is untrusted input, so encode it, validate it, parameterise it, sandbox anything executed.

Where this is heading

Adversarial tooling is commoditising in the same way penetration testing tooling did: automated attack suites, jailbreak libraries and agent-targeting frameworks that need no research background to run. The realistic effect is not novel attacks but a much larger population of people able to attempt the known ones.

Threat modelling an AI system

A list of attacks is not a threat model. Threat modelling asks four questions in order, and the AI-specific content sits in the answers rather than in the method.

What are we building? Decompose the system into the layers from the architecture chapter — data sources, training pipeline, model, orchestration, tools, interfaces — and mark every trust boundary, including the ones inside the context window.

What can go wrong? Walk each component against the attack taxonomy above, and against non-technical harms. It helps to run the classic categories with an AI reading: spoofing becomes impersonation of a user or an agent; tampering includes the training corpus and the retrieval store; repudiation raises the question of whether your logs can attribute an action to a person or only to a model; information disclosure includes memorisation and embedding inversion; denial of service includes cost exhaustion; and elevation of privilege is what excessive agency delivers.

What are we going to do about it? Map each identified threat to a control, and — the step most often skipped — to the stage where that control has to sit.

Did we do a good job? Validate through adversarial testing rather than review, and revisit when the system changes, which for AI means frequently.

Key idea

Threat modelling for AI must include non-technical threats, and this is where security-trained people under-scope. A model that is never attacked can still produce discriminatory outcomes, fabricate a policy that binds the organisation, or be used by staff in a way nobody sanctioned. Those belong in the model alongside poisoning and injection, because the exam — and the regulator — treat them as the same category of failure.

Two frameworks, two jobs

The OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications is a list of weaknesses for people building and assessing applications: prompt injection, sensitive information disclosure, supply chain, data and model poisoning, improper output handling, excessive agency, system prompt leakage, vector and embedding weaknesses, misinformation, unbounded consumption. Use it as a checklist for what to test.

MITRE ATLAS is a knowledge base of adversary tactics and techniques against machine learning systems, structured like ATT&CK and grounded in real case studies. Use it for threat modelling and detection engineering — for reasoning about how an adversary would actually proceed.

Choosing a threat modelling method

Threat modelling does three things: builds a simplified abstraction of the system, profiles the attackers including their goals, methods and skills, and catalogues the potential threats. The adversary questions that prioritise the output: how often would they encounter this target; how likely are they to be detected; what is the target worth to them; what skills and resources would they need; and how much total effort does the attack demand?

Three attack surfaces

A second lens, complementary to the training-time and inference-time split, and worth carrying because it maps to who owns the defence. Development-time threats arise while the system is being built — poisoning, corrupted labels, compromised components. Runtime threats come from ordinary, non-AI weaknesses in the surrounding infrastructure: the unpatched server, the exposed storage bucket, the over-permissioned service account. And threats through use arise during normal input and output activity — injection, extraction, leakage through responses.

The middle category is the one security teams underweight precisely because it is unglamorous. A great deal of real-world AI compromise involves no AI-specific technique whatsoever; the attacker took the training data from a storage location that was simply left open.

Nontechnical threats you are still expected to own

The syllabus treats these as part of the threat landscape, not as an ethics footnote: hallucination and bias amplification, overreliance on AI by staff who stop checking, job displacement, societal manipulation through synthetic media, brand-damaging incidents, regulatory infractions, privacy intrusion through surveillance capability, rogue or unintended system behaviour, AI-powered cybercrime, and the environmental cost of training and inference in energy and water.

Key idea

Prevention is preferable, but harms can still require correction, redress, compensation, withdrawal of decisions and process redesign. Do not treat ethical failures as either ordinary software patches or inherently beyond remedy.

Vulnerability management, adapted

Continue ordinary patching of the application, serving stack, dependencies and infrastructure. For model-behaviour failures, use evaluation, data/model remediation, configuration changes or replacement as appropriate. Combine vulnerability handling with regression tests and revalidation on material changes; testing only the weights misses much of the system.

What actually changes for the defender

Phishing and pretexting. Generic mass campaigns and hand-crafted spear-phishing used to be different things with different detection profiles. They no longer are: personalised, fluent, contextually accurate messages can be produced at volume, in any language, referencing real organisational detail scraped automatically. Awareness training built around spelling errors, odd phrasing and generic greetings is training people to detect a threat that has retired.

Voice and video impersonation. A short sample of public audio is enough to clone a voice convincingly on a phone call, and video impersonation on a live call is now within reach of ordinary attackers. This matters because a great many organisational controls rest, unwritten, on recognising a person: approving an unusual payment because the finance director asked on the phone, resetting credentials because the voice was familiar, joining a video call and therefore believing who is on it.

Key idea

The control that survives is out-of-band verification against a pre-established channel. Not "does this look and sound right" but "we call back on the number in the directory, we confirm through a separate system, we require a second authoriser for transactions above a threshold, and no exception is granted for urgency." Urgency is the pressure every one of these attacks applies, so the procedure must explicitly refuse to bend to it.

Reconnaissance and target selection become exhaustive rather than sampled — every employee profiled, every public document mined, every relationship mapped, cheaply. Reduce what is publicly inferable about roles and approval authority where you can, and assume the rest is known.

Malware and vulnerability discovery receive assistance rather than transformation. The realistic effect is faster iteration and more variants, which pressures signature-based detection and shortens the window between a vulnerability becoming public and being exploited.

Synthetic identity and account fraud gain from cheap generation of plausible documents, images and histories, which pressures identity proofing and know-your-customer processes that were designed against manual forgery.

The defensive response, in order

  1. Rewrite the procedures that assume recognition. Payment authorisation, credential reset, access approval, executive requests. Anywhere the control is "we knew it was them," specify how that is now established.
  2. Move verification out of band and make it mandatory. Callbacks to directory numbers, confirmation through a separate system, dual authorisation above thresholds, and no urgency exceptions.
  3. Update awareness training to the current threat. Teach the pressure patterns — urgency, secrecy, authority, an unusual channel — rather than the surface tells, and include voice and video, not just email.
  4. Strengthen identity proofing where onboarding or recovery relies on documents or a live human check.
  5. Adopt provenance where it exists — content credentials and signing for material you publish, so your own communications can be verified.
  6. Assume detection will be imperfect and invest correspondingly in limiting what a successful social engineering attempt can achieve: transaction limits, segregation of duties, delays on high-value changes.
Where people go wrong

Reaching for a detection tool as the primary answer. Deepfake and AI-content detectors exist, their accuracy is contested, and they are in an arms race they do not clearly win. A detector is a supporting control. The durable answer is procedural: remove the reliance on recognising a person, and limit what any single deceived person can authorise.

Chapter Nineteen

Vendors and the supply chain

After this chapter: Verify supplier evidence, component responsibilities and usable exit conditions.

Vendor and supply-chain decisions require concrete evidence. Use the questions in this chapter to test whether the service, its dependencies and its contractual commitments support your intended use.

The reason it matters is arithmetic. Most organisations consume AI rather than build it, which means most of their AI risk sits in someone else's environment, governed by a contract rather than by a control. The supply chain here is longer than people assume: the base model and its provenance, the data behind it, pre-trained components and adapters, open-source libraries, the hosting and inference platform, the vector database, the orchestration framework, plugins and tool integrations, data labelling vendors, and every SaaS product that has quietly added an AI feature since you approved it.

The warning signs

General red flags weigh against any supplier and hit harder with AI, because dependence sets in faster: financial instability, regulatory non-compliance, poor security practice, reputational baggage, weak operational resilience.

AI-specific red flags: no transparency or explainability in the model; unacceptable ethical risk; high likelihood of privacy violation; vendor lock-in; immature technology used where health or safety is at stake; no risk management framework that accounts for AI; inability to meet your security requirements; no way to prove the origin of generative content; and resistance to due diligence, auditing or security assessment — which is the one that should end the conversation, because every other concern becomes unverifiable behind it.

Key idea

The single highest-leverage clause to negotiate is advance notice of new AI features. Vendors are adding AI to existing services with or without telling customers, which quietly introduces business, privacy, security and compliance risk into a relationship you assessed years ago. A notice clause converts a surprise into a review trigger. Vet at the start, before dependence makes switching prohibitive.

What to ask, in three groups

About the model. What is its provenance and the lineage of its training data? Is there documented evaluation — a model card, a system card, published safety testing? What are the known limitations and failure modes? How and when do you change versions, can we pin one, and what notice do we get?

About our data. Is our input used for training or improvement, and is opt-out available or default? How long are prompts, outputs and logs retained, and who at the vendor can read them? Where is data processed and stored, and which sub-processors are involved? How is tenant isolation implemented?

About assurance and contract. Is there independent certification or attestation, and — the question people forget — does its scope actually cover the AI service rather than the vendor's corporate environment? What are the incident notification commitments and timelines? What is the liability position, the IP indemnity for outputs, the warranty on accuracy? On exit, what happens to our data, and is there portability and transition assistance? Do obligations flow down to sub-processors? And how are regulatory roles allocated — who is provider, who is deployer, and what information will they supply so we can meet our own obligations?

Worked through

Your fraud detection service runs on a vendor's hosted model. One Tuesday the false positive rate doubles. Nothing in your environment changed.

The vendor updated the model. Everything you validated — accuracy, subgroup fairness, guardrail behaviour, the thresholds in your operating procedure — was validated against a model that no longer exists. Your controls did not fail; they were silently invalidated. This is why version pinning and advance change notification are contract requirements rather than nice-to-haves, and why a model version change belongs on your list of reassessment triggers.

Key idea

Onboarding due diligence is not enough, because the thing you assessed keeps changing. Vendor management for AI must be continuous: reassess on version change, monitor the vendor's incident disclosures, periodically re-test outputs yourself, track certification currency, and watch for changes in their sub-processors.

Where this is heading

Version pinning and advance change notice are moving from unusual asks to standard contract terms, and regulatory documentation duties on general-purpose model providers are starting to push information downstream to deployers. If your current contracts lack these, they are likely to be renegotiable sooner than you think — ask.

What it means to be the deployer

Many organizations consume third-party AI, but their legal role depends on what they develop, supply and use. An organization integrating a model into its own system may have responsibilities beyond purchasing access. The following separates supplier evidence from the customer’s own use duties.

First, buying does not remove the deployer’s own applicable duties. The supplier may carry separate model/system obligations, while the customer remains responsible for its use, data, oversight and impact within the scope assigned by law.

Second, the duty split depends on the actual system/model, use and applicable law. Providers have obligations over what they develop or supply; deployers have obligations over use, monitoring and impacts. Contracts allocate operational tasks and evidence but do not redefine every statutory role.

Third, affected users may contact the organization deploying the system even where a supplier caused the fault. Coordinate support and reporting in advance. One entity can hold different roles for different systems; identify the role for the specific object and use.

Monitoring a vendor's AI in practice

Choose metrics that reflect what the model actually does rather than what is easy to collect. Set logging and alerting thresholds covering output relevance, bias, sentiment and toxicity. Run adversarial tests including jailbreak attempts. Maintain data integrity and input standards. Verify rather than trust — check accuracy and demand sources. Compare results across multiple AI systems where the stakes justify it. Establish a trusted gold image baseline before fine-tuning, so you have a known-good reference to compare against and return to. And balance model size against running cost, because an oversized model is a permanent operating expense as well as a larger attack surface.

Seven faces of third-party risk

AI does not introduce a new category of supplier risk so much as add suppliers and reshape existing relationships. The full set worth screening against: operational risk of disruption or delay; financial risk of the supplier failing; cybersecurity risk of their breach becoming yours; compliance and ethics risk; geopolitical risk from sanctions, export controls or conflict; environmental risk to their operations; and reputational risk from their conduct attaching to your name.

What each side actually does

Beyond the regulatory framing, the operational split that belongs in the contract. The deployer audits and tests privacy and security controls including penetration testing; implements data governance covering quality, integrity, privacy and provenance; enforces identity and access management; builds and tests an AI-specific incident response plan; validates models including adversarial testing for bias and vulnerabilities; manages supply chain risk through vetting and contract terms; and ensures transparency to users through explainable models and output monitoring.

The provider holds relevant compliance and audit certifications; protects training data, model weights, parameters, logs and inference artefacts; runs an incident response plan coordinated with subscribers; develops models responsibly with an ethics framework, secure coding and bias-reduced training data; provides transparency and explainability tooling and documentation; and manages vulnerabilities proactively.

Confusion arises only when nobody wrote down who fixes what. Put service levels, incident response, incident notification and audit rights in the contract, or the duties fall into the gap between the two parties.

The duty split, stated plainly

Under the EU AI Act the provider carries the heavier compliance load for high-risk systems: ensuring the system meets legal requirements, running the conformity assessment, maintaining technical documentation, monitoring performance in the field, and reporting serious incidents to authorities. The deployer's duties are narrower but not light: proper use in line with the instructions, human oversight, transparency to affected people, and keeping logs.

The wider cast

Responsibility is distributed further than two roles suggest. The NIST framing names AI actors across the whole life cycle — designers, data scientists and domain experts at design; developers and integrators in build; operators, evaluators and auditors in use; and governance leaders and boards providing oversight. The exam discipline is to identify which actor, at which stage a scenario is asking about, because the correct answer often hinges on that alone.

Integration risk

Two integration exposures are easy to miss because they arise from what the AI is connected to rather than from the AI itself.

Legacy systems. Connecting a model to systems built decades before anyone imagined it creates brittle joins: undocumented data semantics, no API to speak of, screen-scraping or robotic automation as the integration layer, weak or absent authentication on internal interfaces, and no ability to log at the granularity an AI incident investigation needs. An agent given access through a legacy integration frequently inherits far more authority than intended, because the legacy system has no way to express a narrower one.

Questions to answer before you connect anything

Before committing to an integration: does using AI here make sense at all; can the provider's system actually connect to what you already run, or would it require a full overhaul; is your data good enough, or does data governance have to come first; how does the change affect everyone currently in the process; and do you understand the true effort, time and cost? Confirm the provider will support you across the whole integration rather than at the boundary.

One cost is consistently underestimated. Modernisation work undertaken to make legacy systems AI-ready tends to uncover hidden technical, privacy and security debt that was invisible while the old system was left alone. That discovery is genuinely useful and it inflates the programme budget, so it belongs in the business case as a likely finding rather than as a surprise.

Intellectual property. Feeding proprietary designs, source code or trade secrets into an integrated model can compromise confidentiality and, where trade secret protection depends on reasonable steps to preserve secrecy, may weaken the protection itself. This is a legal exposure created by an integration decision, and it is worth flagging before the connector is built rather than after.

Chapter Twenty

Architecture and the trust boundary

After this chapter: Enforce permission and export policy throughout an end-to-end data flow.

Place enforceable boundaries around information access and actions. Model instructions and learned safeguards can reduce risk, but should not be the sole mechanism authorizing disclosure or consequential action. Layer those imperfect controls with deterministic permission checks and tested response paths.

It helps to think in layers, because exam questions usually place a problem at a specific one. The interface layer is the user interface, API and plugins — authentication, rate limits, and disclosure that this is AI. The orchestration layer holds prompts, chains, agents, tools and memory — least privilege and approval gates. The model layer is the weights, registry and serving infrastructure — versioning, signing, rollback. The data layer is corpora, feature stores and vector stores — classification, provenance, retrieval access control. The infrastructure layer is compute, containers, network and identity. And the observability layer spans all of them.

The first common failure: retrieval as a service account

This is a consequential retrieval mistake that can arise in enterprise deployments. The application authenticates the user at the interface. Then, to fetch relevant documents, it queries the corpus using a service account — because that is how applications usually access data stores. The service account can read everything.

The result is that every user now has, in effect, the entitlements of that service account, mediated by a system that is explicitly designed to find and surface relevant content. Ask about the acquisition and the model will helpfully retrieve the board pack. No control was bypassed; the architecture simply placed access control at the wrong layer.

Key idea

In a retrieval system, enforce requesting-user entitlements before unauthorized material enters model context. Retain interface authentication and downstream controls as well. A later UI-only filter cannot reliably remove influence from context the model has already seen; compare designs against the specific disclosure threat.

Think of it this way

The librarian analogy again, and this is where it earns its keep. The reader shows a card at the front desk — that is authentication at the interface. But if the librarian in the stacks fetches whatever is asked for without ever checking the card, the desk was theatre. The check has to happen where the book is handed over.

The second: the over-credentialed agent

Because injection remains possible, bound data access, action authority and external destinations independently of model compliance. Use scoped identities, validated tool calls, necessary approval, sandboxing and egress restrictions alongside training, source separation, filtering and detection. Permissions strongly bound consequences; they do not alone prevent harmful advice or every authorized misuse.

Think of it this way

A "staff only" sign is not a locked door. It works on people who were going to comply anyway, and it stops nobody else. A system prompt is a sign. A scoped credential is a lock. When a question offers you one of each, take the lock.

A third set of roles

You have met provider and deployer as regulatory roles. Architecture literature uses a different and complementary trio, and questions can use either vocabulary. A provider offers products or services that use AI. A producer designs, develops, tests and deploys them — the model designers and developers. A customer uses the product, directly or by passing it on to its own users. Work out which hat you are wearing for the system in front of you before deciding what you are accountable for.

Secure by design, and the point about existing weaknesses

Secure by design means building so the system reasonably resists attack from inception through end of life, rather than hardening it later. Three principles anchor it: take ownership of customer security outcomes rather than pushing configuration burden downstream; embrace radical transparency and accountability, which for AI explicitly includes explainability; and build the organisational structure and leadership to actually deliver on the first two.

Key idea

Fix what is already broken before adding AI. AI magnifies existing weakness rather than compensating for it: weak identity management becomes an over-credentialed agent, poor data governance becomes an ungovernable training corpus, absent logging becomes an uninvestigable incident. Adding AI to a shaky foundation is the fastest route to a problem you cannot unpick.

Data dependency and the input contract

A model is trained to expect data with very specific attributes, which makes it acutely sensitive to change in its inputs. An alteration to the source, the format or the preprocessing can quietly degrade it with no error anywhere. The architectural control is an explicit input contract: document exactly what the model requires, verify that production inputs match, and add preprocessing that brings data back within tolerance when it drifts outside.

Think of it this way

A model is a machine calibrated for one grade of raw material. Feed it something slightly different and it does not jam and stop — it keeps running and produces subtly worse parts, indefinitely, until someone measures the output. The input contract is the goods-inwards inspection.

Change management when a fix takes weeks

Use ordinary emergency change procedures for software/configuration fixes and appropriate review for model/data changes. Some retraining is lengthy; tested rollback or a bounded compensating control may be appropriate while remediation proceeds. Define an owner, expiry and evidence for the interim decision.

Zero trust, extended to the model itself

NIST zero trust focuses on resource access without implicit trust from network location or ownership. Apply explicit identity and permission checks to AI data and tools, while separately evaluating output quality and safety. Model-output validation complements, rather than defines, zero-trust architecture.

  • Explicit verification: authenticate the subject and validate each resource request using current policy and context.
  • Least privilege: bound information, tool and action access to the task and the caller’s delegated authority.
  • Ongoing evaluation: use posture, telemetry and changing context to reassess access and control effectiveness.
Where people go wrong

Missing how AI blurs access boundaries. Consider a supervisor who has no rights to staff records, and who is granted access to a workforce-planning model that was built on those records. They can now reach restricted data through the model, and no access control was violated in the process. An AI access policy therefore has to specify the authorisation, the duration and the type of data reachable — with role-based access, multifactor authentication, particular validation for models built on mixed-sensitivity data, and a review of access after every release.

Integrate rather than parallel

The public tasks include integrating AI and enterprise architecture. Bring identity, data governance, logging, change control and response into a coherent design. A separate component is not automatically a finding; assess whether its boundaries, records and accountabilities integrate and meet requirements.

Chapter Twenty-one

Agents, autonomy and non-human identity

After this chapter: Bind agent identity, approval and budgets to the real transaction.

A model that answers questions is a content risk. A model that can act is an access control problem, and it is the fastest-moving part of this field.

An agent runs a loop: consider the goal, choose a tool, call it, observe the result, decide what to do next. Each pass through that loop pulls new content into the context window — the output of the tool it just called — and that content is untrusted. This is the structural reason agents amplify every problem in Chapter 17: an injection that would have produced a bad sentence now produces a bad action, and then feeds its result back in to inform the next one.

Think of it this way

A tool-less model can mislead a reader or disclose information. An agent adds direct operations across systems, so it needs both content/data safeguards and transaction authorization. The five-level autonomy ladder is an author-created discussion aid, not an official exam classification.

Autonomy is a tiering decision, not a build decision

The most useful thing a security manager can do here is force autonomy to be chosen deliberately rather than arrived at incrementally. Teams rarely decide to build a fully autonomous agent; they build one that suggests, then let it act with approval, then remove the approval because it was slowing things down.

Non-human identity

An agent needs credentials, and how you issue them determines the blast radius of every failure. Four rules cover most of it.

The agent has an attributable identity, distinct from other agents and linked to its invoking user where delegated. Shared accounts can make attribution and selective revocation difficult, especially without trustworthy per-request context. Prefer scoped identities and auditable delegation rather than relying on a shared credential alone.

Credentials are scoped and short-lived. Scoped to the specific tools and specific resources the task needs; short-lived so a leaked token expires rather than persisting.

Delegated authority is bounded by the user's own. An agent acting on behalf of a user should never be able to do more than that user could do directly — a constraint that sounds obvious and is violated constantly, because the agent was given a broad credential for convenience.

Every tool call is logged with intent and outcome: which agent, on whose behalf, invoking what, with what parameters, producing what result. Without this you cannot investigate, and the evidence is gone by the time you know you need it.

Memory, and why it is a data store

Agents that persist memory across sessions create a target that has no equivalent in a stateless chat. Content written into memory during one interaction shapes behaviour in later ones, potentially for other users if the memory is shared. That makes memory poisoning a durable attack: plant an instruction once, have it apply repeatedly. Treat agent memory as a data store — classified, access-controlled, retained under a policy, and inspectable.

In the wild

In 2024 academic researchers demonstrated a proof-of-concept they called Morris II: a self-replicating prompt injection that spread between AI-powered email assistants. An assistant processing an infected message would exfiltrate data and reproduce the payload in its own outgoing mail, infecting the next assistant. It was a laboratory demonstration, not an incident. It is worth knowing because it shows what changes when agents talk to agents: injection stops being a per-request problem and starts behaving like a worm.

Tools, plugins and the extended supply chain

Every tool an agent can call is a component in your supply chain, whether or not procurement saw it. A tool definition describes what it does in text the model reads, which means a malicious or compromised tool description is itself an injection vector. Controls: an allowlist of approved tools rather than open registration, review of tool definitions as code, pinned versions, and the same due diligence you would apply to any third-party integration.

Where this is heading

Standardised protocols for agents to discover and call tools — and each other — are being adopted quickly, and they create exactly the surface this chapter describes: a registry of callable capabilities, described in text the model reads, invoked across organisational boundaries. Treat any such protocol adoption as a supply chain event, not an integration detail.

Multi-agent systems

When agents call other agents, two problems compound. The confused deputy pattern appears — an agent with high privilege performs an action requested by one with low privilege, and the privilege check happens at the wrong boundary. And failures cascade: an error or injected instruction propagates through the chain, with each step lending it credibility because it now arrives from a trusted internal source. Keep chains short, re-authorise at each privilege boundary rather than trusting the caller, and make the chain observable end to end.

Where people go wrong

Assuming human approval scales with agent volume. An agent that takes two hundred actions an hour cannot have each one meaningfully approved, and a reviewer clicking through them is providing no oversight at all. Either reduce the autonomy, or move the control from per-action approval to enforced envelopes plus exception alerting. Choose deliberately — do not let the approval step decay into a formality.

Chapter Twenty-two

Controlling the lifecycle

After this chapter: Evaluate with independent data and evidence-based release/change gates.

Chapter 7 described how AI systems are built. This one attaches a control to each stage — which is exactly the mapping the exam asks you to perform.

The seven phases, named

The seven phases used here are a teaching view consistent with common lifecycle guidance: plan/design; collect/process data; build/adapt; test/evaluate/verify/validate; make available; operate/monitor; retire. Chapter 7 expands collection and preparation separately; the two views should map to the same controls, not be memorized as conflicting official lists.

Two useful distinctions inside evaluation are verification and validation. Verification checks the model was built correctly to specification. Validation checks it actually serves its intended purpose with accurate, reliable output. A model can pass verification and fail validation — built exactly as specified, and the specification was wrong.

Retirement has three triggers: the solution becomes obsolete, a better alternative emerges, or ongoing risk assessment finds the risk now exceeds appetite. That third one is the one organisations miss, because nothing has broken — the appetite moved, or the risk did.

Problem definition

Before the technical stages there is a decision that is often skipped: should this be an AI system at all? Some decisions should not be automated, either because the consequences of error are unacceptable or because the affected person's right to an explanation cannot be honoured. The intake and tiering process exists partly to force this question, and "use a deterministic rules engine instead" is a legitimate treatment option that candidates rarely select.

Model selection

The evaluation criteria are more legal and evidential than technical: licence terms and what they permit; provenance of the model and, as far as it can be established, of its training data; documented evaluation and safety testing; stated limitations; update and support commitments; the provider's viability; and whether the deployment options can satisfy your data residency requirements. Selecting a model is a procurement decision with security consequences, not a benchmark comparison.

Data acquisition and preparation

Rights and lawful basis, provenance records created at the time, quality assessment, poisoning checks, classification and minimisation. For labelling, vetted vendors under appropriate contracts, quality review, and integrity protection on the resulting datasets.

Training

An isolated environment; access control over data and compute; secrets management; integrity of the pipeline code; and reproducibility — dataset version, configuration and seeds recorded so that the model can be tied back to its inputs and rebuilt if needed. Where privacy exposure is material, differential privacy can be applied during training at a measurable cost to accuracy.

Validation

Held-out test sets; adversarial and robustness testing; fairness testing across subgroups rather than in aggregate; safety and harmful-content evaluation; red teaming; and documented acceptance criteria with a recorded sign-off.

Where people go wrong

Testing performance in aggregate. A model can be highly accurate overall while performing badly for a subgroup that is a small share of the test set — and aggregate metrics will never show it. Fairness testing must be disaggregated by group, which requires having decided in advance which groups matter and how you will measure disparity.

Chapter Twenty-three

Data controls

After this chapter: Trace permitted use, access, retention and recovery across derived data.

The data layer is where most AI risk originates, and where several familiar controls behave differently than expected.

Provenance and lineage. Record where every dataset came from, what rights attach, what transformations were applied, and which version fed which model. Without lineage you cannot answer a regulator, cannot respond to a discovered poisoning, and cannot prove what a model was trained on. Provenance reconstructed after the fact is not provenance.

Classification with permitted use. Covered in Chapter 11, and it belongs here too: sensitivity and permitted-use are separate axes.

Minimisation. Limit data at collection and before training; plan verified deletion, retraining or appropriate unlearning for later changes. Prevention reduces the cost and uncertainty of remediation.

De-identification. Pseudonymisation, anonymisation, tokenisation and redaction all reduce exposure and none of them is absolute. Re-identification risk rises when de-identified data is combined with other sources, and models are unusually good at inferring attributes that were removed. Treat de-identification as risk reduction, not elimination.

Access control and integrity. On datasets, feature stores, vector stores, model artefacts and the pipeline itself. Separate the duty of approving data for training from the duty of running training. Hash and version datasets; restrict write access; detect change.

Encryption. At rest and in transit as normal. For high-sensitivity workloads, confidential computing or trusted execution environments protect data in use, which matters when training or inference happens on infrastructure you do not fully control.

Retention and deletion. Prompts, outputs, logs, embeddings and caches all need retention rules, and deletion obligations must be traceable through every derived artefact.

In the wild

The Italian DPA restricted ChatGPT processing in March 2023 over stated data-protection concerns, followed by changes and later proceedings. This historical action illustrates why lawful basis and transparency belong before deployment. Later enforcement or appeals require current primary sources; this case is not a current legal-status summary.

Where this is heading

Confidential computing is becoming available for inference at reasonable cost, which changes a calculation that has held for years: it starts to be practical to run sensitive workloads on infrastructure you do not control without exposing the data in use. Watch this one, because it may reopen deployment options you have already ruled out.

The vocabulary of the data estate

Four artefacts that are routinely confused. A data inventory lists your data assets. A data dictionary describes each data element at the level of a single application. A business glossary sits above both, defining shared concepts so a term means the same thing everywhere. A data catalog ties them together by holding the metadata.

The five Vs characterise data feeding a training pipeline: volume, velocity, variety, veracity and value. The six dimensions of data quality are the ones to quote when asked what "good data" means: accuracy (free of error), completeness (no missing required fields), consistency (uniform formats), timeliness (current when needed), validity (follows defined rules), and uniqueness (no duplicates).

Three risks at collection

At collection, establish lawful basis and rights, fitness for purpose and currency. Consent is relevant where it is the selected legal basis. RAG can supply current facts to a suitable generative application; it does not generally repair a predictive model whose input/outcome relationship has changed.

Where a dataset is unbalanced or scarce, the techniques are oversampling the minority class, undersampling the majority, and augmentation to generate additional variations. Each changes the distribution the model learns from, so each is a decision with fairness consequences rather than a neutral preprocessing step.

The access-versus-protection tension

Every AI programme runs into the same conflict: leadership wants data unlocked so the AI can use it, and the data still deserves the protection it always had. Least privilege is the resolution — applied to systems, identities and processes, not only to people. The model, the pipeline and the agent each need their own minimum access, and none should inherit a human's.

Encoding, and the data lake problem

Text models commonly tokenize text into model inputs; other deep-learning workflows use numeric features, images or signals without text tokenization. Encodings, shards, vectors and associated metadata can retain source sensitivity. Binary representation is neither a privacy technique nor the definition of tokenization.

Where people go wrong

The data lake problem. Data pulled together from many sources into one aggregated store frequently arrives stripped of the access controls it carried in its original system. Everything is now in one place, readable by whatever the pipeline runs as, and the restrictions that governed it are gone. Aggregation for AI is one of the most common ways an organisation quietly loses a control it believed it still had.

Confidentiality during training

Training usually requires data to be machine-readable, and common tokenization works on cleartext, so sensitive data is often decrypted during development. Homomorphic encryption can let a model train without decrypting, at heavy computational cost. The practical answer for most organisations is compensating controls: restrict who can reach unencrypted production data, monitor sensitive data use, and apply disk-level encryption underneath for defence in depth.

What to back up, specifically

Decide retention and recoverability from lawful purpose, licensing, source stability, reproducibility and recovery requirements. An externally available dataset may change or disappear, so preserve permitted versioned evidence or an authorized recoverable copy where needed. Back up model artifacts, configuration, relevant datasets and evaluation evidence as a coherent set.

Integrity, and the three tampering targets

Integrity breaks in four ways worth naming separately: data poisoning alters training data; model tampering changes weights or parameters directly; embedding tampering skews retrieval results without touching the model at all; and ordinary ETL errors corrupt data with no attacker involved. Layered defences: anomaly detection on training data, separation of training data from production data, validation and model ensembles, and hardening of the model itself.

Governing the data stages

The guiding principle is that AI data governance builds on existing data governance rather than replacing it. Three controls carry the organisation-and-preparation stage: dataflow mapping, documenting every data element and where it physically resides down to the hardware; access control review, applying least privilege to training data and the model; and data integrity monitoring, watching the cleansing and standardisation steps where silent corruption enters.

In use and storage: monitor privacy-by-design more frequently where decisions are automated, treat newly generated data with the same rigour as acquired data, apply information-flow controls that adapt to the threat environment, and classify AI output like any other data.

Where people go wrong

An anomaly may require a scoped preservation hold approved through incident/legal procedures. Record the evidence scope, lawful basis, access restrictions and review/expiry; do not suspend all retention schedules automatically. Restore normal deletion when the justified hold ends.

Backup, integrity and destruction

Three controls that transfer from conventional data governance with a twist in each.

Backup and recoverability now covers objects that are expensive or impossible to reconstruct. A trained model may represent months of compute; a curated and labelled dataset may represent more. Losing the training data can mean the model can never be rebuilt or explained — which turns a backup failure into a permanent compliance failure. Back up datasets, model artefacts, configuration and the evaluation evidence together, because any one of them alone is insufficient.

Integrity matters more than usual because corruption is silent here. A subtly altered dataset produces a subtly worse model, and nothing errors. Hashing, signing and immutable versioning are how you detect what you cannot see.

Secure destruction is where AI most often breaks an existing process. A deletion instruction that reaches a source repository and stops there leaves the derived estate untouched: embeddings, caches, prompt and output logs, fine-tuned variants, evaluation datasets and backups. Destruction has to be traced through the lineage, which is another reason lineage cannot be reconstructed after the fact.

Chapter Twenty-four

Privacy, ethics, trust and safety

After this chapter: Make lawful-basis, fairness and oversight choices testable and contestable.

The subtopic that most distinguishes this credential from a traditional security certification, and the one candidates most often skip. Do not.

The seven trustworthiness characteristics

Widely echoed across AI standards and worth knowing as a set: valid and reliable — it works consistently for its intended purpose; safe — it does not create unacceptable risk of harm; secure and resilient — it withstands attack and recovers; accountable and transparent — someone is responsible and information about the system is available; explainable and interpretable — outputs can be understood and justified; privacy-enhanced; and fair, with harmful bias managed.

Key idea

Trustworthiness objectives can trade off in a particular system, but not every pair necessarily conflicts. Measure the actual effect: interpretable models can perform competitively on suitable tasks, and privacy costs depend on the method and setting. Document justified choices rather than assuming accuracy always requires opacity.

Think of it this way

A map projection can illustrate a genuine trade-off, but it does not prove a universal incompatibility among trustworthiness objectives. Establish the relevant measures and actual evidence before accepting a sacrifice in safety, privacy, explainability or performance.

NIST groups the same territory into three categories, and this is the version most likely to appear as a question. Systemic bias comes from institutional practice that quietly favours some groups. Statistical or computational bias arises when the data sample does not represent the real population. Human bias is the mental shortcuts and systematic errors in human judgement. Learn these three alongside the fuller taxonomy below — they are the compact answer.

Bias, by type

Systemic or historical bias: the world the data came from was already unequal, and the model learns that faithfully. Representation bias: some groups are under-represented in training data. Measurement bias: the proxy variable does not measure what you think — using arrests as a proxy for offending, or spend as a proxy for creditworthiness. Aggregation bias: one model fits the majority group and serves others poorly. Human bias in labelling and interpretation, including automation bias — the tendency to over-trust machine output. Deployment bias: the system is used in a context it was not designed for.

Define the relevant harm and population before selecting measures. Equalized odds requires parity of true-positive and false-positive rates; equal opportunity relaxes this to the true-positive rate. They are not mutually incompatible: equalized odds satisfies equal opportunity. Other criteria can conflict, for example calibration and error-rate parity when base rates differ under the relevant assumptions.

Think of it this way

Use the map analogy only for demonstrated trade-offs, not as proof that all measures conflict. A fairness decision should name the metric, rationale, affected groups, uncertainty, legal constraints and review/redress process. Multiple compatible measures may be appropriate.

In the wild

The COMPAS dispute illustrates that different fairness measures answer different questions. Distinguish reported group error patterns from calibration claims, and state the population, outcome definitions and mathematical assumptions before claiming an incompatibility result. The lesson is to justify and test measures, not to treat any fairness choice as equally lawful or acceptable.

Accepting the existence of a reviewer as evidence of oversight. Agency, and the two loop patterns

Safety changes character once a system can act rather than only advise. AI agency — the ability to decide and act independently — raises four questions a security manager should be able to answer for any autonomous deployment: who is accountable for the consequences and to what extent; what keeps the system acting within bounds when nobody is watching; what happens when its decision conflicts with the user's values; and what explanation users are entitled to when even the designers cannot fully interpret it.

Two supervision patterns are easy to confuse and are examined as a pair. Human in the loop designs the workflow to pause and require explicit human approval before continuing — a clinician signing off an automated recommendation before it is acted on. AI in the loop inverts the relationship: the AI assists and augments a human decision while the person stays in control throughout. HITL deliberately slows the system down, which is why it is reserved for high-stakes decisions rather than applied everywhere.

Deepfakes can be generated through GANs, diffusion, autoregressive and other methods. A GAN’s training discriminator is not the same as an operational forensic detector. Evaluate detection empirically; strengthen independent verification, provenance and transaction controls without claiming detection is mathematically destined to fail.

Transparency and redress

Transparency controls: disclosing that a person is interacting with AI; labelling synthetic content, including watermarking and provenance metadata; explaining automated decisions to those affected; publishing intended use and limitations; and providing a redress mechanism — a route by which a person can contest an outcome and have it reviewed. Redress is the element most often missing from an otherwise complete answer, and it is the point of the whole transparency cluster: information without recourse is not accountability.

Consent is specific, and rights persist

Data collected for one purpose is not automatically available for training. Determine an appropriate lawful basis, purpose compatibility and applicable conditions before reuse. Where consent is the basis, ensure it validly covers the processing; consent is not the only possible basis, and special-category data can require additional conditions.

Applicable individual rights can include information, access, correction, erasure and restriction, subject to the relevant conditions and exemptions. Trace those obligations through the processing estate. Training may make a remedy difficult, but that does not itself remove the right; evaluate the actual model, data and legal context.

Determine whether the parties are controllers, joint controllers or controller/processor. Use the required contractual terms for that relationship. A DPIA is required when its applicable high-risk trigger is met; a broader privacy review can still be good practice below that threshold.

Where people go wrong

A common exposure occurs when protected production data is copied to a weaker development environment. Assess each transfer, intended use, access, retention and supplier path. Both malicious compromise and accidental handling failures can cause harm.

Ethics as a managed programme

Two capabilities make AI ethics distinctive rather than a general corporate concern: automatic decision-making and self-learning. Both mean the system acts with some independence, and independence at scale is what converts an ethical lapse into widespread harm.

Four strategies keep it manageable. Review the intended purpose against your own context, applicable law and societal norms — the same system can be appropriate in one setting and not another. Ensure genuine diversity and collaboration in development across demographics, departments and disciplines, because it determines which problems get noticed at all. Gather benchmarking data from comparable projects. And build feedback mechanisms so users can surface unexpected harms early.

The concrete controls: a human in the loop over consequential decisions; an AI ethics and compliance committee; an AI code of conduct, created and then regularly re-evaluated rather than published once; documented evidence that systems adhere to standards covering health, safety, law, environment and fundamental rights; demonstrable transparency, fairness and bias management; and an assessment of environmental impact, which appears in this syllabus as an ethics obligation rather than a sustainability footnote.

Safety controls

Use task-specific safety evaluation, scope limits, input/output inspection, action budgets and escalation. If confidence is a trigger, establish how it is measured and calibrated for the task; a model’s own confidence claim is not sufficient evidence. Provide a safe response when quality or safety cannot be established.

Global and local explanations answer different questions

A global explanation describes what the model does in general — which factors matter most across all decisions. It is what you need for validation, for documentation, and for answering a regulator's question about how a system works.

A local explanation describes one particular output — why this applicant, this transaction, this claim. Techniques include local approximations around a single decision, attributions distributing the outcome across input features, and counterfactuals: what would have needed to be different for the outcome to change.

Counterfactual explanations show which changes would affect an outcome, subject to the model and method’s limits. Check that proposed changes are feasible, lawful and actionable; an immutable attribute or years of future residence is not an immediate remedy. Explain how the person can contest incorrect data or seek review.

Where people go wrong

Treating a language model's own account of its reasoning as an explanation. Ask a generative model why it produced an answer and it will produce a fluent, plausible rationale — generated the same way the answer was, not derived from the computation that produced it. It may be accurate. It is not evidence, and it should never be offered to a regulator or an affected person as the basis of a decision.

Where this is heading

Mechanistic interpretability — reading what is actually happening inside a network rather than approximating it from outside — is advancing quickly in research. It is not yet something you can buy or rely on for a regulatory explanation. If it matures, the trade-off drawn in the diagram above becomes considerably less painful.

The black box problem, and documenting what you do not know

Opacity runs deepest in deep learning and neural networks, where the reasoning is distributed across millions of weighted connections and simply cannot be read out. That is the black box problem, and it does something uncomfortable to a security professional's normal method: you cannot fully enumerate the failure modes, so you cannot fully control them.

The response is not to pretend otherwise. Document the data flows and the model design in detail — and then, wherever the system is opaque, document precisely what is and is not known about that opacity. An AI risk assessment run alongside the privacy assessment should identify where black-box behaviour might occur and set out a plan for remediating it if it does.

Key idea

Recording uncertainty is a governance control that makes it visible and assignable. It does not itself demonstrate safety, explainability or legal compliance. Pair the record with a justified treatment, verification and a decision within the owner’s authority.

From deterministic to experimental

Conventional and AI software both involve uncertainty and experiments. AI governance needs explicit statistical evidence, deterministic safeguards and documented unknowns. A deployed model does not necessarily learn or adapt continuously; separately control any pipeline that updates it.

Where people go wrong

Carrying the deterministic instinct into AI governance and treating every unexplained behaviour as a bug awaiting a fix. That framing produces two failures: indefinite delay while someone tries to explain the unexplainable, or quiet acceptance once the pressure to ship wins. The professional stance is neither — it is to state the residual uncertainty explicitly, bound it with monitoring and oversight, and have the accountable owner accept it knowingly.

Chapter Twenty-six

Monitoring and operations

After this chapter: Specify a monitoring rule with ground truth, uncertainty, response and protected evidence.

Useful monitoring depends on the deployment and configuration. Specify what evidence is required for investigation, effectiveness measurement and reporting; verify that collection works without unnecessarily retaining sensitive content.

What to log

Prompts and responses — with privacy controls on the logs themselves, since they will contain whatever users typed. Model identity, version and the configuration used. Retrieval events: which documents were returned, for which user. Tool and agent invocations and their results. The identity of the invoking user and any delegated identity. Metric families for the monitoring programme itself

Beyond the model, measure whether the programme works: preparedness (update compliance, identification of high-risk vulnerabilities); shadow activity (discovered tools and inventory completeness); intrusion attempts (counts and source analysis); data-loss-prevention effectiveness (prevention ratio, response time); awareness effectiveness (engagement and actual behaviour change, not completion rates); and AI solution performance itself — false-positive rate, drift frequency, and response latency.

Where AI genuinely helps the defender

The syllabus expects the positive case as well as the cautionary one. Four areas where AI meaningfully strengthens defence:

  • Network and endpoint defence — automated log analysis that pairs an error with a likely cause and a suggested fix; detection of stealthy persistent malware by its command patterns; and phishing and spam filtering trained on safe versus suspicious messages.
  • Behavioural analysis — learning what normal activity looks like for a user or system so that deviations surface early. This is the technique that catches what signature-based tooling structurally cannot.
  • Decision support — prioritising vulnerabilities across volumes no team can triage manually, selecting among thousands of possible remediations, and supporting zero-trust enforcement decisions.
  • Threat detection and training — models trained on historical breaches spotting new ones faster, and generating synthetic examples of novel attacks to train other defensive systems.

The discipline that keeps this from becoming a single point of failure is unglamorous and familiar: genuine due diligence on the tooling, ongoing monitoring, repeated rather than one-off risk assessment, strong controls around the tool itself, awareness training, and continuous watch for emerging risk.

Threat intelligence for AI

Monitoring tells you what is happening in your systems. Threat intelligence tells you what is happening to everyone else, and for AI the useful sources are not the ones your existing programme subscribes to. Worth tracking: vulnerability disclosures affecting model-serving and orchestration frameworks; advisories from your model providers, including model deprecations and behaviour changes; public AI incident registers, which are increasingly cited by regulators and researchers; adversarial technique knowledge bases; and reports of malicious artefacts on public model hubs.

Feed it back into three places specifically: your reassessment triggers, your evaluation and red-team suites, and the scenarios you tabletop. Intelligence that arrives and does not change any of those is being collected rather than used.

What awareness training has to contain

An annual slideshow does not address the human risks that technical controls cannot reach — shadow AI and information leakage above all. An effective programme covers security, privacy and ethics together; explains what the AI is actually for, so people can judge misuse; teaches staff to interpret model output, ideally through hands-on exercises using outputs that look authoritative and are wrong; and builds the ability to notice bias in data and results. Round it out with copyright and intellectual property, explainability and the black-box problem, model drift, adversarial techniques, and — the component most often absent — a defined route for staff to give feedback when something looks wrong.

The skills gap is a governance problem

Adoption decisions are usually made without weighing the skills needed to support them, and the resulting gap is real. Three responses. Recruit and develop people whose backgrounds reflect the users the AI will affect, because that materially affects which problems get noticed. Train existing staff for responsible practice, with deeper technical training for security and development teams. And set realistic experience expectations — commercial AI is young, and advertising for a decade of experience in it is a way of not hiring anyone. Fold the skills requirement into the governance programme and the security architecture rather than waiting for a market that does not yet exist.

Control types, mapped

Preventive: access control on training data, input validation, guardrails, least-privilege agents, tool allowlists, procurement gates. Detective: output monitoring, drift detection, evaluation regression tests, audit logging. Corrective: model rollback, retraining, guardrail tuning, corpus cleanup. Deterrent: acceptable use policy, watermarking. Compensating: human review where technical controls cannot reach. Directive: policy, standards, training.

Where this is heading

Autonomous defence is arriving: agents that triage, investigate and act in the security operations centre. Note the symmetry, because it is the whole point — an agent with the standing access a SOC requires is the highest-privilege agent in the organisation, and every constraint in the agents chapter applies to it with more force, not less.

AI in the security function itself

The syllabus is not only about securing AI — it also covers leveraging AI within security operations, and reviewing AI security tools as part of the program. Expect questions on the benefits and the caveats: reduced analyst load against false positives and limited explainability; automation of triage against the excessive agency risk that applies to a security agent exactly as it applies to any other.

Key idea

The security function is not exempt from its own AI policy. A detection tool that ingests sensitive logs into a third-party model is subject to the same due diligence, the same inventory entry and the same oversight requirements as anything the business deploys. Questions sometimes test exactly this by making security the one cutting corners.

What each category is genuinely good for

Discovery and posture management answers "what do we have," which is the prerequisite for everything else. It finds unapproved tools, AI features enabled inside software you already own, and models pulled into development environments. Its limit: it finds usage, not risk. An inventory entry with no owner and no tier is a list, not governance.

An AI gateway or proxy gives you a single enforcement point in front of model calls — authentication, rate and cost limits, logging, and policy on which models may be used for which data. It is the highest-leverage single control for organisations consuming models by API, because it converts a scattered problem into a chokepoint. Its limit: it only sees traffic routed through it, so it must be paired with discovery.

Guardrails and output inspection classify inputs and outputs against policy — blocking prompt injection attempts, filtering harmful content, running data loss prevention across responses. Useful and necessary. Its limit is the one from Chapter 17: filtering cannot fully solve injection, and treating guardrails as the primary defence rather than one layer is the recurring architectural error.

Artefact scanning and model registries address the supply chain — scanning model files for unsafe serialisation and embedded code, signing and versioning artefacts, controlling which models may be loaded. Its limit: a scan proves the file is not obviously malicious, not that the model is not backdoored.

Evaluation and red-team platforms run adversarial, bias and safety test suites, ideally in the deployment pipeline so a failing model does not ship. This is what turns "validation is a control gate" from a principle into a mechanism. Its limit: a test suite measures what it was written to measure, and the threat landscape moves.

Observability and tracing captures the record described in Chapter 26 — prompts, versions, retrieval events, tool calls, overrides. Its limit is a privacy one: the trace store now contains everything users typed, and needs classification and retention rules of its own.

Data lineage and vector store access control is the least glamorous category and often the one that closes the largest gap, because it is where the retrieval entitlement failure gets fixed.

Where this is heading

This category is consolidating fast: discovery, gateway, guardrails and observability are being folded into single platforms, and several will be absorbed into existing security suites. Favour tools that produce portable evidence and enforce at a documented policy boundary, because those survive a vendor being acquired or a category collapsing.

How to review a tool before buying it

  1. Which layer does it defend, and what is left uncovered? Tools are frequently bought in overlapping pairs while a layer sits bare.
  2. Does it see everything, or only what is routed to it? Coverage is usually the binding constraint, not capability.
  3. What does it do to your data? Most of these tools are themselves AI systems processing your prompts and documents. They belong in the inventory, go through vendor due diligence, and are subject to the same policy as anything else.
  4. Does it produce evidence? A control that blocks something without a retrievable record is hard to assure and useless in an investigation.
  5. What is the failure mode? If the tool is unavailable, does the AI system fail open or closed — and is that the behaviour you chose?
Where people go wrong

Define the permitted behaviour, objective and authority before relying on a tool to enforce them. Where these are already established, implementing or repairing the tool may be the appropriate next action. Verify coverage and effectiveness instead of treating either policy or a product purchase as sufficient by itself.

NIST AI Risk Management Framework

Voluntary, outcome-based, and the most widely referenced structure for organising AI risk work. Four functions. Govern is cross-cutting — culture, policies, accountability, workforce — and it runs through the other three rather than preceding them. Map establishes context: the use case, stakeholders, impacts, capabilities and limitations. Measure analyses, benchmarks and monitors risk using both quantitative and qualitative methods. Manage prioritises, treats, responds to incidents and allocates resources.

There is a companion profile for generative AI that enumerates risks specific to that class of system — including confabulation, information integrity, information security, data privacy, harmful bias, intellectual property, and value chain integration — with suggested actions against each. Know that it exists and what it covers.

Reach for the RMF when a scenario asks how to organise AI risk management systematically. It is a structure for work, not a certification.

Think of it this way

Three instruments, three different things. The NIST framework is the training programme — how you learn to do the work. ISO 42001 is the certificate on the wall saying an assessor watched you do it properly. The EU AI Act is the law that decides whether you are allowed to practise at all, and what happens if you get it wrong.

ISO/IEC 42001

A certifiable management system standard for AI, structured like ISO 27001 — plan, do, check, act, with the familiar clause pattern and an annex of controls. It requires context and leadership, an AI policy, defined roles, AI risk assessment and treatment, AI system impact assessment, lifecycle management, requirements on third parties, operation, monitoring, internal audit, management review and improvement.

Reach for 42001 when the scenario asks how to demonstrate systematic management to an outside party — a customer, a regulator, a board. It is also the natural thing to look for when assessing a vendor, with the caveat from Chapter 19: check that the certificate's scope actually covers the AI service.

Related standards worth knowing by function alone: 23894 gives AI risk management guidance aligned to ISO 31000; 22989 is terminology; 5338 covers AI lifecycle processes; 42005 addresses impact assessment; 24028 covers trustworthiness; and 27001 and 27002 remain the information security baseline that AI controls extend rather than replace.

COBIT, mapped to the AI life cycle

This is ISACA's own governance framework, so its vocabulary shapes how questions are phrased. Its five domains map onto AI work directly. Evaluate, Direct and Monitor is the governance layer — appetite, direction, oversight — and sits above the other four, which are management. Align, Plan and Organise shapes strategy and policy. Build, Acquire and Implement covers development, procurement, integration and testing. Deliver, Service and Support runs operations. Monitor, Evaluate and Assess drives continuous checking against performance, compliance and risk objectives.

COBIT distinguishes governance’s evaluation, direction and oversight from management’s execution. Apply the organization’s actual delegations: a CISO may own a defined operational risk, while independent assurance remains separate from operating that control.

The Four Pillars: documenting a model honestly

A framing from the Cloud Security Alliance that assembles four artefacts into a continuous learning loop:

  • Model card — the model's objective, training data, capabilities and limits.
  • Data sheet — the training dataset, including its known biases and ethical concerns.
  • Risk card — identified risks, their analysis, and the mitigations planned against them.
  • Scenario planning — deliberate imagining of misuse and malfunction, to surface risks the first three did not.

Risk cards link identified exposure and treatment; scenario planning explores misuse, malfunction and changed context. All four artifacts can reveal unknowns when critically reviewed. Use them together rather than claiming one alone discovers every novel failure.

The centre of gravity has already moved twice

The research literature that produced most of the attack names in Chapter 17 was written when the model was the system: you had a classifier, someone tried to fool it or steal it, and the controls attached to the model itself. Then applications wrapped models in retrieval, tools and user interfaces, and the interesting failures moved outward — into what the system could be made to say and reveal. Now they are moving outward again, into what the system can be made to do.

The practical consequence is that security effort is migrating from data science towards identity, authorisation and workflow design. If you are deciding where to build capability, that is the direction of travel. The organisations that will handle agentic AI well are the ones that were already good at least privilege and delegated authority, not the ones with the best model expertise.

Key idea

Nothing gets retired as the centre of gravity moves. Poisoning did not stop mattering because agents became interesting; it stopped being discussed. This is a real risk for anyone learning the field from current commentary rather than from a syllabus — you end up fluent in the newest layer and blank on the ones underneath it, which is precisely the gap this exam is built to find.

Five things worth planning for

1 · Non-human identity becomes a first-class problem. Most organisations today issue agents credentials the way they issued service accounts in 2010 — broad, long-lived, shared, and attributed to nobody. That does not survive contact with agents acting on behalf of individual users at volume. Expect delegated-authority tokens, per-agent identity, short lifetimes and agent registries to become ordinary infrastructure. The work you can do now is unglamorous and durable: get every agent off shared credentials and make delegated authority bounded by the user's own.

2 · Discovery replaces procurement as how you learn what you have. The assumption behind every intake process is that new capability arrives through a gate. Increasingly it arrives through an update to software you bought years ago, enabled by default. Intake still matters, but it stops being sufficient, and continuous discovery becomes the primary instrument of the inventory. Organisations that treat their inventory as a document rather than a feed will find it is wrong within a quarter.

3 · Point-in-time assurance stops matching the object. Certifying a system whose model changes underneath it is a structural mismatch, and it is already visible in the awkwardness of applying conventional audit to AI. Expect movement towards continuous assurance: automated evidence, evaluation suites running in the deployment pipeline, and attestations that expire quickly. This is also where an independent AI assurance market seems likely to form, in roughly the way financial audit did — which would be good for the profession and would put real weight on the evidence trail discussed in Chapter 13.

4 · Verification moves from perception to provenance. Chapter 18's argument only gets stronger. As synthetic audio and video become ordinary rather than remarkable, controls that depend on recognising a person will keep failing, and the response will be procedural and cryptographic rather than perceptual: out-of-band verification as standard practice, signed and provenance-marked content, stronger identity proofing at onboarding and recovery. Detection tooling will keep improving and will keep being the weaker half of the answer.

5 · Inference moves inside the boundary. Smaller, cheaper, more capable models running on your own infrastructure — or on the endpoint itself — change the data question materially. Some of today's hardest vendor problems dissolve when the model is local. New ones appear: model artefacts distributed across endpoints, update integrity at scale, and capability in places your governance never had to reach.

Where this is heading

Whether ISACA reorganizes future domains is unknown. Do not allocate exam effort based on a predicted new agent domain. Agent permissions and accountability remain useful applications of the current architecture, lifecycle and oversight tasks.

Three things to be sceptical about

Do not assume injection has a general solution. Training, role/source separation and classifiers can improve measured resistance. Maintain independent authorization, bounded access and verified effects because the residual failure matters more than a universal prediction about future capability.

Do not assume a universal winner in synthetic-media detection. Performance depends on methods, distribution and attacker adaptation. Combine tested detection with independent verification and provenance. Provenance can authenticate an asserted origin or modification history; it does not establish that the content is true or that a requested action is authorized.

Regulatory forecasts remain uncertain. Maintain documented obligations, sources, roles and review triggers. Common controls ease adaptation but do not guarantee compliance with every future law. Update the legal assessment when the text or applicable context changes.

Where people go wrong

Treating the horizon as a reason to defer. "We will wait until the regulation settles" and "we will wait for the agent standards to mature" both sound prudent and both produce the same outcome: an unmapped estate of AI systems that were adopted anyway, by people who needed to get work done. Every durable control in this book — inventory, ownership, tiering, least privilege, evidence — is worth building before the picture clarifies, because none of them depend on which way it clarifies.

Glossary

The vocabulary the exam assumes, defined the way it is used in this syllabus rather than the way a data scientist would define it.

Ability, integrity, benevolence
An interpersonal trustworthiness lens, distinct from NIST zero trust. For architecture use explicit verification, least privilege and continuously informed access decisions. Chapter 20
Agent
A model given tools, memory and autonomy to take actions. Shifts the security question from what the system can say to what it can do. Chapter 21
AI agency
A system's capacity to decide and act independently. Raises accountability, self-regulation, value-conflict and explanation questions that advisory systems do not. Chapter 21
AI as a service
Model capability consumed from a provider rather than hosted. The consumption mode that gives you the least control and is the most common. Chapter 8
AI bill of materials
An inventory of the models, datasets and components in a system. What lets you answer "are we affected" when something upstream is found compromised. Chapter 22
AI charter
The formal document that makes an initiative governable: objectives, scope, stakeholders, governance structure, timeline, budget, risks, success metrics and signed sign-off. Chapter 9
AI impact assessment
An assessment of a system's effects on individuals and society — fairness, transparency, oversight and redress — distinct from a security risk assessment. Chapter 16
AI in the loop
AI assists and augments a human decision while the person stays in control — the inverse of human-in-the-loop, where the workflow pauses for explicit approval. Chapter 24
AI lead
A named individual tracking the organisation's evolving relationship with AI, working across security, privacy, legal, procurement, risk and audit. Stops AI governance from being everyone's concern and nobody's job. Chapter 12
AI readiness
Assessment of whether data, skills, infrastructure, governance and culture can support AI. Readiness gaps determine which failure mode you get first. Chapter 9
ANI, AGI, ASI
Narrow, general and super intelligence: broad capability labels with disputed boundaries. Govern demonstrated system behaviour and applicable legal classifications. Chapter 4
Appropriate risk
Risk worth taking to achieve an objective — as distinct from inappropriate risk, which brings no clear benefit, exceeds tolerance and threatens brand, trust or legal standing. Chapter 15
Automation bias
The human tendency to over-trust machine output. The reason a reviewer's presence is not by itself evidence of oversight. Chapter 24
Backdoor
A hidden trigger causing chosen behaviour. It may enter through poisoning, modified weights or a compromised supplied artifact; ordinary evaluation may miss it. Chapter 17
Black box problem
Results that cannot be explained because the reasoning is distributed across millions of weights. Managed by documenting what is and is not known about the opacity, not by eliminating it. Chapter 25
Calibration
Agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcome frequencies in the evaluated task/population. Self-reported confidence alone does not establish it. Chapter 26
Concept drift
Degradation because the relationship the model learned has stopped holding. Distinct from data drift, where the inputs change distribution. Chapter 7
Confabulation
Generated claims that are false or unsupported despite appearing plausible. Evaluate factuality and use task-appropriate grounding, verification and oversight. Chapter 6
Conformity assessment
The provider's demonstration that a high-risk system meets legal requirements, examining its quality management system and technical documentation. Repeated after substantial modification. Chapter 16
Consent specificity
Consent given for one purpose does not extend to another. Data collected to deliver a service does not thereby become available for training. Chapter 24
Control effectiveness
Evidence that a control achieves its intended objective in the relevant scope. Coverage or completion alone is insufficient. Chapter 12
Data lag
A gap between training information and the current environment. Retrieval can refresh context for suitable applications; predictive concept drift may require model/process revalidation. Chapter 23
Data lake problem
Aggregation can lose source permissions or permitted-use context unless they are deliberately preserved and enforced. It is an implementation risk, not an unavoidable property of all data lakes. Chapter 23
Deployer
An entity using an AI system under its authority, subject to applicable definitions. High-risk role changes under Article 25 have specific conditions; hosting or fine-tuning alone is not a universal test. Chapter 28
Development-production gap
The classic AI privacy breach: tightly controlled data copied into a looser development environment for model work. No attacker required. Chapter 23
Differential privacy
A bound on how the output distribution changes when one protected record or individual’s data changes, under stated adjacency, privacy budget and algorithm assumptions. Utility and composition matter. Chapter 23
DPIA
Data protection impact assessment: a legal instrument where the applicable high-risk processing trigger is met. Distinct from every general privacy review. Chapter 16
Embedding
A numeric vector representing content for retrieval. Not encryption — partially invertible, and classified at the sensitivity of its source. Chapter 6
Embedding tampering
Altering stored vectors to skew retrieval results without touching the model. A third tampering target alongside data poisoning and model tampering. Chapter 23
Evasion attack
A crafted input causing misclassification at inference. The model is intact; the input is adversarial. Chapter 17
Excessive agency
An agent holding more permission, autonomy or functionality than its task requires. The blast radius of a successful injection. Chapter 21
Experimental risk posture
Accepting that an adaptive system can behave in ways nobody can fully account for, and bounding that uncertainty rather than treating it as a defect awaiting a fix. Chapter 25
FAIR
Factor analysis of information risk — a method for expressing risk in monetary terms, so it can compete with the other numbers at a board table. Chapter 16
Fine-tuning
Further training that adapts model parameters. Creates a versioned asset and may retain information from tuning data; source deletion alone does not remove that influence. Chapter 6
Five Vs
Volume, velocity, variety, veracity and value — the characteristics of data feeding a training pipeline. Chapter 23
FRIA
Fundamental rights impact assessment. A legal obligation on the deployer of certain high-risk systems, completed before first use and filed with the regulator. Distinct from the provider's conformity assessment. Chapter 16
FRIA owner
The deployer. Worth memorising separately from the conformity assessment, which the provider owns. Chapter 16
Generative adversarial network
A generator and discriminator trained competitively. GANs are one approach to synthetic content; their discriminator is not equivalent to every operational deepfake detector. Chapter 24
Gold image
A trusted baseline model or dataset held as a known-good reference for comparison and rollback, particularly before fine-tuning. Chapter 19
Guardrails
Input and output filtering and policy enforcement wrapped around a model. Compensating controls, never a substitute for architecture. Chapter 27
Human in command
A person or governance body controls whether and under what conditions AI is used. Complements rather than replaces per-action approval or operational monitoring. Chapter 24
Human in the loop
A person approves each action before it takes effect. For consequential or irreversible decisions. Chapter 24
Human on the loop
A person monitors and can intervene. For reversible, lower-impact decisions at volume. Chapter 24
Hyperparameter
A setting chosen before training rather than learned from data. Part of the configuration recorded for reproducibility. Chapter 4
Idempotency
A design property allowing a repeated request with the same identifier to avoid duplicate consequential effects. Chapter 21
Identify, never punish
Encourage safe disclosure of unapproved use through a clearly scoped process. State any amnesty and its limits; discovery is not a promise of immunity for deliberate misconduct. Chapter 11
Incident threshold
The predefined point at which a wrong output stops being expected error and becomes an incident. Without one, AI incident detection has no line to cross. Chapter 14
Indirect prompt injection
Malicious instructions hidden in content the model reads on a user's behalf — a document, a web page, an email. The attacker never touches your interface. Chapter 17
Inference
Running a trained model to produce output. Where runtime attacks land. Chapter 6
Inherent risk
Exposure before the defined controls, under stated scope and assumptions. Chapter 15
Input contract
Documented specification of the data attributes a model requires, verified against production inputs. Guards against silent degradation when a source or format changes. Chapter 20
Lawful basis
The applicable legal justification for a specific processing purpose. Consent is one possible basis in some regimes; additional conditions and purpose limits may apply. Chapter 24
Least privilege for systems
The principle applied to models, pipelines and agents rather than only to people. Each needs its own minimum access, and none should inherit a human's. Chapter 20
Machine unlearning
Methods intended to remove training-data influence under stated assumptions. Exactness, privacy assurance, utility and cost depend on the method/model and need verification. Chapter 6
MAESTRO
A threat modelling method built for AI, addressing multi-agent environments, adversarial machine learning and system autonomy — where STRIDE struggles. Chapter 17
Membership inference
Determining whether a particular record was in the training set — which can itself be the disclosure. Chapter 17
Model card
Documentation of a model's intended use, performance, limitations and evaluation. A transparency and accountability artefact. Chapter 22
Model extraction
Copying useful model behaviour or parameters through queries or other access. Query requirements vary; access, output and rate controls can reduce exposure. Chapter 17
Model inversion
Inferring attributes or representative information about training data from a model. Not necessarily recovery of exact records; distinguish training-data extraction. Chapter 17
Nth party
Vendors beyond the fourth and fifth tiers of the supply chain, down to power utilities and data-centre facilities. Rarely mapped, occasionally the shared point of failure. Chapter 19
Overfitting
Fitting training-specific patterns too closely and generalizing poorly. It can increase privacy exposure, but generalization and leakage need separate evaluation. Chapter 4
Poisoning
Malicious alteration of training or other ingested data to influence system behaviour. Runtime controls may contain harm; validate underlying data/model remediation separately. Chapter 17
Problem families
Classification, regression, clustering, dimensionality reduction, anomaly detection, recommendation and sequential decision-making. If a proposal does not match one, question whether AI is the right tool. Chapter 5
Producer
In architectural terms, the party that designs, develops, tests and deploys an AI product — distinct from the provider who offers it and the customer who uses it. Chapter 20
Provenance
The recorded origin, rights and transformation history of a dataset or model. Must be captured at collection; cannot be reconstructed later. Chapter 23
Provider
An organisation that develops an AI system or places it on the market under its own name. Carries the heaviest regulatory obligations. Chapter 28
Quality dimensions
Accuracy, completeness, consistency, timeliness, validity and uniqueness — the six-part answer to what "good data" means. Chapter 23
RAG
Retrieval-augmented generation. Documents are fetched at query time and placed in the model's input, keeping data outside the weights and access-controllable per user. Chapter 6
Red teaming
Adversarial testing of a system, covering not only security but harmful content, bias and safety failures. Chapter 17
Redress
A route by which an affected person can contest an outcome and have it reviewed. The element most often missing from a transparency answer. Chapter 24
Reinforcement learning
Learning by trial and error against rewards. Carries a distinctive failure mode: optimising the reward given rather than the outcome intended. Chapter 4
Residual risk
Exposure remaining after the evidenced effect of the defined controls, including uncertainty and limitations. Chapter 15
Risk appetite
The broad kinds and amount of risk an organization is willing to pursue in achieving objectives. Mandatory duties remain constraints. Chapter 10
Risk card
Documentation of a model's identified risks, their analysis and planned mitigations. The artefact most often missing from a documentation set. Chapter 28
Risk tolerance
Acceptable variation around an objective within appetite, translated into measurable or otherwise verifiable operating limits. Chapter 10
RPO
Recovery point objective: the approved point in time to which relevant data must be recoverable, reflecting tolerated loss of recent data. Chapter 14
RTO
Recovery time objective: the approved target time to restore the relevant service/process after disruption, derived from business impact. Chapter 14
Scenario planning
Deliberately imagining misuse and malfunction to surface risks that documentation of the known will not reveal. Chapter 28
Secure by design
Building resistance to attack in from inception: owning customer security outcomes, radical transparency and accountability, and the leadership structures to deliver both. Chapter 20
Security for AI / AI for security
Protecting AI systems themselves, versus using AI to strengthen defence. A complete programme covers both directions. Chapter 12
Shadow AI
Unapproved AI use, arising from staff tools, vendor features switched on in existing software, and models pulled from public repositories. Chapter 8
Shared responsibility model
The split of duties between provider and deployer. Evaluation for your use case, oversight and redress never move to the provider, whatever you buy. Chapter 19
Steering committee
Cross-functional body that keeps AI initiatives aligned and makes the calls on budget, scope, risk and ethics. Typically chaired by a CIO, CTO or chief data officer. Chapter 9
System prompt
Instruction text placed before user input to shape behaviour. Configuration, not a security boundary — leakable and arguable with. Chapter 6
Target risk
The intended acceptable exposure after planned treatment; distinguish it from the currently evidenced residual risk. Chapter 15
Test data
A final independent evaluation set representing intended deployment. Reusing it for tuning contaminates the performance estimate. Chapter 22
Three attack surfaces
Development-time, runtime (ordinary infrastructure weakness), and through use. The middle one is underweighted and accounts for much real-world AI compromise. Chapter 17
Token limit
The input/output/context budget of a model. An application may reject, truncate or summarize over-limit content; make the chosen behaviour visible and test completeness. Chapter 6
Tokenization
Converting content into model input units, commonly text tokens. Binary storage does not define it; intermediate representations can retain the source’s sensitivity. Chapter 23
Training-data extraction
Obtaining particular memorized training material from a model; distinguish this from copying model functionality. Chapter 17
Trust but verify
The organising principle of an AI security programme: every output that matters is validated by something other than the model that produced it. Chapter 12
Unbounded consumption
Resource or cost exhaustion through excessive inference. Sometimes called denial of wallet. Chapter 17
Underfitting
Insufficient fit to the task or data patterns. It may be subtle and harmful; assess overall and subgroup outcomes rather than assuming it is safer than overfitting. Chapter 4
Validation data
Data used during development for tuning and model selection; not an untouched final estimate once used to guide choices. Chapter 22
Value alignment
Designing AI to behave consistently with human values, shaped by community feedback, ethical foundations, legal compliance and continuing stakeholder engagement. Differs by organisation and country. Chapter 10
Vector store
The database holding embeddings for retrieval. Governed at the sensitivity of the source corpus, with entitlements enforced at query time. Chapter 6
Verification vs validation
Verification checks the model was built to specification; validation checks it serves its intended purpose. A model can pass the first and fail the second. Chapter 22
