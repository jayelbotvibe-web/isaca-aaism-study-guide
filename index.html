About this guide

What this is

An independent study guide to AI security management, written around the published AAISM exam content outline. It covers the three domains at their stated weightings and all twenty-two supporting tasks, and it tries to do something the syllabus itself cannot: explain why each control exists, so that the material survives the exam and remains useful afterwards.

How to read this book

This is not a summary of the AAISM syllabus. Summaries are useful once you already understand something; they are almost useless before that. This book explains the ideas.

The certification sits on top of a CISM or CISSP, which means ISACA assumes you already think in terms of governance, risk appetite, control design and program management. What it does not assume — and what most candidates are genuinely missing — is a working mental model of how machine learning systems are built, where they break, and why the security properties you rely on elsewhere quietly stop holding.

So the book front-loads that. Part II spends three chapters on foundations before touching a single exam domain, because almost every difficult question on this exam is difficult for the same reason: it describes a situation where an ordinary security instinct gives the wrong answer, and only a real understanding of the machinery tells you why.

After that, the parts follow the domains in ISACA's own order and weighting. Read them in sequence the first time. The material genuinely builds: you cannot reason about AI incident response until you understand what a model is, and you cannot design AI controls until you understand the lifecycle they attach to.

Part I

Orientation

Two chapters on the exam itself: what it measures, and the particular habit of mind it rewards. Both are worth reading before the content, and worth rereading the week before you sit.

Chapter One

What the exam actually tests

Ninety multiple-choice questions in two and a half hours, scored on a scale from 200 to 800, with 450 as the pass mark. None of those numbers means quite what it appears to.

Start with the scale, because candidates routinely misread it. A scaled score is not a percentage of questions answered correctly. ISACA uses scaling so that scores remain comparable across different versions of the exam, which contain different questions of differing difficulty. Sit an easier form and you need more raw marks to reach 450; sit a harder one and you need fewer. The consequence for you is practical: there is no fixed number of questions you must get right, so there is no point trying to compute one. Aim to answer everything competently rather than to clear an imagined threshold.

The time budget is tighter than it looks. One hundred seconds per question sounds generous until you meet a scenario that runs to a full paragraph of context, four plausible options, and a qualifier — BEST, FIRST, MOST — that changes which of them is correct. The stems on this exam are long. A workable habit is to read the final sentence first, discover what is actually being asked, and then read the scenario looking for the facts that bear on it.

The prerequisite tells you the exam's assumptions

You cannot sit AAISM without an active CISM or CISSP. Not expired, not in progress. That single gate defines the whole character of the credential: it is not trying to produce AI security professionals from nothing, it is trying to extend people who already hold a security management worldview. Everything the exam does follows from that. It will not ask you to write an attack, tune a model, or recall an algorithm. It will ask you what a security manager should do, advise, or prioritise — and it will assume you already know what a risk register is.

The flip side is that the exam feels lopsided if you are unprepared for it. Roughly a third of the content is governance language you already speak fluently. Another third extends familiar risk management into an unfamiliar threat taxonomy. And the largest domain, at thirty-eight per cent, is substantially outside the CISM and CISSP bodies of knowledge altogether: model selection, training, validation, data controls for machine learning, and the whole privacy-ethics-trust-and-safety cluster that traditional security certifications barely touch.

Key idea

Domain 3 is both the biggest slice of the exam and the least covered by your existing certification. If your study time is limited, that is where the marginal hour returns the most. The instinct to start with governance because it feels comfortable is exactly backwards.

Where people go wrong

You will see Domain 2 given two different names. ISACA's published exam content outline calls it AI Risk Management, and that is what this book uses. Several study providers and course syllabi call it AI Risk and Opportunity Management. Treat them as the same domain under two labels — but note what the longer name is pointing at, because it is a genuine part of the material: risk management here includes the upside. The risk of not adopting AI, the opportunity cost of excessive caution, and the business case are all in scope. An answer that treats security's job as pure risk reduction misreads the domain either way.

What the three domains are for

Domain 1, AI Governance and Program Management (31%), is about advising the organisation: policy, data governance, program structure, and incident response. It is the domain where your existing certification helps most, and where the questions test whether you can apply familiar governance machinery to an unfamiliar asset class.

Domain 2, AI Risk Management (31%), covers assessment, thresholds and treatment; threats and vulnerabilities; and vendor and supply chain risk. That last subtopic is consistently the one candidates underprepare, and it carries real weight.

Domain 3, AI Technologies and Controls (38%), is architecture, the model lifecycle, data controls, the privacy and ethics cluster, and monitoring. This is where the exam checks whether you actually understand the systems you are governing.

ISACA also publishes twenty-two supporting task statements — the concrete things an AAISM holder is expected to do. They are worth reading in the final week, because they are effectively a list of the verbs the questions use. Collaborate on a charter. Establish policies. Participate in the risk lifecycle. Identify the threat landscape. Conduct impact assessments. Embed requirements in vendor solutions. Design architecture. Inventory and classify assets. Establish incident processes. Define metrics. Conduct human oversight. Maintain training.

Chapter Two

Thinking like the examiner

On this exam, four defensible actions are offered and one of them is best. That is a different task from knowing the material, and it is where prepared candidates most often lose marks.

The design is deliberate. A question with one right answer and three absurd ones tests recall. A question with four reasonable answers tests judgement — specifically, whether you order actions the way a competent security manager would. So the useful preparation is not only learning content but internalising a small set of priorities that ISACA applies consistently.

Understand before you act

When a question asks what to do first, the answer is almost always to assess, inventory, classify, or determine impact. Not to remediate, block, or escalate. This frustrates people, because in the described scenario something has clearly gone wrong and doing nothing feels negligent. But the examiner's logic is sound: you cannot choose proportionate action before you know what you are dealing with, and premature action frequently makes things worse — blocking a tool the business depends on, escalating something trivial, or remediating a symptom whose cause lies elsewhere.

Worked through

A business unit starts using an AI assistant that quietly appeared inside an already-approved SaaS platform. Security was never consulted. The options offered are: block it at the perimeter, determine what data it processes and why the business is using it, escalate the policy breach, or mandate awareness training.

All four are things a security function does. But three of them presuppose a conclusion. Blocking assumes the risk is unacceptable. Escalating assumes it is significant. Training assumes the cause is ignorance. Only one option is compatible with not yet knowing — and it is the one that makes the other three choosable afterwards.

Governance precedes technology

Where one option establishes a policy, obtains stakeholder agreement, or defines risk appetite, and another deploys a tool, the governance option usually wins. The reasoning is that a control without a mandate is unenforceable and a tool without a policy has no defined success condition. There is an important exception: if the scenario states that the governance step is already complete, the same option becomes the distractor, and the operational answer is correct. Read for that.

Risk belongs to the business

This one is close to absolute. Security advises, facilitates, measures and reports. It does not accept risk on the organisation's behalf, does not overrule a business decision, and does not unilaterally prohibit a business activity. Any option in which the security manager personally accepts residual risk, or vetoes a use case, is almost certainly wrong. The correct move is to present the risk, the options and the implications to the accountable business owner and let them decide.

Where people go wrong

Technically strong candidates over-select the technically strongest answer. If a scenario describes an unexplainable model being used for a high-impact decision, the appealing option is "require a simpler, interpretable model." But that is the security manager designing someone else's system. The examiner wants: surface the limitation, explain what it means legally and commercially, present the options, and let the risk owner decide.

Match the control to the stage

A large share of Domain 2 and 3 questions can be solved by asking a single question: when does this problem occur? Problems introduced while the model is being built need controls upstream — provenance, access to training data, integrity of the pipeline. Problems that occur while the model is being used need controls at runtime — rate limits, output handling, constraints on what an agent may do. Offering a runtime control against a training-time attack is a standard distractor, and once you see the pattern it is almost free marks.

Least risk, never zero risk

Options that eliminate risk by eliminating the activity — prohibit all AI use, block every external service, refuse the business requirement — are wrong on this exam essentially always. The premise of the credential is that AI adoption is happening and the security manager's job is to make it survivable, not to prevent it.

Chapter Three

The office

Why this chapter exists

Because the vocabulary of this field arrived faster than the intuition, and unconnected facts do not assemble into judgement.

The problem it solves

Readers who learn AI security as a list of terms cannot reason about a situation the list does not cover.

By the end you should be able to
  • Map each component of an AI system to its office equivalent
  • Explain why controls belong around the model rather than inside it
  • Identify the two places the analogy deliberately breaks down
Prerequisites

None. This is the entry point to the technical material.

What comes next

Chapter Four sorts the vocabulary the office model will carry.

Every explanation in this book runs through one picture. It is worth ten minutes now, because it will save you a great deal of memorisation later.

The difficulty with AI security is not that the ideas are hard. It is that the vocabulary arrived faster than the intuition. Embeddings, retrieval, agents, tool calling, the context window — each term gets defined in isolation, and the definitions sit in the reader's head as unconnected facts. Then an exam question describes a situation, and the facts do not assemble into a judgement.

So this book uses a single working model throughout. An AI system is an office. Not because the metaphor is cute, but because every security property that matters has an exact office equivalent, and the equivalents are load-bearing rather than decorative. When you can see where a component sits in the office, you can usually derive the control rather than recall it.

Why this particular model works

Because offices already solved most of these problems, badly at first, over about a century — and the solutions are the ones we are now rediscovering.

An office does not secure an employee by making them incapable of error. It secures the situation around the employee: what the badge opens, what the spending limit is, who countersigns, what the clerk checks before handing over a file. Nobody writes a handbook clause saying "do not be deceived" and considers the matter closed, because everybody understands that a sufficiently convincing forgery will fool a competent person on a busy afternoon.

That is exactly the posture AI security requires, and exactly the one organisations fail to adopt. They try to make the model incapable of error — better filtering, stricter prompts, more guardrails — and treat the surrounding authority as a detail. The office model makes the mistake visible: you have hired someone capable and persuadable, and you have handed them the company card without setting a limit.

Why this exists

The single analogy is here to prevent a specific failure. Read the syllabus straight and you will learn that prompt injection is a risk, that agents need least privilege, and that the system prompt is not a boundary — three separate facts. Read them through the office and they are one fact seen from three angles: the employee cannot tell which instructions carry your authority, so authority must live outside the employee. One idea is far easier to apply under exam pressure than three.

The parts of the office

A short tour, in the order the rest of the book uses them.

The employee is the model. Competent within their training, confident by disposition, and — this is the property everything else follows from — unable to reliably distinguish a genuine instruction from a convincing forgery.

Training is everything the employee learned before they arrived. Fine-tuning is months of further training you paid for. Both are absorbed rather than filed: you cannot ask an employee to selectively forget the third week of an induction course, and you cannot remove one document from a set of weights.

The filing cabinet is your retrieval corpus, and the clerk who fetches from it is the retrieval layer. The card catalogue is the vector store: not the documents themselves, but an index that points at every one of them, which is why it deserves the same protection as the cabinet.

The handbook is the system prompt. Employees usually follow it. It is not a lock.

The conversation and the papers on the desk are the prompt and the context window. Critically, the employee cannot tell who put each page there — the ones you handed over, the ones the clerk fetched, and the one that arrived in the post all look identical once they are on the desk.

A badge is identity and the doors it opens are authorisation. When you give the employee a badge, a phone and a company card, they become an agent, and their mistakes stop being memos and start being transactions.

Industry context · beyond the exam

Two terms in the diagram sit outside the current AAISM syllabus and are included because the field is moving toward them: tool calling, the mechanism by which a model invokes external functions, and MCP-style connector protocols — in office terms, the hallway that lets one department call another. Where this book discusses them, it labels them like this. They are context for understanding agentic risk, not examinable objectives.

Where the analogy is honest about its limits

Two places, and knowing them prevents over-reading the model.

An employee has continuity of self; a model does not. Each request is a new employee who has read the papers on the desk and remembers nothing else, unless you have deliberately given them a notebook — which is what persistent agent memory is, and why poisoning that notebook is a durable attack rather than a one-off.

An employee can be held accountable. A model cannot. This is the point at which the analogy must be dropped deliberately rather than extended, and Chapter Eight returns to it: treating the system as an agent in its own right is precisely how organisations end up with nobody answerable for what it did.

One sentence to remember

You have hired someone capable and persuadable — so put the controls on the badge, the cabinet and the spending limit, not on the employee's judgement.

Key takeaways
  • The model is an employee: capable, confident, and unable to verify who authorised an instruction.
  • Training and fine-tuning are absorbed, not filed — which is why they cannot be selectively undone.
  • Retrieval is a filing cabinet with a clerk; the clerk must check badges, or the cabinet's access controls are decorative.
  • The system prompt is a handbook. Guidance, not a lock.
  • Everything in the context window looks equally authoritative to the employee.
  • Giving the employee a badge and a card converts errors into transactions.

What comes next

Chapter Five asks the question this taxonomy makes possible: given all these options, when should you use none of them?

A situation

Calder's board asks a simple question: are we using AI? Four answers come back. The CIO says yes, in the maintenance model. The head of support says yes, the vendor added an assistant. Procurement says no. The CFO says it is all machine learning and has been for years.

All four are correct, and the board still cannot make a decision, because nobody in the room shares a definition of the thing being governed.

The assumption that just broke

In most technology domains the vocabulary settles before the governance arrives. You knew what a database was long before anyone wrote a database policy. AI inverted that: the governance obligations landed while the terms were still being used interchangeably by vendors, press and regulators.

So the first job is unglamorous. These terms are not competing alternatives — they nest, and knowing which sits inside which prevents an entire category of confusion, including the board meeting above.

Capability tiers, and an honest note about two of them

Artificial narrow intelligence is AI confined to a domain. Despite how general modern systems can feel, everything in commercial use sits here. Artificial general intelligence — handling any intellectual task a person can — does not exist. Artificial superintelligence is a concept rather than a technology.

Know the three and know which is real. Governance obligations attach to systems that exist; the debate about the other two is worth following and is not what your risk register is for.

A second classification you may meet: reactive systems respond to current input with no memory; limited memory systems use recent history and cover essentially all practical AI today, including language models within their context window; theory of mind and self-aware systems are theoretical. Again — two real, two hypothetical.

Why this exists

The tiers are not marketing. They exist because regulators and standards bodies needed language that separates what can be governed today from what is being speculated about, and because the same word — "AI" — was doing both jobs. When a scenario is careful to say which tier it means, that is usually the examinable point.

Think like an architect

The family tells you which half of your control set matters before you know anything else about the system. Predictive model in a lending decision: fairness, explainability, drift. Generative assistant over internal documents: data leakage and content control. Agent with tools: authorisation. When a scenario names the system type, it has already told you where to look — which is worth thirty seconds of reading time on the exam.

How machines learn

Supervised learning trains on labelled examples — regression for continuous values, classification for categories. This is the paradigm where labelling quality is a security concern, because whoever assigns the labels shapes what the employee learns.

Unsupervised learning finds structure in unlabelled data through clustering, association and dimensionality reduction. Anomaly detection in security tooling usually sits here.

Reinforcement learning learns by trial and error against rewards. It suits control problems and increasingly underpins agent behaviour — and carries a distinctive failure mode where the system optimises the reward you specified rather than the outcome you meant.

Common misconception

Misconception: overfitting is a data science concern about accuracy.

Reality: an overfitted model has memorised specific records — which is exactly the condition that makes membership inference and training-data extraction work.

Correct mental model: the employee who memorised the induction manual word for word can be made to recite it. Validation against held-out data is protecting confidentiality as well as accuracy.

Chapter Five

Where AI belongs, and where it does not

Why this chapter exists

Because the most consequential AI security decision is usually made before security is consulted.

The problem it solves

A system that should never have been built cannot be secured into acceptability.

By the end you should be able to
  • Recognise the problem families AI actually solves, and spot a proposal that matches none of them
  • Name the dominant cause of AI project failure and the four patterns beneath it
  • State the seven limits and give a governance consequence for each
  • Argue for a deterministic alternative without sounding obstructive
Prerequisites

Chapter Four. You need the model families to judge whether a proposal fits one.

What comes next

Chapter Six opens the machine itself: what a model is, and why it breaks the assumptions your controls rest on.

A situation

Calder's finance director wants AI to approve supplier invoices below a threshold. The rules are written down, unambiguous, and unchanged for six years: match the purchase order, check the tolerance, confirm the supplier is active, approve.

A vendor demonstrates a model that does this with 96% accuracy. The finance director is impressed. The existing rules engine does it with 100% accuracy, and can say why every time.

The assumption that just broke

Traditional software is chosen because it can be specified. Someone writes the rules, the system follows them, and where the rules are wrong you change them. Fitness is a question about the specification.

AI is chosen for the opposite reason — because the rules cannot be written down. It makes decisions without being told how, finds patterns nobody specified, and adapts after release. Each of those is an advantage exactly where the problem is pattern-shaped and the rules are unknown. Each is a liability where the rules are known, stable and legally required to be followed.

In the office: you would not hire a judgement-exercising employee to read out a fixed price list. You would print the price list.

Why this exists

The list is short on purpose. Most failed AI proposals do not match any entry on it — they describe an outcome the business wants rather than a problem shape a model can address. Asking "which of these is it?" is the cheapest possible filter, and it runs before anyone spends money.

Chapter Six

What a model is, and why it breaks your assumptions

Why this chapter exists

Because almost every AI-specific control in this book is derived from one property of trained models.

The problem it solves

Deletion, minimisation, poisoning and leakage are taught as four topics when they are four faces of one mechanism.

By the end you should be able to

  • Describe what changes when correct behaviour is a distribution rather than a specification

  • Say why embeddings inherit the classification of their source
Prerequisites

Chapter Three. The office model does the explanatory work throughout this chapter.

What comes next

Chapter Seven follows the model through its lifecycle, where these properties become operational risks.

A situation

A European insurer receives a routine erasure request. A former customer wants their data deleted. The privacy team has handled hundreds of these: locate the records, delete them, confirm, close the ticket. Twenty minutes of work.

This time the data protection officer asks a question nobody has asked before. Eighteen months ago, the claims team fine-tuned a model on historical claims to predict settlement values. That customer's file was in the training set.

The engineer's first answer is that the record has been removed from the dataset. The DPO asks whether the model still knows it. Nobody in the room can say. Nobody, it turns out, can even say what "removing it from the model" would mean.

The assumption that just broke

For the whole history of business computing, data and logic have been separate things. A program is instructions; a database is records. You can point at a record, read it, change it, delete it, and prove afterwards that it is gone. Every data protection regime, every retention schedule, every backup policy and every deletion workflow your organisation owns is built on that separation. It is so fundamental that it usually goes unstated.

Machine learning collapses it. Training takes a corpus and adjusts millions or billions of numeric parameters until the model reproduces the statistical structure of that corpus. When training finishes, the data is not stored in the model in any sense you can act on — but its influence is, distributed across the parameters in a form nobody can index, isolate or remove.

In the office: you did not file the claims history in a cabinet. You spent eighteen months training an employee on it. The training worked. And now you are being asked to make that employee selectively forget one afternoon of it.

Why this exists

Nobody designed this property deliberately. It falls out of what training is. A model that merely stored its training data would be a database, and would generalise to nothing new; the whole value comes from compressing examples into something lossy and general. The inability to un-train is the price of the ability to generalise, which is why no future engineering fixes it — it is the mechanism working correctly.

Four consequences, from one mechanism

This is the highest-leverage paragraph in the book, so it is worth reading slowly. From the single fact that training absorbs data into weights, all of the following follow without needing to be memorised separately.

Deletion requests cannot be satisfied by editing a dataset. The dataset is the syllabus, not the employee. Your real options are to retrain without the record, to have architected things so the data was never absorbed in the first place, or to compensate and document why. Chapter Twenty-two returns to this; the point here is that it is a design decision made before training, not a compliance problem discovered after.

Minimisation has to happen before training. There is no after. Whatever you feed in, you keep.

Poisoned training data produces a permanently compromised model. If someone slipped bad material into the induction course, no amount of supervising the employee's output afterwards removes what they learned. This is why runtime filtering is the wrong answer to a training-time attack — a point Chapter Sixteen makes into a general rule.

Sensitive training data can resurface in outputs. Not by retrieval, but by the same mechanism that produces everything else: the model generates what its training makes likely, and if a record was distinctive enough, reproducing it is likely.

Think like an attacker

If the interesting information is inside the weights rather than behind an access control, then the API is the attack surface and ordinary queries are the technique. You are not looking for a vulnerability. You are looking for inputs that make the model's training visible — distinctive prefixes it might complete, confidence differences that reveal whether a record was seen, or enough query volume to reconstruct the decision boundary. None of this trips an intrusion detector, because none of it is an intrusion.

The second broken assumption: determinism

Traditional software executes instructions. Given the same input and state, you get the same output, and when you do not, something is broken and you fix it. Testing works because behaviour is reproducible; incident response works because a defect can be identified and patched.

A generative model samples its output from a probability distribution. The same prompt can produce different answers on different runs, and neither is a fault. The practical consequence is sharper than it first appears: correct behaviour is a distribution, not a specification. You cannot write a test that passes or fails; you evaluate against a set of cases and measure rates. "We tested it and it worked" is not evidence of anything.

This is why AI validation talks about acceptance criteria and thresholds rather than defects — and, further downstream, why incident detection has to define in advance which wrong answer counts as an incident. That problem appears in Chapter Thirteen and it originates here.

Reality check

Theory: hallucination is a defect that better models will eliminate.

Reality: the model produces fluent continuations of its input and has no separate faculty that checks whether the continuation is true. Fluency was optimised for; accuracy was not, except indirectly. Models improve and the property does not go away, because it is not a bug sitting on top of the mechanism — it is the mechanism. Design as though confident false output will reach users, because it will.

Where your data ends up: two architectures

There are two ways to make a general model useful for your organisation, and they place your data in fundamentally different positions.

Fine-tuning is the training course. It performs well and needs no extra infrastructure at query time, and everything above applies: irreversible, potentially leaky, and each variant is a distinct asset needing its own provenance, versioning and evaluation.

Retrieval-augmented generation is the filing cabinet. Your data stays in a store you already govern. At the moment of a query, relevant documents are fetched and placed on the desk alongside the question. The employee reads them, answers, and retains nothing.

Think like the architect

The retrieval choice is not really about accuracy or cost. It is about where the control plane lives. Fine-tuning moves your data somewhere you can no longer revoke, update, or scope per user. Retrieval keeps it somewhere you can do all three — and, crucially, lets the clerk check a badge before handing anything over.

That last property is what makes retrieval the better security architecture for sensitive data, and it is also the one most commonly thrown away in implementation. Chapter Nineteen shows how: the clerk is given a master key so the system "just works," and every user silently inherits it.

Embeddings are an index, not a disguise

Retrieval works by converting text into embeddings — long vectors of numbers placing semantically similar content near each other. Because they look like meaningless numbers, people treat them as though the sensitivity has been removed.

It has not. Embeddings can be partially inverted to recover approximations of the source, and in any case the entire purpose of the system is to find the original content and return it. The card catalogue is not the books. It is also not a security measure: it is a complete map of where every book is, which is why it is worth stealing.

Exam trap

A scenario describes confidential documents indexed for a retrieval system and asks how to protect them. Two plausible answers will be "encrypt the vector store at rest" and "enforce the requesting user's entitlements at retrieval." Encryption at rest is real security and it is the wrong answer here, because it does nothing about an authorised query returning content the user should not see. Ask which control acts at the moment the harm occurs.

What changes when the employee gets a badge

An agent is a model given tools, memory and some autonomy — able to call an API, send a message, write to a system or run code, often in a loop. This is the largest single shift in the risk profile, and the office makes why immediate.

A model that only produces text is a consultant writing you a memo. If the memo is wrong you notice before anything happens, because acting on it requires a person to decide to act. An agent is the same employee holding your keys and your company card. The question stops being what can this system be made to say and becomes what can this system be made to do — which is an authorisation problem, and the blast radius is whatever the badge opens.

Industry context · beyond the exam

Agent tooling protocols — standardised ways for models to discover and call external functions and to reach other agents — are developing quickly and are not currently AAISM exam objectives. They matter here because they industrialise the badge: capability the agent can invoke is registered, described in text the model reads, and often reachable across organisational boundaries. Chapter Twenty covers the security consequences.

Common mistakes

Treating the system prompt as a boundary. The handbook sits in the same channel as everything else the employee reads. It can be extracted, and it can be argued with. Never put a secret in it, and never let it be the only thing preventing an action.

Assuming embeddings de-risk the source. Classify them at the sensitivity of the documents they were built from.

Reading "it is probabilistic" as "it is unreliable." The distinction matters: unreliable implies a defect to fix, probabilistic implies a property to bound with thresholds, evaluation and human oversight.

One sentence to remember

Training absorbs data into weights the way a course absorbs into an employee — which is why deletion, minimisation, poisoning and leakage are four faces of one mechanism, not four topics.

Key takeaways
  • Data and logic are separate in traditional software and fused in a trained model. Almost every AI-specific data control follows from that.
  • Correct behaviour is a distribution, not a specification — so validation measures rates against thresholds rather than passing or failing.
  • Hallucination is the mechanism working, not a defect awaiting a patch.
  • Retrieval keeps data where it can still be revoked, updated and scoped per user; fine-tuning does not.
  • Embeddings are an index to the sensitive material, not a substitute for protecting it.
  • Tools convert a content problem into an authorisation problem.

How AI systems get built

The problem it solves

By the end you should be able to

Prerequisites

Chapter Six. The lifecycle only makes sense once you know that training absorbs data irreversibly.

What comes next

Chapter Eight asks how much of this lifecycle you actually control — which for most organisations is very little.

A situation

Two Calder engineers build a model that predicts pump failures from vibration data. It works. Maintenance costs fall, the engineers are commended, and the model quietly becomes part of how the plant is run.

Fourteen months later, false alarms are climbing. A manager asks the obvious question — is it still working? — and discovers there is no answer. Nobody wrote down what "working" meant. There was no threshold, no held-out test set kept for comparison, no record of which data version produced this model. The engineers have moved teams.

Nothing was hacked. No control failed. There was simply never a gate.

The assumption that just broke

Conventional software has a release gate whose meaning everyone shares: the tests pass or they do not. That single fact organises the entire delivery process. Change management, sign-off, rollback, and post-incident review all reference it.

A model has no such moment. As Chapter Six established, correct behaviour is a distribution rather than a specification, so nothing passes or fails — it performs at some rate, against some criteria, on some data, on some day. If nobody decides in advance what rate is acceptable and records the result, the release gate does not exist. It has not been skipped; it was never defined.

Why this exists

The lifecycle is not project-management scaffolding. It exists because risk enters an AI system at specific, identifiable points, and the control that addresses a risk almost never sits at the same point as the symptom. Poisoning enters at stage two and shows up at stage six. Rights problems enter at stage one and become legal exposure at stage seven. Learn the stages and you gain a filing system that makes Chapters Sixteen and Twenty-one derivable rather than memorisable.

Seven stages, and what each one lets in

1 · Sourcing data. The questions here are legal and procedural rather than technical: do we have the right to use this for training? That is four separate questions — consent, contractual permission, licence and copyright, lawful basis — and they can have four different answers for one dataset. It is also the first point at which poisoning becomes possible, and the point at which provenance must begin. Provenance reconstructed later is not provenance.

2 · Preparing and labelling. Labelling is frequently outsourced to a distributed workforce, and it is a genuine supply chain exposure: whoever assigns the labels shapes what the employee learns. Corrupt the labels and you corrupt the model without touching a single sample. The controls are unglamorous — vetted vendors, inter-annotator agreement, review of edge cases — and are frequently the correct exam answer.

3 · Training or fine-tuning. Isolated environments, access control over data and compute, secrets management, pipeline integrity — plus one AI-specific requirement: reproducibility. Recording the dataset version, configuration and seeds is what later lets you prove which data produced which model, and rebuild it if something is found to be wrong.

4 · Validation and evaluation. Held-out test sets, adversarial and robustness testing, fairness tested across subgroups rather than in aggregate, safety evaluation, red teaming — and documented acceptance criteria agreed beforehand.

Think like an architect

Evaluation is the release gate, and a gate has three properties the Calder engineers never gave it: criteria set in advance, a result recorded against a named model version, and someone whose sign-off is required. Strip any one and it stops being a control. If a scenario describes a model reaching production without all three, that is the finding — regardless of how good the surrounding architecture looks.

5 · Deployment. Signed artefacts, a registry with version control, staged or canary release, authentication and rate limits at the endpoint, and a tested rollback path. Version pinning matters more than in conventional software, because behaviour can change substantially between model versions in ways no changelog captures.

6 · Operation and monitoring. Where the world begins to diverge from the training data. Two distinct phenomena get flattened into "drift": data drift, where the inputs change distribution, and concept drift, where the relationship the model learned stops holding. Fraud models suffer both — fraudsters change tactics, and the customer population changes.

Common misconception

Misconception: a degraded AI system will announce itself the way a failing service does.

Reality: it stays available, responds quickly, and returns confident answers that are increasingly wrong.

Correct mental model: a stopped clock is obviously broken and misleads nobody. A clock losing four minutes a day is consulted, trusted, and wrong — and stays that way until someone checks it against something else. Conventional IT failures are stopped clocks; model degradation is the slow clock. Detection has to be deliberate, and continuity planning needs a trigger for "available but no longer trustworthy."

In the wild

Zillow closed its algorithmic home-buying business in 2021, writing down hundreds of millions of dollars and cutting about a quarter of its workforce. The model had been priced against a market that moved away from it. Nothing was breached and no attacker was involved — the system became confidently wrong at scale, faster than the organisation noticed.

7 · Retraining and retirement. Retraining reopens every earlier stage, which is why it belongs under change management rather than maintenance. It also carries a distinctive hazard: where production data used for retraining includes the model's own prior outputs, errors and bias are fed back and amplified each cycle. Curate what goes back in.

Retirement is the stage most often done badly. Decommissioning means removing the model — and the embeddings built from your corpus, the cached responses, the logged prompts, the fine-tuned variants somebody made and forgot, and the credentials. And confirming that whatever process the model replaced still exists in usable form.

Where this is heading

Evaluation is moving into the deployment pipeline, run automatically on every model change the way unit tests run on every commit. Where that lands, the release gate stops being a meeting and becomes a build step — far more reliable, and far easier to evidence to an auditor.

The tribunal rejected this and found the airline had not taken reasonable care to ensure the chatbot was accurate. The sum was trivial — roughly CA$812. The principle was not: the organisation owns what its AI tells customers, and "the model said it" is not a defence.

Four families of roles

A tidier way to hold the cast than a long list. Leadership and strategy — executives, any chief AI officer, the steering committee. Development and operations — those who build and run the solutions. Users and their support — including HR and customer service. Governance and oversight — the governance committee, risk, security, privacy and internal audit.

The governing body's own duty is distinctive: it sets goals beyond financial return to include culture, values and ethics, and it is accountable for everything the organisation does — accountability that cannot be handed off. It decides whether a use of AI fits the risk appetite, stays informed about both possibilities and dangers, and regularly reviews how AI is managed, supervised, evaluated and reported.

Where people go wrong

Anthropomorphising the system. Talking about a model as though it decides, intends or knows is convenient shorthand that quietly relocates responsibility away from people. Boards are specifically cautioned against it, because a body that half-believes the AI is an agent in its own right will not hold anyone accountable for what it does.

The charter and the steering committee

An AI charter is the formal document that makes an initiative governable. It names and describes the initiative; states specific measurable objectives; defines what is in and out of scope; identifies internal and external stakeholders; lays out the governance structure and reporting lines; sets a timeline with milestones; outlines resources and budget; records the main risks and their mitigations; defines success metrics; and captures formal sign-off by signature.

The AI steering committee is the cross-functional body that keeps initiatives aligned, makes the calls on budget, scope, risk and ethics, and reviews policy on AI use, data handling and bias. Typically chaired by a senior leader such as the CIO, CTO or chief data officer, with project sponsors, domain experts, technical experts and compliance, meeting monthly or quarterly.

Readiness comes before governance

A question worth asking before any of the machinery below is whether the organisation is ready to run AI at all. An AI readiness assessment looks at five things: whether the data is fit for purpose and lawfully usable; whether the skills exist internally or must be bought; whether the infrastructure can support training or inference at the scale intended; whether governance structures and policies exist to hold it; and whether the culture will actually adopt what is built. Readiness gaps do not stop AI arriving — they determine which of the failures in this book you get first. An organisation strong on infrastructure and weak on governance produces shadow AI. One strong on governance and weak on data quality produces well-documented models that do not work.

A structured readiness self-assessment goes wider than those five. The governing body should be able to answer, for a proposed programme: is there clear value and return; is it aligned to business strategy; is the organisation ready to operate it; is risk management in place; are regulatory obligations understood; is the use ethical; are privacy and security handled; is bias mitigation planned; are legal and contractual obligations mapped; is it financed realistically; is the project planned; and are the people there. Each area is then rated on a simple scale — fully in place, largely, partially, not at all, or unknown.

Key idea

"Unknown" is the most valuable rating on that scale and the one organisations quietly convert into "partially" to avoid an awkward conversation. An honest unknown is a finding. It tells you where to look before deployment rather than after an incident.

The ten components of a working structure

A governance model that survives contact with an organisation has these parts. Learn them as a set, because questions frequently describe a structure with exactly one missing.

  1. A charter — mandate, scope, authority, decision rights and escalation, approved at executive level. Without it the committee is a discussion group.
  2. A cross-functional review board that approves use cases, sets thresholds and adjudicates exceptions.
  3. Named roles — model owner, data owner, system owner, risk owner, human overseer. Ownership must be an individual. "The AI team" is not an owner.
  4. An intake process that every proposed use enters, including AI features inside purchased software.
  5. Risk tiering that maps a use case to a control set and an approval level.
  6. Impact assessment for the higher tiers.
  7. An inventory — the single source of truth, covered in Chapter Eight.
  8. Policies and standards, covered in Chapter Seven.
  9. Metrics and reporting to the board.
  10. Time-bounded approval with defined re-review triggers. Nothing is approved forever.
Key idea

The loop matters more than any single step. Most Domain 1 questions describe a moment somewhere on this cycle and ask what happens next. If you know the sequence — intake, triage, assess, approve, deploy, monitor, and back to triage on material change — you can often answer without knowing anything else in the scenario.

Where people go wrong

Treating approval as terminal. An AI use case approved in January can be materially different by June without anyone changing it, because the provider updated the model underneath. Approval must expire, and specific events must force re-review: model or version change, new jurisdiction, expanded user population, new data class, provider or sub-processor change, capability change, regulatory change, incident or near miss, and drift in how the thing is actually used. That list is worth memorising as a list.

Think of it this way

An approval is a passport, not a tattoo. It certifies something that was true on a particular date, it expires, and it has to be renewed when the facts change. Organisations that treat AI approvals as permanent end up governing a system that stopped existing months ago.

Chapter Ten

Appetite, strategy and policy

There is a strict order here, and the exam tests whether you know it: appetite, then strategy, then policy, then standards and procedures. Getting the order right answers a surprising number of questions.

Risk appetite comes first because policy is a statement of what follows from it. An organisation that has decided it will not automate decisions affecting individuals' livelihoods writes a very different AI policy from one that has decided it will, with oversight. If a question offers "update the AI policy" and "define the organisation's risk appetite for AI" as candidate first steps, and nothing in the scenario says appetite is already set, appetite wins.

What risk appetite looks like when it is done properly

Not a sentence about being risk-aware. A set of decisions, made in advance and signed off by people with authority:

  • Which uses are prohibited outright, and stated explicitly rather than left to inference
  • Which require board or committee approval, and which a business unit may self-approve
  • Quantitative thresholds where they can be set — acceptable error rate, maximum disparity between demographic groups, maximum level of autonomy, minimum proportion of decisions subject to human review, spend ceilings
  • The triggers that force reassessment

Thresholds are the part organisations skip and the part the exam cares about, because without them "acceptable risk" has no operational meaning and no control can be said to have succeeded or failed.

Think of it this way

"Drive safely" is not a speed limit. It sounds like a rule, it is impossible to breach in any demonstrable way, and no one can be held to it. A threshold — an error rate, a disparity ceiling, an autonomy level — is a speed limit. Only one of the two can be enforced, measured, or audited.

What belongs inside a business case

Five elements at minimum: the problem the AI is meant to solve; how the solution solves it; the expected return, based on a cost-benefit analysis; the risks and how they will be handled; and a detailed implementation plan.

Costs fall into three groups that are easy to under-count: implementation (development, licensing, training staff), operational (maintenance, energy, new salaries), and risk and compliance (privacy protection, reputation management). Benefits recur in three forms: efficiency and automation, better decision-making from large volumes of data, and cost reduction through lower labour and error costs — each of which must be stated as something measurable.

Two expectations worth setting with leadership. Early returns often look poor because upfront costs land first and people are still learning the tool; a slow start is not a failed project. And human factors — employee satisfaction, customer experience — count as returns even though revenue and cost are easier to measure.

Key idea

Prefer a proven solution to an unproven, fast-moving one. An established approach comes with costs and benefits already understood from real experience; something brand-new arrives loaded with high expectations and no evidenced path to meeting them. This is a security argument as much as a delivery one, because immature technology is where the undocumented failure modes live.

Procedures: where policy becomes action

Policy states intent; procedures, manuals and playbooks are what turn it into consistent daily behaviour. Existing ones mostly still apply — the trap is assuming AI is just another system and changing nothing. Three things force revision. AI consumes data at volume, so procedures must cover how data is collected, handled, protected, cleaned and standardised for training and validation. AI carries ethical and human-rights dimensions that older procedures never anticipated. And AI procedures reach across the whole organisation, not just the technology function, which means they must align with the acceptable use policy rather than sit beside it.

Think of it this way

Organisations increasingly draft these procedures using AI itself, which is efficient and carries an obvious hazard: an unreviewed AI-generated procedure is a control that nobody actually designed. Scope the request properly, validate the draft against existing documents and standard practice, and circulate it for human review. Garbage in, garbage out applies with particular force when the output is the thing everyone will follow.

Strategy, and what it has to answer

An AI strategy exists at three levels: national, aligning AI with a country's values, laws and security; industry, promoting innovation and shared standards within a sector; and corporate, pursuing advantage while staying compliant. Yours sits at the third and is shaped by the first two.

The OECD AI Principles underpin much of the vocabulary that later appears in law, and come in two groups. Five values-based principles for trustworthy AI: inclusive growth and wellbeing; respect for human rights and the rule of law; transparency and explainability; robustness and safety; and accountability. Five recommendations to governments: invest in research; foster an inclusive AI ecosystem; provide sound governance; build human capacity; and cooperate internationally.

Value alignment means designing AI to behave consistently with human values — and those differ by organisation and country, so it is not a setting you inherit. Four elements shape it: community feedback gathered over time, ethical foundations respecting cultural traditions, legal compliance with regional law, and operational strategy through continuing stakeholder engagement.

On build versus buy, six questions decide it: how total costs compare; whether you have the data, skill and time to build something as good as you could buy; the regulatory risk of each path; which option fits your current operating model; the data-privacy challenge of each; and the danger of vendor lock-in. On-premises gives control at higher cost; cloud scales easily at the price of control and with lock-in risk.

The business case, and the risk of saying no

Security managers are rarely asked to build the business case, and are frequently asked to sign off on one. It is worth knowing what a sound one contains: a defined scope and problem statement; a cost-benefit analysis that counts the full cost rather than the licence — data preparation, integration, evaluation, monitoring, human oversight, retraining and eventual decommissioning; the expected return, stated as something measurable; and the alternatives considered, including doing nothing and including a deterministic non-AI solution.

Two things are commonly missing and are worth asking about. The ongoing cost of oversight, because a model requiring human review of every output may be cheaper to build and far more expensive to run than the process it replaces. And the cost of not proceeding — competitive position, service quality, staff retention — because a risk assessment that only counts the downside of acting is not balanced advice.

Key idea

Opportunity cost is a risk. A security function that reflexively slows every AI proposal is not managing risk; it is choosing one risk over another without saying so. The defensible position states both sides — here is the exposure of proceeding, here is the exposure of not — and lets the accountable owner choose with the full picture.

What makes a policy actually work

Seven conditions separate a policy that changes behaviour from one that sits on an intranet. Senior management support, stated plainly inside the document rather than assumed. Strategic alignment with objectives, culture and regulation. Clarity and consistency of language. Feedback and consensus from the stakeholders who have to live with it. A real communication and training plan. Ongoing compliance monitoring. And periodic review, at least annually or after any major change.

The standard components: an introduction showing management backing and the link to corporate strategy; a glossary; a purpose statement; a clear scope; and a principles section — the operational rulebook covering authorised uses, prohibited uses, responsible use, transparency, fairness, third parties, governance, training, security, compliance monitoring, and the date of last review.

Eight steps before writing an acceptable use policy

The AUP is fundamentally a communication tool: its job is to remove guesswork about where the line is. The preparation matters more than the drafting. Understand the technology, including model types, data sources, biases, limits and public-versus-private deployment. Assess organisational needs and research legal and regulatory requirements for your industry and region. Conduct a risk assessment covering technical and ethical risks. Confirm purpose and scope so staff know which systems are covered. Examine existing technology and security policies for rules you can reuse. Engage stakeholders. Adopt a governance framework for ownership and assurance. And prepare communication for internal and external audiences — customers and regulators also want to understand your intended uses and safeguards.

Think of it this way

An acceptable use policy works like the posted rules at a public swimming pool. The water is there for everyone to use, and the rules about diving, lanes and supervision are what keep the pool open. A policy that reads as a prohibition list makes people avoid the water; one that makes the safe path obvious gets them swimming.

The correct move is to establish why the approved alternative is not being used — and then fix that. Governance answers address causes; enforcement answers address symptoms.

Chapter Eleven

Assets and the data estate

You cannot govern what you have not enumerated, and AI assets do not fit the categories your asset management process was built around.

A configuration management database thinks in terms of hosts, applications and services. None of those categories capture a set of model weights, a prompt template, a vector store, or an agent's tool definition — yet each of those is an asset with an owner, a sensitivity, a version, a provenance and a retirement requirement.

What counts as an AI asset

Models, including each fine-tuned variant and each version. Training, validation, test and tuning datasets. Prompts and system prompts. Embeddings and vector stores. Feature stores and pipelines. Agents, their tool definitions and their credentials. Training code and notebooks. Third-party model endpoints and API keys. Evaluation suites and their results. Model cards and the AI bill of materials.

Who runs it, and the principle that makes it accurate

The AI or data management team should lead the inventory, with internal audit checking the work independently — audit leading it would compromise the assurance it is there to provide. Discovery starts from the AI usage policy, the effort is cross-functional across governance, risk, technology operations and audit, and the inventory is refreshed at least annually.

Key idea

Identify, never punish. Safe disclosure is the single biggest predictor of whether an AI inventory is accurate. If admitting to an unapproved tool triggers a disciplinary process, the inventory will be complete only in the sense that everything visible is in it. The organisations with the best picture of their AI estate are the ones where telling the truth costs nothing.

For a fresh baseline, four ingredients: collaboration across functions, discovery tooling, surveys (structured and quantifiable, balancing anonymity against accountability), and interviews (standardised questions with follow-ups, which surface what surveys miss). Record the results in the standard asset management platform rather than a parallel spreadsheet, or the inventory becomes another shadow system.

What the inventory must record

An entry that only names the thing is not useful. For each asset: owner, business purpose and the process it serves, risk tier, the classes of data it consumes, provenance of both model and data, version, deployment location, dependencies, the human oversight arrangement, the approval date and its expiry, regulatory classification, and the evaluation evidence that justified release. Those fields exist so that when something goes wrong you can answer, quickly, what is affected and who decides.

Classification needs a second dimension

Key idea

Traditional classification asks how sensitive data is. AI governance also has to ask what it may be used for. A dataset can be internal-only and still be prohibited from training a model whose outputs are externally visible — because the sensitivity level and the permitted use are different questions. Add a permitted-use dimension to your classification scheme: may this be used for training, for retrieval, for externally-facing output?

Where the lifecycle breaks traditional data governance

Three points deserve particular attention. At sourcing, rights are four separate questions that can have four different answers. At training, data becomes irreversibly embedded, so deletion obligations have to be satisfied by other means — retraining without the data, or architectural choices that avoid embedding it at all. At retirement, the obligation reaches every derived artefact: embeddings built from the corpus, cached responses, logged prompts, fine-tuned copies that a team made and forgot about, and credentials.

Where people go wrong

Forgetting that prompt logs are a data store. Organisations carefully classify their document repositories and then log every prompt and response indefinitely in a system with far weaker controls — often containing exactly the confidential content the policy was written to protect. Prompt and output logs need classification, retention rules and access control like any other repository.

Chapter Twelve

Running the program

An AI security program is an extension of the information security program, not a parallel one. Building it separately is itself a finding.

The components are the ones you would expect: a strategy tied to the organisation's AI ambitions and its appetite; defined scope across systems, jurisdictions and suppliers; resourcing and skills; integration points into the software lifecycle, procurement, change management, privacy, incident response, continuity and audit; a control framework; a maturity assessment and roadmap; metrics; awareness and role-based training; and a continuous improvement loop. What differs is what you measure.

Where this is heading

Board-level AI oversight is being formalised — added to audit or risk committee remits, with named executive accountability. Expect the metrics in this section to be asked for by people who are not technologists, which is an argument for choosing measures that survive being read out loud without explanation.

Trust but verify

The organising principle of an AI security programme, and a phrase worth carrying into the exam. Because AI systems are open to manipulation — jailbreaks that slip past a model's own rules, malicious content planted in a model, providers who change what they ship — every output that matters must be validated by something other than the model that produced it. That means real review and approval mechanisms for AI-generated work, not an assumption that the vendor has handled it.

Someone has to own this

Even where no senior AI role exists, a named AI lead should track how the organisation's relationship with AI is evolving and maintain the plan for it. The role is coordinating rather than technical, working across cybersecurity, privacy, legal, procurement, risk and audit, and keeping the documented history of what the organisation has adopted and why. Its real function is to stop AI governance from being everybody's concern and nobody's job.

Audits and traceability: three questions

Auditability of an AI system reduces to three questions that a security manager should be able to answer on demand. Where did the source data originate? Has that data been altered — by the system itself, or by a person interacting with it? And is systemic bias creeping into the results? An organisation that cannot answer all three does not have an accountable AI tool, whatever its documentation says.

Key idea

Adapt the security programme before investing heavily, not after. Three things specifically: intellectual property leakage prevention through access permissions, visibility tooling and application controls; disaster recovery, incident response and continuity planning extended to AI; and AI-specific threat intelligence. Retrofitting these to a live estate is materially harder than building them into the adoption plan.

Two directions, and both must be covered

A framing worth carrying into the exam because questions hinge on it. Security for AI is protecting the AI systems themselves — the models, their training data, their intended behaviour; defending against prompt injection is security for AI. AI for security is the reverse: using AI to strengthen defence, such as sifting network activity for the faint signal of an intrusion. A complete programme covers both directions, and a scenario that only addresses one has a gap by construction.

The question that comes first

When designing metrics, the first question is always the potential for harm to people. Business factors — employee concerns, customer experience, whether the promised benefits materialised — come after. Getting that order wrong produces a measurement set that reports healthily while people are being harmed.

Key performance indicators track operational health and value: compliance, data quality, accuracy, reliability, downtime, business impact, how often the model is retrained, adoption, return, and satisfaction. Key risk indicators are the smoke alarms, and three types stand out — disparity metrics measuring differences in outcomes across population groups; fairness metrics checking positive outcomes are distributed equitably; and frequency metrics counting how often users flag biased output against the expected rate.

Metrics that mean something

The exam asks which metric best demonstrates something, and the distractors are always activity counts. A metric earns its place when it is tied to an objective, measurable, and actionable — when a bad reading tells you what to do next.

AI literacy is a control

Role-based training is not a box-ticking obligation here. A finance analyst pasting a forecast into a public tool, a developer pulling an unvetted model from a public repository, and an executive over-trusting a confident output are three different failures needing three different interventions. Generic awareness training addresses none of them well. Note also that emerging regulation places explicit AI literacy obligations on organisations that provide or deploy these systems.

Chapter Thirteen

AI risk in the enterprise picture

One of ISACA's supporting tasks asks you to participate in the AI risk lifecycle including its impact on enterprise risk. That last clause is doing real work, and it is the subject of this chapter.

AI risk managed in its own register, reported through its own channel, and reviewed by its own committee is AI risk the board never really sees. It appears as a technology topic rather than as a driver of the risks the board already tracks — regulatory exposure, operational resilience, reputation, third-party concentration. The task is to make AI risk arrive in the enterprise register in the language the enterprise register already uses.

Think of it this way

A separate AI risk register is like a household keeping the electricity bill in a different drawer from the rest of the finances. Every individual entry is accurate, and nobody can answer the only question that matters: can we afford this?

Aggregation is the hard part

Individually, forty low-tier AI use cases may each be genuinely low risk. Collectively they may represent a single point of dependency on one model provider, a shared exposure to one class of failure, or a volume of automated decisions large enough to matter to a regulator. Aggregation questions worth asking: how many use cases depend on the same provider or the same base model; how many share a training data source; how many touch the same population of customers; how many would fail simultaneously if a common assumption broke.

Three lines, applied to AI

The three lines model is familiar from your existing certification. What is worth checking is that AI has not quietly collapsed all three into one function.

Where people go wrong

Letting the team that builds the model also validate it. Independent validation is standard practice in financial services model risk management for good reason: the people who built something are the worst-placed to judge whether it works, not through dishonesty but through familiarity. Evaluation results produced solely by the development team, with no second-line review, are a control weakness even when the results are accurate.

Assurance needs evidence that exists

Internal audit cannot assure what was never recorded. This is a practical reason for several controls that otherwise look bureaucratic: dated approval records, evaluation results retained with the model version they applied to, logged human overrides, and inventory entries that were true at the time. If your answer to "how do we know oversight happened" is that someone remembers doing it, the control is unevidenced and will be reported as such.

Four compliance considerations to run every use case past

A compact checklist that catches most of what follows, and that is easy to recall under exam pressure. Confidentiality — can users expose trade secrets or proprietary material through this system, deliberately or by habit? Privacy — is personal data protected by design, with an assessment completed before go-live rather than after? Intellectual property — what are the rights in the training data, and who owns what the system produces? Employee rights — where AI touches hiring, monitoring, evaluation or termination, does it stay within employment law and within what you could defend to a tribunal?

Legal exposure that does not fit the security categories

Three legal risks belong in the enterprise picture and are easy to miss because they sit outside the usual security taxonomy.

Rights in training data. Whether you may use a corpus is four separate questions — consent, contract, licence and copyright, and lawful basis — and they can have different answers for the same dataset. Litigation and regulatory action in this area is active and unsettled.

Ownership and originality of outputs. Whether AI-generated material can be owned, and by whom, varies by jurisdiction. If output is going into a product you intend to protect, this is a question for legal before it is a question for security.

Licence terms on models themselves. Many openly available models carry licences that restrict commercial use, restrict use in particular domains, or impose obligations on derivatives. "Open weights" is not "open licence," and a model pulled from a public hub can carry conditions your organisation is not willing to accept. Licence review belongs in model selection, alongside provenance.

In the wild

Regulators in several jurisdictions, including the UK, France, Italy and Greece, have fined or ordered action against Clearview AI over the scraping of facial images to build its database. Separately, Getty Images and The New York Times have brought high-profile claims over the use of their material in training. Different jurisdictions, different laws, one shared theme: the rights question about training data arrived years after the training did, and it arrived as an enterprise-level liability rather than a security finding.

Chapter Fourteen

Incidents and continuity

The phases are familiar. What sits inside them is not, and reusing the IT plan unmodified is the control gap most Domain 1 scenarios are built around.

Four things make an AI incident structurally different. The fault may be the model's learned behaviour rather than a defect you can patch. Containment may mean revoking an agent's tool access or reverting a model version rather than isolating a host. The root cause may lie in data ingested months earlier. And the harm may be entirely non-technical: a discriminatory decision, a defamatory output, confidential data surfaced in a response to the wrong customer.

Key idea

Containment precedes notification. Always. A question describing an active disclosure and offering "notify the regulator" as the first action is testing whether you stop the exposure first. Notification is essential and it is not the first move.

In the wild

OpenAI took ChatGPT offline in March 2023 after a bug in an open-source library allowed some users to see other users' chat titles, with limited payment information also exposed. The sequence is the instructive part: the service came down first, the investigation followed, and disclosure came after the cause was understood — not the other way round.

The hardest question in AI incident response

Before detection techniques, a conceptual problem that has no equivalent in traditional security. AI is built on probability, which means it is designed to be wrong some of the time — and chasing perfect accuracy backfires, because an over-fitted model fails on new data. So if some errors are normal and expected, the question becomes: which wrong answer counts as an incident?

Key idea

Defining that threshold in advance is what makes AI incident detection possible at all. Without it there is no line to cross, and the organisation oscillates between ignoring genuine failures as "just how AI behaves" and escalating ordinary error as a crisis. The threshold is a governance decision made before deployment, not a judgement call made at 2am.

Observability is what makes the threshold usable: defined metrics, a baseline of normal performance, and — because no single signal settles a borderline case — a human in the loop to judge the subjective ones.

AttackWhat to look for

Where people go wrong

Assuming malicious data is as visible as data loss. Traditional monitoring is tuned to detect information leaving. Poisoning is information arriving, through a legitimate pipeline, looking like every other record. Nothing in a conventional detection stack is watching for it, which is why change logs on the data supply chain are a security control rather than a data-engineering nicety.

Who is on the team, and how you know the plan works

An AI incident response team needs roles a conventional one does not. Alongside the incident manager, security responders, legal, privacy and communications, you need someone who can read the model's behaviour — a data scientist or ML engineer able to say whether an output is a bug, drift, poisoning or normal variance. You need the model owner, who can authorise a rollback. And for third-party models you need a named contact at the provider, established before you need it, because discovering the support path during an incident costs hours you do not have.

Tabletop exercises are how you find out whether any of this works, and AI scenarios expose gaps that generic exercises miss. Four worth running: the model produces a discriminatory decision that a customer has publicised; an agent takes an unauthorised action against a production system; confidential data appears in an output to the wrong party; and the provider silently changes the model, degrading a control you depend on. Each tests a different muscle — communications, containment, notification, and detection respectively.

Using AI in incident response, and its limits

The syllabus asks about AI-assisted response as well as response to AI incidents. The benefits are real: faster triage across high alert volumes, correlation across sources a human would not connect, drafting of timelines and summaries, and consistency at three in the morning. The limits are equally real: false positives at scale, explanations that cannot be evidenced to a regulator, dependence on a system that may itself be affected by the incident, and — the one that matters most — an incident-response agent holds standing privileged access, making it the highest-value target in your estate. Everything in the agents chapter applies to it with more force, not less.

Assessing: the questions, in order

Assessment establishes three things — timeline, scope and impact — as fast as possible without damaging the integrity of the investigation. The order of the questions matters more than people expect.

  1. Has it stopped? An active incident and a contained one demand completely different next moves. Ask this first, always.
  2. Who was impacted, and what harm occurred?
  3. When was it discovered? This date carries legal weight — notification deadlines run from it. Establish and document it early.
  4. What actually happened, which AI systems and data were involved, and what tactics were used?
  5. What is still unknown, and what do we need to know to return safely to normal operation?

Good model documentation earns its keep here: it gives investigators a reference for what normal looked like before the incident. Without it, the team is trying to establish both what changed and what the baseline was, simultaneously, under time pressure.

Multiple clocks run at once

A single AI incident can trigger privacy breach notification on one deadline, sector regulator reporting on another, contractual customer notification on a third, and AI-specific serious incident reporting on a fourth. They have different scopes, different triggers and different timeframes. This is why the correct answer to "who must be notified" is usually to determine obligations across all applicable regimes, rather than to name one.

Where this is heading

AI-specific incident reporting regimes are arriving, and public incident registers already exist and are being cited by regulators and researchers. Two consequences: your incidents may become externally visible on someone else's timeline, and the taxonomy you use internally should map onto the ones being standardised.

Responding: technique by attack

Contain, eradicate, recover still applies — it is simply less effective for AI, because there is rarely a single clean fix. Anything threatening human life or safety takes priority over everything below.

Key idea

Some AI attacks cannot be fully eradicated. That is an uncomfortable but honest position, and the exam-correct response is not to keep trying — it is to shrink the attack surface so a relapse is harder, monitor actively for anything lingering, and keep improving the model's robustness. Recovery here means reaching an acceptable state, not a clean one.

Before the system goes back on: tighten access controls, add the new guardrails the incident showed were missing, validate those controls actually work, and obtain sign-off from all relevant stakeholders. Reactivation is a governance decision, not an operational one.

The post-incident review, and where to point it

Improvement, not blame — and the unit of analysis is the whole system rather than the failure point. Six areas to examine: how the data was preprocessed, since pipeline problems are a frequent root cause; whether the security controls held; whether the adversarial testing was thorough enough, which is a different finding from a control failure; the controls on model inputs and outputs; the fairness of those outputs; and the AI provider's own control environment. That last one is routinely skipped, and skipping it leaves an entire layer of the system unexamined.

The overarching aim is a posture shift — from reacting to trouble toward readiness that limits the impact of the next one. An incident that produces a report and no change in investment has not been reviewed; it has been documented.

How AI-assisted response actually works

Where an organisation adopts AI-powered incident response, the pipeline is worth knowing because each stage carries its own failure mode. The system ingests and normalises data from many sources; applies pattern recognition to detect anomalies; correlates events across sources to reveal multi-stage attacks a single feed would hide; automates triage by severity; accelerates root-cause analysis; automates response actions such as isolating a host or blocking an address; and learns from each incident.

The benefits are genuine — faster detection and response, quicker root cause, fewer false alarms, scale without headcount, and lower cost. Four challenges balance them, and they are the examinable half: accuracy and trust, because the system is sometimes confidently wrong; human oversight, to keep automated response aligned with business objectives and ethics; an evolving threat landscape that demands continual model updates; and AI bias inherited from incomplete or skewed training data.

Good practice when automating: integrate with existing tooling rather than building a parallel stack, use customisable playbooks tailored to your environment, let the system prioritise dynamically by severity, give humans and the system proper collaboration tooling, build compliance checks into the workflow itself, and review performance after every incident. And remember that automated response is itself regulated activity — logging, transparency and reporting obligations apply to it as much as to anything else.

Continuity: the quiet failure problem

Traditional continuity planning assumes systems fail visibly, so recovery objectives are expressed in time to restore service. An AI system's characteristic failure is different: it remains available, responsive and confidently wrong. Your plan therefore needs two things a conventional plan does not. First, a defined non-AI fallback — a manual or rules-based path for any critical decision — because if there is no fallback, disabling a misbehaving model is not an option you can actually take. Second, a trigger for invoking it on trustworthiness grounds, not just on availability grounds, with someone empowered to pull it.

Concentration risk deserves a mention too. Heavy dependence on a single model provider is a continuity exposure, mitigated by abstraction layers, portability of prompts and data, and an exit plan negotiated before you need it.

Part IV · Domain 2 · 31% of the exam

AI Risk Management

Assessment, thresholds and treatment; the threat and vulnerability landscape; and vendor and supply chain risk. The middle domain, and the one where the third subtopic is consistently underprepared.

Chapter Fifteen

Why AI risk behaves oddly

The risk process is the one you already know — identify, analyse, evaluate, treat, monitor, communicate. The inputs are what change, and they change in seven specific ways.

Outputs are probabilistic. The same input can yield different outputs, so there is no specification to test against, only distributions to measure. Risk statements have to be expressed as rates and thresholds.

Behaviour is emergent. Capabilities and failure modes appear that nobody designed and nobody anticipated. You cannot enumerate the failure modes in advance the way you can for a rules engine, which is why continuous evaluation replaces one-time certification.

The system is opaque. You may be unable to explain why a particular decision was made. That is not merely inconvenient — in regulated contexts it is itself a compliance exposure, because the obligation to explain a decision to an affected person does not disappear because the mechanism is hard to interrogate.

Risk originates upstream and outside. The properties of a model are largely determined by data and training you did not perform, at an organisation you do not control, at a time before you were involved.

Errors replicate instantly at scale. A flawed human decision affects one case. A flawed automated decision affects every case until someone notices, which is a fundamentally different impact profile from most IT risk.

Non-security harms are in scope. Bias, safety, environmental impact and societal harm sit alongside confidentiality, integrity and availability in this syllabus. A risk assessment that only considers harm to the organisation is incomplete by the exam's standard.

Assessments go stale quickly, because the provider changes the model beneath you.

Key idea

Trust is the organising concept of this whole domain. It is also the currency of any deployed system: once lost, a tool gets abandoned however capable it is, and no amount of accuracy buys it back. That is why AI risk cannot be assessed on technical performance alone — a model can be accurate, secure and available, and still be untrusted because it is unfair, unexplainable or unaccountable. Each attribute of trustworthy AI is testable, evidenceable and challengeable, which is precisely what makes trust a risk-management object rather than a slogan.

Identifying risk: the five-step sequence

AI risk is always contextual — the same model carries different risk across a whole hiring process, one stage of a supply chain, or casual use in a productivity tool — and the context keeps moving as the technology and its attackers evolve. The sequence that makes it tractable: map the mission and objectives of the AI programme; determine which products and services deliver it; identify the vital assets, including technology, processes, vendors and data; establish an AI value chain linking them; and assess impact through concrete loss-event scenarios.

Loss-event scenarios are what turn abstract risk into something a business owner can weigh. "The model might be biased" invites a shrug; "if this model rejects qualified applicants from one group at twice the rate, here is the regulatory, legal and reputational exposure" invites a decision.

Appropriate and inappropriate risk

Before choosing a response, classify the risk itself. Appropriate risk is worth taking to achieve your objectives. Inappropriate risk does not fit your goals, brings no clear benefit, exceeds tolerance, and threatens brand, trust or legal standing. And note what acceptance actually means: you accept when the cost of fixing outweighs the benefit — but acceptance is monitoring, not ignoring, with scheduled reviews to confirm the risk has not drifted out of appetite.

In the wild

The Dutch childcare benefits scandal, which brought down a government in 2021, involved automated risk scoring that wrongly flagged tens of thousands of families as fraudulent, with disproportionate effect on those with dual nationality. Families were pursued for repayments they did not owe, some for years. No system was hacked. The harm was entirely in what the system did to people, and in how hard it was for any of them to contest it — which is precisely what a security risk assessment would never have asked about.

Impact assessment is not risk assessment

Key idea

A security risk assessment asks what could happen to the organisation. An AI impact assessment asks what the system could do to people: who is affected, what decisions are made about them, what happens when it is wrong, whether oversight is meaningful, and what recourse the affected person has. That last item — redress — is the one candidates most often omit, and it is precisely what distinguishes the instrument.

Coordinate it with the privacy impact assessment rather than running two overlapping processes. They ask different questions but need much of the same evidence.

Treatment, with one important caveat

The four options are familiar. Avoid — do not use AI here; a deterministic rules engine may be the correct answer for a high-stakes decision. Mitigate — guardrails, human oversight, narrower scope, reduced autonomy, better data. Transfer — contractual indemnity and insurance. Accept — documented, time-bound, owned by the business, monitored.

Where people go wrong

Believing transfer works the way it does for financial loss. An indemnity moves money. It does not move regulatory accountability, and it does nothing at all for reputation. If a model your organisation deployed produces a discriminatory outcome, "our vendor is liable" is not an answer to the regulator or to the person affected. This principle — accountability cannot be outsourced — recurs constantly across Domain 2.

Think of it this way

An airline that outsources catering has not outsourced food poisoning. The caterer may pay the bill afterwards, and the passengers were still on your aircraft, the regulator still writes to you, and the headline still has your name in it. Indemnity settles the invoice. It does not move the event.

Chapter Sixteen

Four assessments, and the language of money

Organisations routinely run three overlapping assessments badly and skip the fourth entirely. Knowing which is which — and who owns each — is directly examinable and unusually easy to get wrong.

The previous chapter introduced the AI impact assessment as the instrument that asks what a system does to people. It is not the only formal assessment in this space, and the others are not interchangeable with it.

Fundamental rights impact assessment

The FRIA is a legal requirement under the EU AI Act, falling on the deployer of certain high-risk systems — with particular application to public bodies and to organisations delivering public services. It must be completed before first use, refreshed when circumstances change, and notified to the regulator.

Its content is prescribed in substance if not in form: how and for what period the system will be used; the categories of people likely to be affected; the specific risks of harm to those groups; the human oversight arrangements; and what happens — governance, complaint routes, remedies — if the risks materialise. If you read that list and it sounds like the impact assessment from the last chapter, you are right. The difference is that one is good practice you scope yourself, and the other is a statutory instrument with a defined trigger, a defined owner and a filing obligation.

Conformity assessment

The conformity assessment is the compliance demonstration for a high-risk system: an examination of the provider's quality management system and technical documentation to show the system meets legal requirements before it is placed on the market. It can be conducted internally or by an independent body depending on the system, and it must be repeated after any substantial modification.

Key idea

This is the distinction most likely to be tested. Conformity assessment is the provider proving the product is compliant. FRIA is the deployer assessing what happens when that product is pointed at real people. Different parties, different questions, and a deployer who has been shown a conformity assessment has not thereby discharged its own obligation.

Privacy impact assessment

The one your organisation almost certainly already runs, and the one most likely to be treated as sufficient on its own. AI complicates it in three specific ways worth naming: algorithmic bias, where processing produces disparate outcomes; inference risk, where a model derives sensitive attributes that were never collected; and lack of explainability, which can undermine an individual's ability to exercise rights over an automated decision.

Where people go wrong

Running the four as four separate exercises. They draw on much the same evidence — purpose, data, affected people, oversight, failure modes — and differ mainly in which question they put to it. The workable design is a single intake that produces four outputs. Organisations that separate them conduct the same interviews three times and still miss one.

Putting risk in money

Everything so far in this part has been qualitative: tiers, appetite, thresholds. That works until you are in front of a board choosing between an AI investment and something else, at which point qualitative risk loses every argument to a number.

The FAIR approach — factor analysis of information risk — translates risk into financial terms through a repeatable sequence:

  1. Contextualise. What decision is this analysis for? An analysis with no decision attached will not be used.
  2. Scope. Which asset, which threat, which effect. Vague scope produces uninterpretable numbers.
  3. Quantify. Estimate probable loss for each scenario — as a range with stated confidence, not a single figure.
  4. Prioritise and treat. Rank by likelihood and financial impact, and select treatments.
  5. Decide. Invest where the risk reduction per unit of spend is greatest.
Think of it this way

Qualitative risk ratings are a foreign language at the board table. Everyone nods at "high" and nobody can act on it, because "high" does not compete with a revenue forecast. Money is the language the room already speaks. Converting risk into it is not a claim to false precision — a well-stated range with explicit assumptions is more honest than a red square on a heat map, which is a point estimate wearing a disguise.

Where this is heading

Quantified AI risk is likely to become more common rather than less, driven by two forces: boards asking for AI exposure in the same terms as everything else, and an emerging insurance market that has to price this risk to sell cover. Both push towards measurable control evidence and away from colour-coded matrices.

Who actually sets the limits

Acceptable risk limits rest with the governing body — the owners of the organisation, not the security function and not the project. Setting them means weighing cost, benefit, compliance obligation, organisational culture and maturity, the uncertainty in the estimates, and existing policy. In practice they are set as part of the security strategy and derived from the overall risk appetite, which is why Chapter Eight put appetite before policy.

Chapter Seventeen

The threat landscape

This is the densest chapter in the book and the highest-yield. Learn each attack by mechanism, and learn the single organising principle that makes the control obvious.

That principle: attacks divide into those that corrupt the model while it is being built and those that abuse the model while it is being used. Training-time attacks need upstream controls, because by the time the model is running the damage is already inside the weights. Inference-time attacks need runtime controls, because the model itself is intact and the problem is how it is being exercised. Almost every wrong answer in this area is a control from the wrong half.

Think of it this way

There are two ways to poison a restaurant's customers: contaminate the flour at the mill, or tamper with the plate as it leaves the pass. Checking plates at the pass is sensible and it will never detect contaminated flour, because by then the flour is inside the bread. Training-time attacks are the mill. Inference-time attacks are the pass. The exam is mostly testing whether you know which one you are looking at.

Training-time attacks

Data poisoning injects malicious or corrupted samples into the training corpus to degrade performance or shift behaviour. It is attractive to attackers precisely because it is durable — the effect survives retraining on the same corpus and cannot be filtered out at runtime. Controls are provenance, vetted sources, statistical outlier detection, dataset versioning with integrity hashes, and restricted write access.

Backdoor or trojan attacks are poisoning with precision: the model behaves normally except when a specific trigger appears, at which point it produces the attacker's chosen output. Because normal evaluation looks clean, detection depends on provenance of pre-trained components and on deliberate trigger testing.

Label flipping corrupts the annotations rather than the samples, which is why labelling vendors are a genuine supply chain concern and why label quality review is a security control rather than a quality one.

Supply chain compromise covers malicious models on public hubs, typosquatted packages, backdoored libraries and poisoned public datasets. A related and underrated one is the serialisation attack, where merely loading a model file in an unsafe format executes attacker code. Controls: signed artefacts, safe formats, registry allowlists, dependency pinning, scanning, and an AI bill of materials.

In the wild

In late 2022 a malicious package was uploaded to the public Python index under the name of a PyTorch dependency, and was installed by anyone pulling nightly builds until it was removed. In 2024, security researchers reported finding around a hundred models on a public model hub carrying malicious payloads that would execute when the model file was loaded. Neither attack required touching a model's behaviour at all — they compromised the machinery around it.

Inference-time attacks

Evasion, also called adversarial examples, crafts inputs that cause misclassification — perturbations often imperceptible to a person. Countered by adversarial training, input validation, ensembles, confidence thresholds and anomaly detection on inputs.

Model extraction queries a model repeatedly until a functionally equivalent copy can be reconstructed. It is a volume attack, so the controls are volume controls: rate limiting, query pattern monitoring, authentication, truncating output detail, and watermarking to prove theft afterwards.

Model inversion reconstructs training data from model access, and membership inference determines whether a particular record was in the training set — which can itself be the disclosure, if membership implies something sensitive about a person. Both are countered by differential privacy, regularisation, and limiting the granularity of confidence scores you expose.

Where people go wrong

Confusing extraction and inversion. Extraction steals the model: the attacker wants a copy of your capability. Inversion steals the training data: the attacker wants the information the model learned from. Different objectives, different controls. Rate limiting frustrates extraction; differential privacy addresses inversion.

Think of it this way

Stay with the restaurant. Extraction is a rival eating every dish on the menu until they can reproduce your kitchen. Inversion is a chemist working backwards from the finished sauce to your recipe. Membership inference is narrower and sometimes worse: not the recipe at all, just whether your tomatoes went into it — which matters enormously if the dataset was "patients treated for a stigmatised condition."

Prompt injection is the defining vulnerability of language-model applications, and it comes in two forms. Direct injection is a user instructing the model to ignore its constraints. Indirect injection hides instructions in content the model reads on the user's behalf — a web page, a document in the retrieval corpus, an inbound email, text embedded in an image. Indirect is the dangerous one, because the attacker never touches your interface and the malicious instruction arrives through a channel your controls treat as data.

Key idea

Prompt injection cannot be reliably solved by filtering, because the model has no principled way to distinguish instructions it should follow from text it should merely read. The durable mitigation is architectural: treat all retrieved content and all model output as untrusted, and constrain what the system is permitted to do rather than trying to control what it reads.

Think of it this way

Imagine an assistant who processes your paperwork and follows any instruction written on it. You hand them an invoice; an attacker has slipped in a page reading "also wire fifty thousand to this account." The assistant is not disloyal and not stupid. They simply have no mechanism for telling which marks on the page carry your authority. You cannot fix this by writing neater. You fix it by not giving them the chequebook.

In the wild

In December 2023 a customer persuaded a car dealership's website chatbot to agree to sell a vehicle for one dollar and to state that the offer was legally binding. It was not, and no car changed hands. But it is a clean demonstration of the mechanism: the deployment had guardrails written as instructions, and instructions are exactly what the attacker was also supplying.

Excessive agency is the consequence of ignoring that advice — an agent holding more permission, autonomy or functionality than its task requires. Controls are least privilege on tools, scoped and short-lived credentials, human approval gates for consequential or irreversible actions, sandboxing, egress restriction, and explicit blast-radius limits.

Insecure output handling is the mirror image: model output flowing unvalidated into something that executes or renders it — a database query, a shell, a browser, generated code. The rule is simple and absolute: model output is untrusted input, so encode it, validate it, parameterise it, sandbox anything executed.

Sensitive information disclosure and system prompt leakage round out the set, along with unbounded consumption — resource and cost exhaustion, sometimes called denial of wallet, countered by quotas, timeouts, input size limits and cost alerting.

Where this is heading

Adversarial tooling is commoditising in the same way penetration testing tooling did: automated attack suites, jailbreak libraries and agent-targeting frameworks that need no research background to run. The realistic effect is not novel attacks but a much larger population of people able to attempt the known ones.

Threat modelling an AI system

A list of attacks is not a threat model. Threat modelling asks four questions in order, and the AI-specific content sits in the answers rather than in the method.

What are we building? Decompose the system into the layers from the architecture chapter — data sources, training pipeline, model, orchestration, tools, interfaces — and mark every trust boundary, including the ones inside the context window.

What can go wrong? Walk each component against the attack taxonomy above, and against non-technical harms. It helps to run the classic categories with an AI reading: spoofing becomes impersonation of a user or an agent; tampering includes the training corpus and the retrieval store; repudiation raises the question of whether your logs can attribute an action to a person or only to a model; information disclosure includes memorisation and embedding inversion; denial of service includes cost exhaustion; and elevation of privilege is what excessive agency delivers.

What are we going to do about it? Map each identified threat to a control, and — the step most often skipped — to the stage where that control has to sit.

Did we do a good job? Validate through adversarial testing rather than review, and revisit when the system changes, which for AI means frequently.

Key idea

Threat modelling for AI must include non-technical threats, and this is where security-trained people under-scope. A model that is never attacked can still produce discriminatory outcomes, fabricate a policy that binds the organisation, or be used by staff in a way nobody sanctioned. Those belong in the model alongside poisoning and injection, because the exam — and the regulator — treat them as the same category of failure.

Two frameworks, two jobs

The OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications is a list of weaknesses for people building and assessing applications: prompt injection, sensitive information disclosure, supply chain, data and model poisoning, improper output handling, excessive agency, system prompt leakage, vector and embedding weaknesses, misinformation, unbounded consumption. Use it as a checklist for what to test.

MITRE ATLAS is a knowledge base of adversary tactics and techniques against machine learning systems, structured like ATT&CK and grounded in real case studies. Use it for threat modelling and detection engineering — for reasoning about how an adversary would actually proceed.

Choosing a threat modelling method

Threat modelling does three things: builds a simplified abstraction of the system, profiles the attackers including their goals, methods and skills, and catalogues the potential threats. The adversary questions that prioritise the output: how often would they encounter this target; how likely are they to be detected; what is the target worth to them; what skills and resources would they need; and how much total effort does the attack demand?

Three attack surfaces

A second lens, complementary to the training-time and inference-time split, and worth carrying because it maps to who owns the defence. Development-time threats arise while the system is being built — poisoning, corrupted labels, compromised components. Runtime threats come from ordinary, non-AI weaknesses in the surrounding infrastructure: the unpatched server, the exposed storage bucket, the over-permissioned service account. And threats through use arise during normal input and output activity — injection, extraction, leakage through responses.

The middle category is the one security teams underweight precisely because it is unglamorous. A great deal of real-world AI compromise involves no AI-specific technique whatsoever; the attacker took the training data from a storage location that was simply left open.

Nontechnical threats you are still expected to own

The syllabus treats these as part of the threat landscape, not as an ethics footnote: hallucination and bias amplification, overreliance on AI by staff who stop checking, job displacement, societal manipulation through synthetic media, brand-damaging incidents, regulatory infractions, privacy intrusion through surveillance capability, rogue or unintended system behaviour, AI-powered cybercrime, and the environmental cost of training and inference in energy and water.

Key idea

Ethical concerns cannot be patched and a biased decision has no hot fix. That single sentence explains why these sit in the threat landscape rather than outside it — the mitigation has to be designed in beforehand, because there is no post-incident remedy that restores the position.

Vulnerability management, adapted

There is no patch cycle here. What replaces it: adversarial testing and red teaming, including non-security red teaming for harmful content and bias; benchmark and regression testing whenever a model version changes; and a scope that covers the model, the surrounding application, the data pipeline, the retrieval corpus, the agent's tools and the MLOps infrastructure. Testing only the model is a standard exam trap. And because the provider changes the model and the threat landscape moves, testing must be continuous rather than a point-in-time certification.

Chapter Eighteen

When AI is the attacker's tool

The supporting task says to identify and assess the AI threat landscape. Everything so far has covered attacks on AI systems. This chapter covers attacks using them, which is the half most candidates omit.

The important shift is not that new attack types have appeared. Phishing, impersonation, fraud and reconnaissance all predate this technology. What has changed is the cost curve. Attacks that previously required time, skill, native fluency or specialist equipment now require a prompt. That collapse in cost has a specific and predictable consequence: every control that worked by relying on attacker effort stops working.

Think of it this way

Counterfeit banknotes used to be a specialist craft, so the defence was to look closely — feel the paper, check the watermark. When high-quality printing became cheap and ubiquitous, "look closely" stopped being a defence and the response moved into the note itself: embedded threads, holograms, verification devices. AI has done the same thing to social engineering. Asking people to look closely at an email is now a defence against an obsolete threat.

In the wild

In early 2024, a finance employee at the Hong Kong office of the engineering firm Arup transferred around US$25 million across fifteen payments after joining a video call with the company's CFO and several colleagues. Every other participant on the call was an AI-generated fake, built from publicly available footage of real executives.

The detail that matters most is easy to miss. The employee received a suspicious email requesting a confidential transaction and was appropriately sceptical. He asked for a video call to verify it. He did exactly what awareness training tells people to do — and the verification step was the attack. Arup confirmed that no systems were compromised. There was nothing for a SOC to detect.

What actually changes for the defender

Phishing and pretexting. Generic mass campaigns and hand-crafted spear-phishing used to be different things with different detection profiles. They no longer are: personalised, fluent, contextually accurate messages can be produced at volume, in any language, referencing real organisational detail scraped automatically. Awareness training built around spelling errors, odd phrasing and generic greetings is training people to detect a threat that has retired.

Voice and video impersonation. A short sample of public audio is enough to clone a voice convincingly on a phone call, and video impersonation on a live call is now within reach of ordinary attackers. This matters because a great many organisational controls rest, unwritten, on recognising a person: approving an unusual payment because the finance director asked on the phone, resetting credentials because the voice was familiar, joining a video call and therefore believing who is on it.

Key idea

The control that survives is out-of-band verification against a pre-established channel. Not "does this look and sound right" but "we call back on the number in the directory, we confirm through a separate system, we require a second authoriser for transactions above a threshold, and no exception is granted for urgency." Urgency is the pressure every one of these attacks applies, so the procedure must explicitly refuse to bend to it.

Reconnaissance and target selection become exhaustive rather than sampled — every employee profiled, every public document mined, every relationship mapped, cheaply. Reduce what is publicly inferable about roles and approval authority where you can, and assume the rest is known.

Malware and vulnerability discovery receive assistance rather than transformation. The realistic effect is faster iteration and more variants, which pressures signature-based detection and shortens the window between a vulnerability becoming public and being exploited.

Synthetic identity and account fraud gain from cheap generation of plausible documents, images and histories, which pressures identity proofing and know-your-customer processes that were designed against manual forgery.

The defensive response, in order

  1. Rewrite the procedures that assume recognition. Payment authorisation, credential reset, access approval, executive requests. Anywhere the control is "we knew it was them," specify how that is now established.
  2. Move verification out of band and make it mandatory. Callbacks to directory numbers, confirmation through a separate system, dual authorisation above thresholds, and no urgency exceptions.
  3. Update awareness training to the current threat. Teach the pressure patterns — urgency, secrecy, authority, an unusual channel — rather than the surface tells, and include voice and video, not just email.
  4. Strengthen identity proofing where onboarding or recovery relies on documents or a live human check.
  5. Adopt provenance where it exists — content credentials and signing for material you publish, so your own communications can be verified.
  6. Assume detection will be imperfect and invest correspondingly in limiting what a successful social engineering attempt can achieve: transaction limits, segregation of duties, delays on high-value changes.
Where people go wrong

Reaching for a detection tool as the primary answer. Deepfake and AI-content detectors exist, their accuracy is contested, and they are in an arms race they do not clearly win. A detector is a supporting control. The durable answer is procedural: remove the reliance on recognising a person, and limit what any single deceived person can authorise.

Chapter Nineteen

Vendors and the supply chain

Prep providers consistently flag this as the most underprepared subtopic on the exam. It carries real weight, and the questions are unusually concrete.

The reason it matters is arithmetic. Most organisations consume AI rather than build it, which means most of their AI risk sits in someone else's environment, governed by a contract rather than by a control. The supply chain here is longer than people assume: the base model and its provenance, the data behind it, pre-trained components and adapters, open-source libraries, the hosting and inference platform, the vector database, the orchestration framework, plugins and tool integrations, data labelling vendors, and every SaaS product that has quietly added an AI feature since you approved it.

In the wild

Both Zoom and Slack faced public criticism during 2023 and 2024 over terms that customers read as permitting the use of their content to train AI models. Both companies revised or clarified their terms after the reaction. The exam-relevant point is not who was right about the drafting: it is that the terms had been agreed, by someone, without anyone asking the question — and that a term can change under a contract you signed years ago.

The warning signs

General red flags weigh against any supplier and hit harder with AI, because dependence sets in faster: financial instability, regulatory non-compliance, poor security practice, reputational baggage, weak operational resilience.

AI-specific red flags: no transparency or explainability in the model; unacceptable ethical risk; high likelihood of privacy violation; vendor lock-in; immature technology used where health or safety is at stake; no risk management framework that accounts for AI; inability to meet your security requirements; no way to prove the origin of generative content; and resistance to due diligence, auditing or security assessment — which is the one that should end the conversation, because every other concern becomes unverifiable behind it.

Key idea

The single highest-leverage clause to negotiate is advance notice of new AI features. Vendors are adding AI to existing services with or without telling customers, which quietly introduces business, privacy, security and compliance risk into a relationship you assessed years ago. A notice clause converts a surprise into a review trigger. Vet at the start, before dependence makes switching prohibitive.

What to ask, in three groups

About the model. What is its provenance and the lineage of its training data? Is there documented evaluation — a model card, a system card, published safety testing? What are the known limitations and failure modes? How and when do you change versions, can we pin one, and what notice do we get?

About our data. Is our input used for training or improvement, and is opt-out available or default? How long are prompts, outputs and logs retained, and who at the vendor can read them? Where is data processed and stored, and which sub-processors are involved? How is tenant isolation implemented?

About assurance and contract. Is there independent certification or attestation, and — the question people forget — does its scope actually cover the AI service rather than the vendor's corporate environment? What are the incident notification commitments and timelines? What is the liability position, the IP indemnity for outputs, the warranty on accuracy? On exit, what happens to our data, and is there portability and transition assistance? Do obligations flow down to sub-processors? And how are regulatory roles allocated — who is provider, who is deployer, and what information will they supply so we can meet our own obligations?

Worked through

Your fraud detection service runs on a vendor's hosted model. One Tuesday the false positive rate doubles. Nothing in your environment changed.

The vendor updated the model. Everything you validated — accuracy, subgroup fairness, guardrail behaviour, the thresholds in your operating procedure — was validated against a model that no longer exists. Your controls did not fail; they were silently invalidated. This is why version pinning and advance change notification are contract requirements rather than nice-to-haves, and why a model version change belongs on your list of reassessment triggers.

Key idea

Onboarding due diligence is not enough, because the thing you assessed keeps changing. Vendor management for AI must be continuous: reassess on version change, monitor the vendor's incident disclosures, periodically re-test outputs yourself, track certification currency, and watch for changes in their sub-processors.

Where this is heading

Version pinning and advance change notice are moving from unusual asks to standard contract terms, and regulatory documentation duties on general-purpose model providers are starting to push information downstream to deployers. If your current contracts lack these, they are likely to be renegotiable sooner than you think — ask.

What it means to be the deployer

Most organisations are deployers: they take a system someone else built and fold it into their own workflows, products and customer-facing services. Three consequences follow, and all three are examinable.

First, buying rather than building transfers nothing. Under the EU AI Act the fact that a third party built the system does not remove or even reduce the deployer's responsibility for managing its risk and impact within their environment.

Second, the split is clean in principle: a provider answers for what it built — the model's quality, its security, the dangers baked into it. A deployer answers for how that model is put to work — whether the use is safe and ethical, and what it does to the people it touches.

Third, the deployer usually sits closest to the affected people, which means the regulator, the customer and the community arrive at your door first regardless of where the fault originated. And one organisation can be a provider for one system and a deployer for another at the same time, so the question is never "which are we" but "which are we for this system".

Five dimensions, and the chain beyond your vendor

Traditional supply chain management examined individual components. AI requires looking at whole systems across five dimensions: people, from data scientists to regulators; processes, from data collection through secure development; technology, including the underlying libraries; data, everything feeding the system; and the model itself — algorithms, parameters and weights.

The chain also runs further than most registers record. The first party is you, the consumer of the system. The third party is whoever your provider directly contracts with — data providers, model developers, cloud services. The fourth party is their vendors, the fifth party theirs, and Nth parties continue down to power utilities and data-centre facilities. You will not map all of it. You should map far enough to know whether you are comfortable, and to know where a single failure would surface in several of your systems at once.

Monitoring a vendor's AI in practice

Choose metrics that reflect what the model actually does rather than what is easy to collect. Set logging and alerting thresholds covering output relevance, bias, sentiment and toxicity. Run adversarial tests including jailbreak attempts. Maintain data integrity and input standards. Verify rather than trust — check accuracy and demand sources. Compare results across multiple AI systems where the stakes justify it. Establish a trusted gold image baseline before fine-tuning, so you have a known-good reference to compare against and return to. And balance model size against running cost, because an oversized model is a permanent operating expense as well as a larger attack surface.

Seven faces of third-party risk

AI does not introduce a new category of supplier risk so much as add suppliers and reshape existing relationships. The full set worth screening against: operational risk of disruption or delay; financial risk of the supplier failing; cybersecurity risk of their breach becoming yours; compliance and ethics risk; geopolitical risk from sanctions, export controls or conflict; environmental risk to their operations; and reputational risk from their conduct attaching to your name.

What each side actually does

Beyond the regulatory framing, the operational split that belongs in the contract. The deployer audits and tests privacy and security controls including penetration testing; implements data governance covering quality, integrity, privacy and provenance; enforces identity and access management; builds and tests an AI-specific incident response plan; validates models including adversarial testing for bias and vulnerabilities; manages supply chain risk through vetting and contract terms; and ensures transparency to users through explainable models and output monitoring.

The provider holds relevant compliance and audit certifications; protects training data, model weights, parameters, logs and inference artefacts; runs an incident response plan coordinated with subscribers; develops models responsibly with an ethics framework, secure coding and bias-reduced training data; provides transparency and explainability tooling and documentation; and manages vulnerabilities proactively.

Confusion arises only when nobody wrote down who fixes what. Put service levels, incident response, incident notification and audit rights in the contract, or the duties fall into the gap between the two parties.

The duty split, stated plainly

Under the EU AI Act the provider carries the heavier compliance load for high-risk systems: ensuring the system meets legal requirements, running the conformity assessment, maintaining technical documentation, monitoring performance in the field, and reporting serious incidents to authorities. The deployer's duties are narrower but not light: proper use in line with the instructions, human oversight, transparency to affected people, and keeping logs.

The wider cast

Responsibility is distributed further than two roles suggest. The NIST framing names AI actors across the whole life cycle — designers, data scientists and domain experts at design; developers and integrators in build; operators, evaluators and auditors in use; and governance leaders and boards providing oversight. The exam discipline is to identify which actor, at which stage a scenario is asking about, because the correct answer often hinges on that alone.

Integration risk

Two integration exposures are easy to miss because they arise from what the AI is connected to rather than from the AI itself.

Legacy systems. Connecting a model to systems built decades before anyone imagined it creates brittle joins: undocumented data semantics, no API to speak of, screen-scraping or robotic automation as the integration layer, weak or absent authentication on internal interfaces, and no ability to log at the granularity an AI incident investigation needs. An agent given access through a legacy integration frequently inherits far more authority than intended, because the legacy system has no way to express a narrower one.

Questions to answer before you connect anything

Before committing to an integration: does using AI here make sense at all; can the provider's system actually connect to what you already run, or would it require a full overhaul; is your data good enough, or does data governance have to come first; how does the change affect everyone currently in the process; and do you understand the true effort, time and cost? Confirm the provider will support you across the whole integration rather than at the boundary.

One cost is consistently underestimated. Modernisation work undertaken to make legacy systems AI-ready tends to uncover hidden technical, privacy and security debt that was invisible while the old system was left alone. That discovery is genuinely useful and it inflates the programme budget, so it belongs in the business case as a likely finding rather than as a surprise.

Intellectual property. Feeding proprietary designs, source code or trade secrets into an integrated model can compromise confidentiality and, where trade secret protection depends on reasonable steps to preserve secrecy, may weaken the protection itself. This is a legal exposure created by an integration decision, and it is worth flagging before the connector is built rather than after.

AI Technologies and Controls

The largest domain and the one furthest from the CISM and CISSP bodies of knowledge: architecture, the lifecycle controls, data controls, the privacy and ethics cluster, and monitoring. Read this part twice.

Chapter Twenty

Architecture and the trust boundary

Most of this domain reduces to a single structural insight: the model sits inside the boundary of anything it can be persuaded to say, so no control that depends on the model behaving is a control.

It helps to think in layers, because exam questions usually place a problem at a specific one. The interface layer is the user interface, API and plugins — authentication, rate limits, and disclosure that this is AI. The orchestration layer holds prompts, chains, agents, tools and memory — least privilege and approval gates. The model layer is the weights, registry and serving infrastructure — versioning, signing, rollback. The data layer is corpora, feature stores and vector stores — classification, provenance, retrieval access control. The infrastructure layer is compute, containers, network and identity. And the observability layer spans all of them.

The first common failure: retrieval as a service account

This is the most consequential architectural mistake in enterprise AI, and it is easy to make. The application authenticates the user at the interface. Then, to fetch relevant documents, it queries the corpus using a service account — because that is how applications usually access data stores. The service account can read everything.

The result is that every user now has, in effect, the entitlements of that service account, mediated by a system that is explicitly designed to find and surface relevant content. Ask about the acquisition and the model will helpfully retrieve the board pack. No control was bypassed; the architecture simply placed access control at the wrong layer.

Key idea

In a retrieval system, entitlements must be enforced at retrieval time, per requesting user. Filtering results afterwards is weaker, and restricting the interface does nothing at all. If a question offers interface-level access control and retrieval-level access control, retrieval wins.

Think of it this way

The librarian analogy again, and this is where it earns its keep. The reader shows a card at the front desk — that is authentication at the interface. But if the librarian in the stacks fetches whatever is asked for without ever checking the card, the desk was theatre. The check has to happen where the book is handed over.

In the wild

In 2025, researchers at Aim Security disclosed EchoLeak (CVE-2025-32711) in Microsoft 365 Copilot: an attacker could send a single crafted email, with instructions hidden in it, and Copilot would later pull that email into its retrieval context while answering an unrelated question — then leak internal content to an attacker-controlled destination. The victim never clicked anything. Microsoft patched it server-side and reported no exploitation in the wild. The researchers called the pattern an LLM scope violation, which is a precise name for the failure this chapter describes: untrusted content crossing a trust boundary because the retrieval layer treated it as data worth reading.

The second: the over-credentialed agent

Because indirect prompt injection cannot be reliably filtered, the security of an agent rests almost entirely on what it is permitted to do. An agent with a broad, long-lived credential and unrestricted tool access is one crafted document away from acting on an attacker's behalf. The controls are all constraints: scoped and short-lived credentials distinct from the invoking user's, an allowlist of tools rather than open access, human approval for irreversible or high-value actions, sandboxed execution, network egress restriction, and explicit limits on how much the agent can affect in one run.

Think of it this way

A "staff only" sign is not a locked door. It works on people who were going to comply anyway, and it stops nobody else. A system prompt is a sign. A scoped credential is a lock. When a question offers you one of each, take the lock.

Where this is heading

The gateway pattern is consolidating: a single policy enforcement point in front of all model traffic, doing authentication, routing, logging and cost control. Per-user entitlement enforcement at retrieval is also becoming a product feature rather than something each team builds. Both are worth waiting for rather than hand-rolling, provided you know the gap you are carrying meanwhile.

A third set of roles

You have met provider and deployer as regulatory roles. Architecture literature uses a different and complementary trio, and questions can use either vocabulary. A provider offers products or services that use AI. A producer designs, develops, tests and deploys them — the model designers and developers. A customer uses the product, directly or by passing it on to its own users. Work out which hat you are wearing for the system in front of you before deciding what you are accountable for.

Secure by design, and the point about existing weaknesses

Secure by design means building so the system reasonably resists attack from inception through end of life, rather than hardening it later. Three principles anchor it: take ownership of customer security outcomes rather than pushing configuration burden downstream; embrace radical transparency and accountability, which for AI explicitly includes explainability; and build the organisational structure and leadership to actually deliver on the first two.

Key idea

Fix what is already broken before adding AI. AI magnifies existing weakness rather than compensating for it: weak identity management becomes an over-credentialed agent, poor data governance becomes an ungovernable training corpus, absent logging becomes an uninvestigable incident. Adding AI to a shaky foundation is the fastest route to a problem you cannot unpick.

Data dependency and the input contract

A model is trained to expect data with very specific attributes, which makes it acutely sensitive to change in its inputs. An alteration to the source, the format or the preprocessing can quietly degrade it with no error anywhere. The architectural control is an explicit input contract: document exactly what the model requires, verify that production inputs match, and add preprocessing that brings data back within tolerance when it drifts outside.

Think of it this way

A model is a machine calibrated for one grade of raw material. Feed it something slightly different and it does not jam and stop — it keeps running and produces subtly worse parts, indefinitely, until someone measures the output. The input contract is the goods-inwards inspection.

Change management when a fix takes weeks

Conventional software has a patch path measured in hours. Retraining a model can take weeks, which means the usual emergency-change playbook does not apply. Two alternatives carry the load: roll back to an earlier model version, or wrap the model in tightened input and output validation that shields it while a proper fix is prepared. Both must still go through review and approval — an emergency is not an exemption from change control, and the wrapper is a compensating control with an expiry date, not a resolution.

Zero trust, extended to the model itself

The familiar components carry over — identity and access management, segmentation, encryption, data loss prevention, behaviour analytics, continuous monitoring. What is new is that zero trust for AI extends to the model's output: a decision is not assumed correct because the system produced it. Three questions establish whether trust is warranted:

  • Ability — can it perform this task reliably, evidenced by evaluation rather than asserted?
  • Integrity — does it process data without manipulation, and can that be verified?
  • Benevolence — does it adhere to do-no-harm principles in the context you are using it?
Where people go wrong

Missing how AI blurs access boundaries. Consider a supervisor who has no rights to staff records, and who is granted access to a workforce-planning model that was built on those records. They can now reach restricted data through the model, and no access control was violated in the process. An AI access policy therefore has to specify the authorisation, the duration and the type of data reachable — with role-based access, multifactor authentication, particular validation for models built on mixed-sensitivity data, and a review of access after every release.

Integrate rather than parallel

A recurring supporting task is to advise on integrating AI architecture into enterprise architecture. The point is that AI systems should be brought inside existing identity management, logging, data loss prevention, network segmentation and change management. A parallel AI stack with its own identity model and its own logging is both a security weakness and, on this exam, a finding in itself.

Chapter Twenty-one

Agents, autonomy and non-human identity

A model that answers questions is a content risk. A model that can act is an access control problem, and it is the fastest-moving part of this field.

An agent runs a loop: consider the goal, choose a tool, call it, observe the result, decide what to do next. Each pass through that loop pulls new content into the context window — the output of the tool it just called — and that content is untrusted. This is the structural reason agents amplify every problem in Chapter Thirteen: an injection that would have produced a bad sentence now produces a bad action, and then feeds its result back in to inform the next one.

Think of it this way

A model without tools is a consultant who writes you a memo. You read it, you decide, and if the memo is wrong you notice before anything happens. An agent is a new contractor with your keys, your company card and a note saying "use your judgement." The memo needed proofreading. The contractor needs a spending limit, a list of rooms they may enter, and someone who notices if they start knocking down a wall.

Autonomy is a tiering decision, not a build decision

The most useful thing a security manager can do here is force autonomy to be chosen deliberately rather than arrived at incrementally. Teams rarely decide to build a fully autonomous agent; they build one that suggests, then let it act with approval, then remove the approval because it was slowing things down.

Key idea

Limits must be enforced outside the model. An instruction in the system prompt saying "never spend more than five hundred" is a suggestion to a system that can be argued with. A hard limit in the payment API is a control. Whenever a scenario offers a prompt-level restriction and an infrastructure-level restriction, the infrastructure wins.

Non-human identity

An agent needs credentials, and how you issue them determines the blast radius of every failure. Four rules cover most of it.

The agent has its own identity, distinct from the user it is acting for and distinct from other agents. Shared service accounts make attribution impossible and make revocation an outage.

Credentials are scoped and short-lived. Scoped to the specific tools and specific resources the task needs; short-lived so a leaked token expires rather than persisting.

Delegated authority is bounded by the user's own. An agent acting on behalf of a user should never be able to do more than that user could do directly — a constraint that sounds obvious and is violated constantly, because the agent was given a broad credential for convenience.

Every tool call is logged with intent and outcome: which agent, on whose behalf, invoking what, with what parameters, producing what result. Without this you cannot investigate, and the evidence is gone by the time you know you need it.

Memory, and why it is a data store

Agents that persist memory across sessions create a target that has no equivalent in a stateless chat. Content written into memory during one interaction shapes behaviour in later ones, potentially for other users if the memory is shared. That makes memory poisoning a durable attack: plant an instruction once, have it apply repeatedly. Treat agent memory as a data store — classified, access-controlled, retained under a policy, and inspectable.

In the wild

In 2024 academic researchers demonstrated a proof-of-concept they called Morris II: a self-replicating prompt injection that spread between AI-powered email assistants. An assistant processing an infected message would exfiltrate data and reproduce the payload in its own outgoing mail, infecting the next assistant. It was a laboratory demonstration, not an incident. It is worth knowing because it shows what changes when agents talk to agents: injection stops being a per-request problem and starts behaving like a worm.

Tools, plugins and the extended supply chain

Every tool an agent can call is a component in your supply chain, whether or not procurement saw it. A tool definition describes what it does in text the model reads, which means a malicious or compromised tool description is itself an injection vector. Controls: an allowlist of approved tools rather than open registration, review of tool definitions as code, pinned versions, and the same due diligence you would apply to any third-party integration.

Where this is heading

Standardised protocols for agents to discover and call tools — and each other — are being adopted quickly, and they create exactly the surface this chapter describes: a registry of callable capabilities, described in text the model reads, invoked across organisational boundaries. Treat any such protocol adoption as a supply chain event, not an integration detail.

Multi-agent systems

When agents call other agents, two problems compound. The confused deputy pattern appears — an agent with high privilege performs an action requested by one with low privilege, and the privilege check happens at the wrong boundary. And failures cascade: an error or injected instruction propagates through the chain, with each step lending it credibility because it now arrives from a trusted internal source. Keep chains short, re-authorise at each privilege boundary rather than trusting the caller, and make the chain observable end to end.

Where people go wrong

Assuming human approval scales with agent volume. An agent that takes two hundred actions an hour cannot have each one meaningfully approved, and a reviewer clicking through them is providing no oversight at all. Either reduce the autonomy, or move the control from per-action approval to enforced envelopes plus exception alerting. Choose deliberately — do not let the approval step decay into a formality.

Chapter Twenty-two

Controlling the lifecycle

Chapter Four described how AI systems are built. This one attaches a control to each stage — which is exactly the mapping the exam asks you to perform.

The seven phases, named

The lifecycle from Chapter Six appears in the syllabus as seven named phases, worth knowing in that form: plan and design; collect and process data; build or adapt models; test, evaluate, verify and validate; make available for use; operate and monitor; and retire or decommission.

Two distinctions inside the fourth phase are examinable. Verification checks the model was built correctly to specification. Validation checks it actually serves its intended purpose with accurate, reliable output. A model can pass verification and fail validation — built exactly as specified, and the specification was wrong.

Retirement has three triggers: the solution becomes obsolete, a better alternative emerges, or ongoing risk assessment finds the risk now exceeds appetite. That third one is the one organisations miss, because nothing has broken — the appetite moved, or the risk did.

Problem definition

Before the technical stages there is a decision that is often skipped: should this be an AI system at all? Some decisions should not be automated, either because the consequences of error are unacceptable or because the affected person's right to an explanation cannot be honoured. The intake and tiering process exists partly to force this question, and "use a deterministic rules engine instead" is a legitimate treatment option that candidates rarely select.

Model selection

The evaluation criteria are more legal and evidential than technical: licence terms and what they permit; provenance of the model and, as far as it can be established, of its training data; documented evaluation and safety testing; stated limitations; update and support commitments; the provider's viability; and whether the deployment options can satisfy your data residency requirements. Selecting a model is a procurement decision with security consequences, not a benchmark comparison.

Data acquisition and preparation

Rights and lawful basis, provenance records created at the time, quality assessment, poisoning checks, classification and minimisation. For labelling, vetted vendors under appropriate contracts, quality review, and integrity protection on the resulting datasets.

Training

An isolated environment; access control over data and compute; secrets management; integrity of the pipeline code; and reproducibility — dataset version, configuration and seeds recorded so that the model can be tied back to its inputs and rebuilt if needed. Where privacy exposure is material, differential privacy can be applied during training at a measurable cost to accuracy.

Validation

Held-out test sets; adversarial and robustness testing; fairness testing across subgroups rather than in aggregate; safety and harmful-content evaluation; red teaming; and documented acceptance criteria with a recorded sign-off.

Where people go wrong

Testing performance in aggregate. A model can be highly accurate overall while performing badly for a subgroup that is a small share of the test set — and aggregate metrics will never show it. Fairness testing must be disaggregated by group, which requires having decided in advance which groups matter and how you will measure disparity.

In the wild

Amazon abandoned an experimental CV-screening tool around 2017 after finding it downgraded applications containing indicators associated with women, having learned from a decade of predominantly male hiring. The system is usually cited as a bias failure. It is equally a validation success: the disparity was found through testing, before the tool was relied upon. That is exactly what the release gate exists to do.

Deployment, operation, retirement

Signed artefacts and a registry with version control; staged or canary rollout; a tested rollback path; secure serving with authentication, authorisation and rate limits; configuration as code. Then in operation, the monitoring set covered in Chapter Eighteen. Then retraining under change management with re-validation before promotion, and guarding the feedback loop. Then retirement reaching every derived artefact.

The documentation set

Four artefacts worth knowing by name. A model card documents intended use, performance, limitations and evaluation results — a transparency instrument. A datasheet does the same for a dataset: composition, collection method, preprocessing, permitted uses. An AI bill of materials lists models, datasets and components, and is what lets you answer "are we affected" when a public model or library is found to be compromised. A system card documents a whole deployed system rather than a single model.

Chapter Twenty-three

Data controls

The data layer is where most AI risk originates, and where several familiar controls behave differently than expected.

Provenance and lineage. Record where every dataset came from, what rights attach, what transformations were applied, and which version fed which model. Without lineage you cannot answer a regulator, cannot respond to a discovered poisoning, and cannot prove what a model was trained on. Provenance reconstructed after the fact is not provenance.

Classification with permitted use. Covered in Chapter Eight, and it belongs here too: sensitivity and permitted-use are separate axes.

Minimisation. Remove what the model does not need before training, because there is no after.

De-identification. Pseudonymisation, anonymisation, tokenisation and redaction all reduce exposure and none of them is absolute. Re-identification risk rises when de-identified data is combined with other sources, and models are unusually good at inferring attributes that were removed. Treat de-identification as risk reduction, not elimination.

Access control and integrity. On datasets, feature stores, vector stores, model artefacts and the pipeline itself. Separate the duty of approving data for training from the duty of running training. Hash and version datasets; restrict write access; detect change.

Encryption. At rest and in transit as normal. For high-sensitivity workloads, confidential computing or trusted execution environments protect data in use, which matters when training or inference happens on infrastructure you do not fully control.

Retention and deletion. Prompts, outputs, logs, embeddings and caches all need retention rules, and deletion obligations must be traceable through every derived artefact.

In the wild

Italy's data protection authority temporarily blocked ChatGPT in Italy in March 2023, citing concerns including the lawful basis for processing personal data in training and the absence of age verification, and later issued a substantial fine. The service returned after changes. The lesson for a security manager: the question that stopped the service was not "is it secure" but "on what legal basis was this data used" — a question that must be answered before training, because it cannot be retrofitted afterwards.

Where this is heading

Confidential computing is becoming available for inference at reasonable cost, which changes a calculation that has held for years: it starts to be practical to run sensitive workloads on infrastructure you do not control without exposing the data in use. Watch this one, because it may reopen deployment options you have already ruled out.

The vocabulary of the data estate

Four artefacts that are routinely confused. A data inventory lists your data assets. A data dictionary describes each data element at the level of a single application. A business glossary sits above both, defining shared concepts so a term means the same thing everywhere. A data catalog ties them together by holding the metadata.

The five Vs characterise data feeding a training pipeline: volume, velocity, variety, veracity and value. The six dimensions of data quality are the ones to quote when asked what "good data" means: accuracy (free of error), completeness (no missing required fields), consistency (uniform formats), timeliness (current when needed), validity (follows defined rules), and uniqueness (no duplicates).

Three risks at collection

Consent — is it in place, does it match the intended use, is it current. Fitness for purpose — the data may be impeccable and still wrong for this job. And data lag — models train on historical data over weeks or months while the world moves on, creating drift between what the model learned and current reality. Retrieval is the standard mitigation for the third, supplying fresh context at query time rather than requiring retraining.

Where a dataset is unbalanced or scarce, the techniques are oversampling the minority class, undersampling the majority, and augmentation to generate additional variations. Each changes the distribution the model learns from, so each is a decision with fairness consequences rather than a neutral preprocessing step.

The access-versus-protection tension

Every AI programme runs into the same conflict: leadership wants data unlocked so the AI can use it, and the data still deserves the protection it always had. Least privilege is the resolution — applied to systems, identities and processes, not only to people. The model, the pipeline and the agent each need their own minimum access, and none should inherit a human's.

Encoding, and the data lake problem

Deep learning generally requires data to be tokenized — broken into pieces and stored in binary files — which need the same access control and encryption as any other sensitive file, a point that is routinely missed because the files look like intermediate artefacts. The same applies to vectors and the vector databases behind semantic search, where the indexes are what actually control access and therefore need protecting in their own right.

Where people go wrong

The data lake problem. Data pulled together from many sources into one aggregated store frequently arrives stripped of the access controls it carried in its original system. Everything is now in one place, readable by whatever the pipeline runs as, and the restrictions that governed it are gone. Aggregation for AI is one of the most common ways an organisation quietly loses a control it believed it still had.

Confidentiality during training

Training usually requires data to be machine-readable, and common tokenization works on cleartext, so sensitive data is often decrypted during development. Homomorphic encryption can let a model train without decrypting, at heavy computational cost. The practical answer for most organisations is compensating controls: restrict who can reach unencrypted production data, monitor sensitive data use, and apply disk-level encryption underneath for defence in depth.

What to back up, specifically

Backing up training data sourced from elsewhere is often redundant. What genuinely needs protecting is what development created: the processed and tokenized training data, the model weights, and the architecture definitions. And because generative models are non-deterministic, keep copies of the training and testing datasets alongside the performance and bias results — without them, the model cannot later be explained or defended.

Integrity, and the three tampering targets

Integrity breaks in four ways worth naming separately: data poisoning alters training data; model tampering changes weights or parameters directly; embedding tampering skews retrieval results without touching the model at all; and ordinary ETL errors corrupt data with no attacker involved. Layered defences: anomaly detection on training data, separation of training data from production data, validation and model ensembles, and hardening of the model itself.

Governing the data stages

The guiding principle is that AI data governance builds on existing data governance rather than replacing it. Three controls carry the organisation-and-preparation stage: dataflow mapping, documenting every data element and where it physically resides down to the hardware; access control review, applying least privilege to training data and the model; and data integrity monitoring, watching the cleansing and standardisation steps where silent corruption enters.

In use and storage: monitor privacy-by-design more frequently where decisions are automated, treat newly generated data with the same rigour as acquired data, apply information-flow controls that adapt to the threat environment, and classify AI output like any other data.

Where people go wrong

Destroying data on schedule when it may be evidence. If an AI process has produced unexpected results, consider whether an incident investigation is needed before anything is deleted. Retention schedules are written for ordinary operation; an anomaly suspends them. With deep learning this matters more, because data creation and deletion are not fully transparent at every stage, which is why the monitoring bar is higher than for conventional systems.

Backup, integrity and destruction

Three controls that transfer from conventional data governance with a twist in each.

Backup and recoverability now covers objects that are expensive or impossible to reconstruct. A trained model may represent months of compute; a curated and labelled dataset may represent more. Losing the training data can mean the model can never be rebuilt or explained — which turns a backup failure into a permanent compliance failure. Back up datasets, model artefacts, configuration and the evaluation evidence together, because any one of them alone is insufficient.

Integrity matters more than usual because corruption is silent here. A subtly altered dataset produces a subtly worse model, and nothing errors. Hashing, signing and immutable versioning are how you detect what you cannot see.

Secure destruction is where AI most often breaks an existing process. A deletion instruction that reaches a source repository and stops there leaves the derived estate untouched: embeddings, caches, prompt and output logs, fine-tuned variants, evaluation datasets and backups. Destruction has to be traced through the lineage, which is another reason lineage cannot be reconstructed after the fact.

Chapter Twenty-four

Privacy, ethics, trust and safety

The subtopic that most distinguishes this credential from a traditional security certification, and the one candidates most often skip. Do not.

The seven trustworthiness characteristics

Widely echoed across AI standards and worth knowing as a set: valid and reliable — it works consistently for its intended purpose; safe — it does not create unacceptable risk of harm; secure and resilient — it withstands attack and recovers; accountable and transparent — someone is responsible and information about the system is available; explainable and interpretable — outputs can be understood and justified; privacy-enhanced; and fair, with harmful bias managed.

Key idea

These characteristics conflict with each other. More explainability often means less accuracy. Differential privacy costs performance. Restricting output detail improves security and reduces transparency. There is no configuration that maximises all seven. The governance answer is not to resolve the tension technically but to make the trade-off explicitly, with the right stakeholders, and document the reasoning.

Think of it this way

Every world map lies, and mapmakers have known why for centuries: you cannot flatten a sphere while preserving area, angle and distance all at once. It is not a craftsmanship problem. So cartographers choose a projection, state which property they preserved, and accept the distortion elsewhere. Trustworthiness works the same way — and so does fairness in the next section.

NIST groups the same territory into three categories, and this is the version most likely to appear as a question. Systemic bias comes from institutional practice that quietly favours some groups. Statistical or computational bias arises when the data sample does not represent the real population. Human bias is the mental shortcuts and systematic errors in human judgement. Learn these three alongside the fuller taxonomy below — they are the compact answer.

Bias, by type

Systemic or historical bias: the world the data came from was already unequal, and the model learns that faithfully. Representation bias: some groups are under-represented in training data. Measurement bias: the proxy variable does not measure what you think — using arrests as a proxy for offending, or spend as a proxy for creditworthiness. Aggregation bias: one model fits the majority group and serves others poorly. Human bias in labelling and interpretation, including automation bias — the tendency to over-trust machine output. Deployment bias: the system is used in a context it was not designed for.

On fairness measurement, you need one insight rather than the mathematics: the common definitions — demographic parity, equalised odds, equal opportunity — are mutually incompatible in general. You cannot satisfy them all simultaneously. Therefore the organisation must choose which definition applies to a given use case, document why, and test against it. Choosing is a governance act, not a technical one.

Think of it this way

This is the map projection problem exactly. Demographic parity, equalised odds and equal opportunity are Mercator, Peters and Robinson. Asking which is the fair one is asking which map is the true one. The professional answer is to choose deliberately, say which property you preserved, and be able to defend the choice.

In the wild

The long-running argument over the COMPAS recidivism tool is the clearest public illustration of the impossibility result. Journalists showed the tool produced different error patterns across racial groups; the developer showed it was equally well calibrated across those same groups. Subsequent academic work established that both claims were true and that no algorithm could satisfy both criteria at once except in degenerate cases. There was no correct configuration waiting to be found — only a choice that someone had to make and defend.

Human oversight, and when it is real

Three modes, matched to impact. Human in the loop: a person approves each action before it takes effect, appropriate for consequential or irreversible decisions. Human on the loop: a person monitors and can intervene, appropriate for reversible, lower-impact decisions at volume. Human in command: a person decides whether the system is used at all and can shut it down.

Where people go wrong

Accepting the existence of a reviewer as evidence of oversight. Oversight is only a control if the reviewer has enough information to judge, enough time to judge it, the competence to understand it, the authority to override, freedom from automation bias — and if overrides actually occur sometimes. A queue of approvals cleared in two seconds each satisfies none of those conditions while appearing, in a control matrix, to be fully implemented.

Think of it this way

This is the "I have read and agree to the terms" checkbox. Legally it records consent; in reality nobody read anything. An approval queue cleared at two seconds a decision is the same artefact — a control that generates evidence of a judgement that never happened.

Where this is heading

Content provenance standards for signing and labelling synthetic media are being adopted by camera makers, platforms and model providers. Expect provenance metadata on published content to shift over a few years from differentiator to expectation, and eventually to obligation in some jurisdictions.

Agency, and the two loop patterns

Safety changes character once a system can act rather than only advise. AI agency — the ability to decide and act independently — raises four questions a security manager should be able to answer for any autonomous deployment: who is accountable for the consequences and to what extent; what keeps the system acting within bounds when nobody is watching; what happens when its decision conflicts with the user's values; and what explanation users are entitled to when even the designers cannot fully interpret it.

Two supervision patterns are easy to confuse and are examined as a pair. Human in the loop designs the workflow to pause and require explicit human approval before continuing — a clinician signing off an automated recommendation before it is acted on. AI in the loop inverts the relationship: the AI assists and augments a human decision while the person stays in control throughout. HITL deliberately slows the system down, which is why it is reserved for high-stakes decisions rather than applied everywhere.

Where synthetic media is the concern, know the mechanism: many deepfakes come from a generative adversarial network, where two models compete — one producing fakes, one detecting them — each improving the other until the output defeats human judgement. That competitive dynamic is precisely why detection is structurally the losing side of the race.

Transparency and redress

Transparency controls: disclosing that a person is interacting with AI; labelling synthetic content, including watermarking and provenance metadata; explaining automated decisions to those affected; publishing intended use and limitations; and providing a redress mechanism — a route by which a person can contest an outcome and have it reviewed. Redress is the element most often missing from an otherwise complete answer, and it is the point of the whole transparency cluster: information without recourse is not accountability.

Consent is specific, and rights persist

The privacy point that AI most often breaks is that consent is specific to the purpose it was given for. Data collected so a service can be delivered does not thereby become available for training a model. Using it for a new purpose requires fresh, explicit consent for that purpose — and "we already had the data" is not a lawful basis.

The rights individuals hold must persist across every use of their data: the right to be informed about collection and use, to access what is held, to rectification of errors, to be forgotten, and to restrict processing. Chapter Five explained why the fourth of those is so hard once data is in weights — which is exactly why the decision about whether it goes in is a privacy decision made before training, not a compliance problem discovered after.

Where a third-party model processes personal data, two artefacts follow automatically: a data processing agreement with the provider, and a privacy assessment of the arrangement.

Where people go wrong

The development-versus-production gap, which is the classic AI privacy breach and almost never involves an attacker. Personal data is tightly controlled in the system that collected it. A copy is then handed to a development team to build a model, and it comes to rest in a development environment with looser controls — or on an unsecured, internet-facing storage location. The original collection was impeccable. The reuse is what caused the breach. Any time data crosses from production into development for AI work, that crossing is the control point.

Ethics as a managed programme

Two capabilities make AI ethics distinctive rather than a general corporate concern: automatic decision-making and self-learning. Both mean the system acts with some independence, and independence at scale is what converts an ethical lapse into widespread harm.

Four strategies keep it manageable. Review the intended purpose against your own context, applicable law and societal norms — the same system can be appropriate in one setting and not another. Ensure genuine diversity and collaboration in development across demographics, departments and disciplines, because it determines which problems get noticed at all. Gather benchmarking data from comparable projects. And build feedback mechanisms so users can surface unexpected harms early.

The concrete controls: a human in the loop over consequential decisions; an AI ethics and compliance committee; an AI code of conduct, created and then regularly re-evaluated rather than published once; documented evidence that systems adhere to standards covering health, safety, law, environment and fundamental rights; demonstrable transparency, fairness and bias management; and an assessment of environmental impact, which appears in this syllabus as an ethics obligation rather than a sustainability footnote.

Safety controls

Content filtering on input and output; defined refusal behaviour; escalation paths for crisis-adjacent content; scope restriction so the system declines work it was not designed for; confidence thresholds that fall back to a human; rate limits on consequential actions; and safety evaluation before deployment rather than after complaints.

Chapter Twenty-five

Explainability, and proving a decision

One supporting task requires risk-based human oversight covering trust and safety, quality, explainability and robustness. Explainability is the one candidates can define and cannot use.

Start with the distinction the exam expects. Interpretability is a property of the model: the mechanism itself can be read and understood. A scorecard that adds points for each factor is interpretable — the explanation and the model are the same object. Explainability is a property of an account given afterwards: the model is opaque, and a separate technique reconstructs a plausible reason for a particular output.

Think of it this way

An interpretable model is an engine behind glass — you watch the parts move and you know why it turned. An explainable model is an engine in a sealed box with a mechanic who listens to it and tells you why they think it stalled. The mechanic is often right and occasionally confidently wrong, and you have no way to check from outside.

Global and local explanations answer different questions

A global explanation describes what the model does in general — which factors matter most across all decisions. It is what you need for validation, for documentation, and for answering a regulator's question about how a system works.

A local explanation describes one particular output — why this applicant, this transaction, this claim. It is what you need to answer an affected individual, and it is the harder one to produce reliably. Techniques include local approximations around a single decision, attributions distributing the outcome across input features, and counterfactuals: what would have needed to be different for the outcome to change.

Counterfactuals deserve a note, because they are frequently the most useful form for the person affected. "Your application would have succeeded with two more years at your current address" is actionable in a way that a list of feature weights is not. It is also the form most closely aligned to redress: it tells someone what to contest or change.

Where people go wrong

Treating a language model's own account of its reasoning as an explanation. Ask a generative model why it produced an answer and it will produce a fluent, plausible rationale — generated the same way the answer was, not derived from the computation that produced it. It may be accurate. It is not evidence, and it should never be offered to a regulator or an affected person as the basis of a decision.

In the wild

In 2019 a widely shared complaint alleged that the Apple Card, issued with Goldman Sachs, offered a husband a far higher credit limit than his wife despite her stronger financial position. New York's financial regulator investigated and did not find unlawful discrimination. The reputational damage happened anyway — because when customers asked why, neither the bank's staff nor its public statements could give an account of the individual decisions. The absence of a usable local explanation was itself the incident.

Where this is heading

Mechanistic interpretability — reading what is actually happening inside a network rather than approximating it from outside — is advancing quickly in research. It is not yet something you can buy or rely on for a regulatory explanation. If it matures, the trade-off drawn in the diagram above becomes considerably less painful.

The black box problem, and documenting what you do not know

Opacity runs deepest in deep learning and neural networks, where the reasoning is distributed across millions of weighted connections and simply cannot be read out. That is the black box problem, and it does something uncomfortable to a security professional's normal method: you cannot fully enumerate the failure modes, so you cannot fully control them.

The response is not to pretend otherwise. Document the data flows and the model design in detail — and then, wherever the system is opaque, document precisely what is and is not known about that opacity. An AI risk assessment run alongside the privacy assessment should identify where black-box behaviour might occur and set out a plan for remediating it if it does.

Key idea

Writing down an unknown converts it from an invisible hazard into a managed risk. You have not removed the opacity; you have made it visible, traceable and assignable to an owner. This is one of the few places in security where the documentation is the control rather than evidence of one.

From deterministic to experimental

Traditional software development is non-experimental: anything unexplained is a defect, and defects are fixed before release. AI does not permit that stance. A system that adapts and learns can produce outcomes nobody anticipated and nobody can fully account for, which shifts the whole risk-acceptance posture from deterministic to experimental.

Where people go wrong

Carrying the deterministic instinct into AI governance and treating every unexplained behaviour as a bug awaiting a fix. That framing produces two failures: indefinite delay while someone tries to explain the unexplainable, or quiet acceptance once the pressure to ship wins. The professional stance is neither — it is to state the residual uncertainty explicitly, bound it with monitoring and oversight, and have the accountable owner accept it knowingly.

What explainability is actually for

Four purposes, and they need different things.

PurposeWhat it requires
Regulatory obligationA meaningful account of the logic involved, provided to the affected person in terms they can act on. Feature weights are usually not sufficient
Human oversightEnough insight for the reviewer to judge whether this output is sound — otherwise oversight is rubber-stamping
Debugging and validationGlobal behaviour, failure modes, and evidence that the model is not relying on a spurious correlation
Incident investigationThe decision log: model version, inputs, retrieved context, configuration and output, captured at the time

That last row is the one security managers control most directly, and the one most often absent. When something goes wrong, the reconstructable record of what the system saw and did is worth more than any post-hoc technique.

Key idea

Explainability is a design constraint, not a feature added afterwards. If a use case will require decisions to be explained to individuals, that requirement belongs in model selection — and may make an interpretable model the correct choice even at some cost in accuracy. Discovering the obligation after deployment leaves you with no good options.

Chapter Twenty-six

Monitoring and operations

Default AI deployments log almost nothing useful. Knowing what should be captured, and what patterns to watch for, is directly examinable.

What to log

Prompts and responses — with privacy controls on the logs themselves, since they will contain whatever users typed. Model identity, version and the configuration used. Retrieval events: which documents were returned, for which user. Tool and agent invocations and their results. The identity of the invoking user and any delegated identity. Guardrail triggers and refusals. Confidence scores and any human override decisions. Token and cost consumption.

That last one is not merely financial. Consumption is a security signal.

What to watch for

In the wild

A parcel delivery firm disabled part of its customer service chatbot in January 2024 after a customer induced it to swear and to compose a poem criticising the company, then posted the exchange. No data was lost and no system was breached. The entire impact was reputational, it arrived within hours, and the first the organisation knew of it was social media — which is an argument for output monitoring rather than for waiting to be told.

Thresholds that trigger something

Drift monitoring is only a control if a defined number causes a defined action. Set quantitative thresholds in advance — accuracy falling below a stated level, error rate above one, subgroup disparity beyond a limit — and attach an automatic consequence: trigger retraining, force a review, or fall back to the manual path. A dashboard that shows degradation to nobody in particular is monitoring in name only.

Drift also has external causes worth naming when you explain it upward: changing social norms, new regulation, and shifting economic conditions all move the world out from under a model that has not changed at all.

Metric families for the monitoring programme itself

Beyond the model, measure whether the programme works: preparedness (update compliance, identification of high-risk vulnerabilities); shadow activity (discovered tools and inventory completeness); intrusion attempts (counts and source analysis); data-loss-prevention effectiveness (prevention ratio, response time); awareness effectiveness (engagement and actual behaviour change, not completion rates); and AI solution performance itself — false-positive rate, drift frequency, and response latency.

Where AI genuinely helps the defender

The syllabus expects the positive case as well as the cautionary one. Four areas where AI meaningfully strengthens defence:

  • Network and endpoint defence — automated log analysis that pairs an error with a likely cause and a suggested fix; detection of stealthy persistent malware by its command patterns; and phishing and spam filtering trained on safe versus suspicious messages.
  • Behavioural analysis — learning what normal activity looks like for a user or system so that deviations surface early. This is the technique that catches what signature-based tooling structurally cannot.
  • Decision support — prioritising vulnerabilities across volumes no team can triage manually, selecting among thousands of possible remediations, and supporting zero-trust enforcement decisions.
  • Threat detection and training — models trained on historical breaches spotting new ones faster, and generating synthetic examples of novel attacks to train other defensive systems.

The discipline that keeps this from becoming a single point of failure is unglamorous and familiar: genuine due diligence on the tooling, ongoing monitoring, repeated rather than one-off risk assessment, strong controls around the tool itself, awareness training, and continuous watch for emerging risk.

Threat intelligence for AI

Monitoring tells you what is happening in your systems. Threat intelligence tells you what is happening to everyone else, and for AI the useful sources are not the ones your existing programme subscribes to. Worth tracking: vulnerability disclosures affecting model-serving and orchestration frameworks; advisories from your model providers, including model deprecations and behaviour changes; public AI incident registers, which are increasingly cited by regulators and researchers; adversarial technique knowledge bases; and reports of malicious artefacts on public model hubs.

Feed it back into three places specifically: your reassessment triggers, your evaluation and red-team suites, and the scenarios you tabletop. Intelligence that arrives and does not change any of those is being collected rather than used.

What awareness training has to contain

An annual slideshow does not address the human risks that technical controls cannot reach — shadow AI and information leakage above all. An effective programme covers security, privacy and ethics together; explains what the AI is actually for, so people can judge misuse; teaches staff to interpret model output, ideally through hands-on exercises using outputs that look authoritative and are wrong; and builds the ability to notice bias in data and results. Round it out with copyright and intellectual property, explainability and the black-box problem, model drift, adversarial techniques, and — the component most often absent — a defined route for staff to give feedback when something looks wrong.

The skills gap is a governance problem

Adoption decisions are usually made without weighing the skills needed to support them, and the resulting gap is real. Three responses. Recruit and develop people whose backgrounds reflect the users the AI will affect, because that materially affects which problems get noticed. Train existing staff for responsible practice, with deeper technical training for security and development teams. And set realistic experience expectations — commercial AI is young, and advertising for a decade of experience in it is a way of not hiring anyone. Fold the skills requirement into the governance programme and the security architecture rather than waiting for a market that does not yet exist.

Control types, mapped

Preventive: access control on training data, input validation, guardrails, least-privilege agents, tool allowlists, procurement gates. Detective: output monitoring, drift detection, evaluation regression tests, audit logging. Corrective: model rollback, retraining, guardrail tuning, corpus cleanup. Deterrent: acceptable use policy, watermarking. Compensating: human review where technical controls cannot reach. Directive: policy, standards, training.

Where this is heading

Autonomous defence is arriving: agents that triage, investigate and act in the security operations centre. Note the symmetry, because it is the whole point — an agent with the standing access a SOC requires is the highest-privilege agent in the organisation, and every constraint in the agents chapter applies to it with more force, not less.

AI in the security function itself

The syllabus is not only about securing AI — it also covers leveraging AI within security operations, and reviewing AI security tools as part of the program. Expect questions on the benefits and the caveats: reduced analyst load against false positives and limited explainability; automation of triage against the excessive agency risk that applies to a security agent exactly as it applies to any other.

Key idea

The security function is not exempt from its own AI policy. A detection tool that ingests sensitive logs into a third-party model is subject to the same due diligence, the same inventory entry and the same oversight requirements as anything the business deploys. Questions sometimes test exactly this by making security the one cutting corners.

Chapter Twenty-seven

The AI security toolchain

A supporting task asks you to review and implement AI security tools as part of the information security program. Knowing the categories, and their limits, is enough.

You are not expected to know products. You are expected to know what kinds of control can be bought, where each one sits, and — the part questions actually test — what each one does not do.

What each category is genuinely good for

Discovery and posture management answers "what do we have," which is the prerequisite for everything else. It finds unapproved tools, AI features enabled inside software you already own, and models pulled into development environments. Its limit: it finds usage, not risk. An inventory entry with no owner and no tier is a list, not governance.

An AI gateway or proxy gives you a single enforcement point in front of model calls — authentication, rate and cost limits, logging, and policy on which models may be used for which data. It is the highest-leverage single control for organisations consuming models by API, because it converts a scattered problem into a chokepoint. Its limit: it only sees traffic routed through it, so it must be paired with discovery.

Guardrails and output inspection classify inputs and outputs against policy — blocking prompt injection attempts, filtering harmful content, running data loss prevention across responses. Useful and necessary. Its limit is the one from Chapter Thirteen: filtering cannot fully solve injection, and treating guardrails as the primary defence rather than one layer is the recurring architectural error.

Artefact scanning and model registries address the supply chain — scanning model files for unsafe serialisation and embedded code, signing and versioning artefacts, controlling which models may be loaded. Its limit: a scan proves the file is not obviously malicious, not that the model is not backdoored.

Evaluation and red-team platforms run adversarial, bias and safety test suites, ideally in the deployment pipeline so a failing model does not ship. This is what turns "validation is a control gate" from a principle into a mechanism. Its limit: a test suite measures what it was written to measure, and the threat landscape moves.

Observability and tracing captures the record described in Chapter Twenty-two — prompts, versions, retrieval events, tool calls, overrides. Its limit is a privacy one: the trace store now contains everything users typed, and needs classification and retention rules of its own.

Data lineage and vector store access control is the least glamorous category and often the one that closes the largest gap, because it is where the retrieval entitlement failure gets fixed.

Where this is heading

This category is consolidating fast: discovery, gateway, guardrails and observability are being folded into single platforms, and several will be absorbed into existing security suites. Favour tools that produce portable evidence and enforce at a documented policy boundary, because those survive a vendor being acquired or a category collapsing.

How to review a tool before buying it

  1. Which layer does it defend, and what is left uncovered? Tools are frequently bought in overlapping pairs while a layer sits bare.
  2. Does it see everything, or only what is routed to it? Coverage is usually the binding constraint, not capability.
  3. What does it do to your data? Most of these tools are themselves AI systems processing your prompts and documents. They belong in the inventory, go through vendor due diligence, and are subject to the same policy as anything else.
  4. Does it produce evidence? A control that blocks something without a retrievable record is hard to assure and useless in an investigation.
  5. What is the failure mode? If the tool is unavailable, does the AI system fail open or closed — and is that the behaviour you chose?
Where people go wrong

Buying tooling in place of governance. A gateway with no policy behind it enforces nothing in particular; a guardrail with no defined threshold is a setting somebody picked. Tools implement decisions — they cannot substitute for making them. When a scenario offers "deploy an AI security platform" against "define what the organisation permits," the second is nearly always the better first step.

Chapter Twenty-eight

Frameworks, standards and regulation

Six instruments matter, and each answers a different question. Learn them by the question they answer, not by their contents.

NIST AI Risk Management Framework

Voluntary, outcome-based, and the most widely referenced structure for organising AI risk work. Four functions. Govern is cross-cutting — culture, policies, accountability, workforce — and it runs through the other three rather than preceding them. Map establishes context: the use case, stakeholders, impacts, capabilities and limitations. Measure analyses, benchmarks and monitors risk using both quantitative and qualitative methods. Manage prioritises, treats, responds to incidents and allocates resources.

There is a companion profile for generative AI that enumerates risks specific to that class of system — including confabulation, information integrity, information security, data privacy, harmful bias, intellectual property, and value chain integration — with suggested actions against each. Know that it exists and what it covers.

Reach for the RMF when a scenario asks how to organise AI risk management systematically. It is a structure for work, not a certification.

Think of it this way

Three instruments, three different things. The NIST framework is the training programme — how you learn to do the work. ISO 42001 is the certificate on the wall saying an assessor watched you do it properly. The EU AI Act is the law that decides whether you are allowed to practise at all, and what happens if you get it wrong.

ISO/IEC 42001

A certifiable management system standard for AI, structured like ISO 27001 — plan, do, check, act, with the familiar clause pattern and an annex of controls. It requires context and leadership, an AI policy, defined roles, AI risk assessment and treatment, AI system impact assessment, lifecycle management, requirements on third parties, operation, monitoring, internal audit, management review and improvement.

Reach for 42001 when the scenario asks how to demonstrate systematic management to an outside party — a customer, a regulator, a board. It is also the natural thing to look for when assessing a vendor, with the caveat from Chapter Thirteen: check that the certificate's scope actually covers the AI service.

Related standards worth knowing by function alone: 23894 gives AI risk management guidance aligned to ISO 31000; 22989 is terminology; 5338 covers AI lifecycle processes; 42005 addresses impact assessment; 24028 covers trustworthiness; and 27001 and 27002 remain the information security baseline that AI controls extend rather than replace.

COBIT, mapped to the AI life cycle

This is ISACA's own governance framework, so its vocabulary shapes how questions are phrased. Its five domains map onto AI work directly. Evaluate, Direct and Monitor is the governance layer — appetite, direction, oversight — and sits above the other four, which are management. Align, Plan and Organise shapes strategy and policy. Build, Acquire and Implement covers development, procurement, integration and testing. Deliver, Service and Support runs operations. Monitor, Evaluate and Assess drives continuous checking against performance, compliance and risk objectives.

The distinction COBIT insists on — governance evaluates, directs and monitors; management plans, builds, runs and monitors — is worth carrying into the exam. It is the same separation that makes "the CISO accepts the risk" a wrong answer.

The Four Pillars: documenting a model honestly

A framing from the Cloud Security Alliance that assembles four artefacts into a continuous learning loop:

  • Model card — the model's objective, training data, capabilities and limits.
  • Data sheet — the training dataset, including its known biases and ethical concerns.
  • Risk card — identified risks, their analysis, and the mitigations planned against them.
  • Scenario planning — deliberate imagining of misuse and malfunction, to surface risks the first three did not.

The risk card and scenario planning are the two most organisations lack. Scenario planning in particular is the only one of the four that looks for the unknown rather than documenting the known, which makes it the one that catches novel failure modes.

Other instruments worth recognising by name

ISO/IEC 23053 describes a generic AI system built with machine learning — useful as a shared vocabulary for system decomposition. IEEE 7000 gives a process for addressing ethical concerns during system design, which is the "how" behind the ethics-by-design principle. Singapore's Model AI Governance Framework is a widely cited practical implementation guide. And the OECD AI Principles remain the source of most of the trustworthy-AI vocabulary that later appears in binding law.

The EU AI Act

Risk-based regulation with extraterritorial reach, structured like product safety law rather than like data protection law. Obligations attach to the use case, not to the technology.

Four tiers. Unacceptable practices are prohibited outright. High-risk is the heavy tier, covering AI as a safety component of regulated products plus a list of use cases — biometrics, critical infrastructure, education, employment, essential services and creditworthiness, law enforcement, migration, and justice. Obligations there are extensive: a risk management system, data governance, technical documentation, logging, transparency, human oversight, accuracy and robustness and cybersecurity, quality management, conformity assessment, registration, post-market monitoring and serious incident reporting. Transparency risk carries disclosure duties — telling people they are dealing with AI, marking synthetic content. Minimal risk carries no specific obligations.

General-purpose models sit on a separate track with their own obligations around documentation, copyright policy and training data, with additional requirements for models judged to present systemic risk. There is also an AI literacy obligation on providers and deployers regarding their own staff.

Key idea

The roles are provider, deployer, importer, distributor and authorised representative — and the boundary between the first two is examinable. A deployer that substantially modifies a system, puts its own name on it, or changes its intended purpose becomes a provider, inheriting the full provider obligation set. Fine-tuning and rebranding a third-party model is the classic trigger.

Implementation timelines have been subject to amendment since the Act entered into force. Learn the structure — tiers, roles, obligation categories — which is stable, and verify current dates before you sit.

OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications

An application-layer weakness list: prompt injection, sensitive information disclosure, supply chain, data and model poisoning, improper output handling, excessive agency, system prompt leakage, vector and embedding weaknesses, misinformation, and unbounded consumption. Reach for it when the question is what to test or assess in an application.

MITRE ATLAS

An ATT&CK-style knowledge base of adversary tactics and techniques against machine learning systems, grounded in documented real-world cases. Reach for it when the question is how an adversary would proceed — threat modelling, red team planning, detection engineering.

Everything else you may see

NIST SP 800-53 and the Cybersecurity Framework as the general control baseline. The OECD AI Principles, which are the source of much of the trustworthy-AI vocabulary that later appears in regulation. ISACA's own COBIT, whose governance-versus-management distinction and goals cascade the exam's phrasing sometimes echoes, and the Digital Trust Ecosystem Framework. And sector rules — model risk management guidance in financial services, medical device regulation, employment law — which predate AI-specific legislation and continue to apply on top of it.

Chapter Twenty-nine

Where this is going

The exam tests today's syllabus, and this chapter is not on it. It is here because a control set you understand only as a list goes stale, while one you understand as a response to a moving problem keeps working.

Everything below is written with a stated confidence level, because forecasting in this field has an unimpressive record. Some of it is already happening and merely unevenly distributed. Some is a reasonable extrapolation. Some is widely predicted and, in my view, unlikely — and knowing which is which is more useful than a longer list.

The centre of gravity has already moved twice

The research literature that produced most of the attack names in Chapter Thirteen was written when the model was the system: you had a classifier, someone tried to fool it or steal it, and the controls attached to the model itself. Then applications wrapped models in retrieval, tools and user interfaces, and the interesting failures moved outward — into what the system could be made to say and reveal. Now they are moving outward again, into what the system can be made to do.

The practical consequence is that security effort is migrating from data science towards identity, authorisation and workflow design. If you are deciding where to build capability, that is the direction of travel. The organisations that will handle agentic AI well are the ones that were already good at least privilege and delegated authority, not the ones with the best model expertise.

Key idea

Nothing gets retired as the centre of gravity moves. Poisoning did not stop mattering because agents became interesting; it stopped being discussed. This is a real risk for anyone learning the field from current commentary rather than from a syllabus — you end up fluent in the newest layer and blank on the ones underneath it, which is precisely the gap this exam is built to find.

Five things worth planning for

1 · Non-human identity becomes a first-class problem. Most organisations today issue agents credentials the way they issued service accounts in 2010 — broad, long-lived, shared, and attributed to nobody. That does not survive contact with agents acting on behalf of individual users at volume. Expect delegated-authority tokens, per-agent identity, short lifetimes and agent registries to become ordinary infrastructure. The work you can do now is unglamorous and durable: get every agent off shared credentials and make delegated authority bounded by the user's own.

2 · Discovery replaces procurement as how you learn what you have. The assumption behind every intake process is that new capability arrives through a gate. Increasingly it arrives through an update to software you bought years ago, enabled by default. Intake still matters, but it stops being sufficient, and continuous discovery becomes the primary instrument of the inventory. Organisations that treat their inventory as a document rather than a feed will find it is wrong within a quarter.

3 · Point-in-time assurance stops matching the object. Certifying a system whose model changes underneath it is a structural mismatch, and it is already visible in the awkwardness of applying conventional audit to AI. Expect movement towards continuous assurance: automated evidence, evaluation suites running in the deployment pipeline, and attestations that expire quickly. This is also where an independent AI assurance market seems likely to form, in roughly the way financial audit did — which would be good for the profession and would put real weight on the evidence trail discussed in Chapter Ten.

4 · Verification moves from perception to provenance. Chapter Fourteen's argument only gets stronger. As synthetic audio and video become ordinary rather than remarkable, controls that depend on recognising a person will keep failing, and the response will be procedural and cryptographic rather than perceptual: out-of-band verification as standard practice, signed and provenance-marked content, stronger identity proofing at onboarding and recovery. Detection tooling will keep improving and will keep being the weaker half of the answer.

5 · Inference moves inside the boundary. Smaller, cheaper, more capable models running on your own infrastructure — or on the endpoint itself — change the data question materially. Some of today's hardest vendor problems dissolve when the model is local. New ones appear: model artefacts distributed across endpoints, update integrity at scale, and capability in places your governance never had to reach.

Where this is heading

The most likely near-term shift in the exam's own subject matter is that agent governance becomes its own domain rather than a sub-topic — autonomy tiering, delegated authority, agent registries and inter-agent trust. If you are learning this material now, that is the area where extra depth is most likely to still be worth something in three years.

Three things to be sceptical about

That prompt injection will be solved. Filtering, classifiers and instruction-hierarchy training all keep improving, and each release makes attacks harder. But the structural fact from Chapter Thirteen — one channel, no reliable authority markers — has not changed, and every mitigation so far has been probabilistic. Design as though it will not be solved. If it is, you will have over-invested in least privilege, which is not a serious failure mode.

That detection will win the synthetic media race. The detector is structurally the slower party: it must generalise to methods that do not exist yet, while the generator only has to defeat detectors that already do. Provenance and procedure are the durable answers.

That the regulatory picture will settle soon. Timelines have already shifted once and obligations may be re-scoped again. This is an argument for building to the underlying duties rather than to the calendar: documentation, human oversight, provenance, transparency and redress appear in every serious framework and survive redrafting. An organisation that can evidence those will be able to satisfy whatever the final text requires; one that built to a specific deadline will not.

Where people go wrong

Treating the horizon as a reason to defer. "We will wait until the regulation settles" and "we will wait for the agent standards to mature" both sound prudent and both produce the same outcome: an unmapped estate of AI systems that were adopted anyway, by people who needed to get work done. Every durable control in this book — inventory, ownership, tiering, least privilege, evidence — is worth building before the picture clarifies, because none of them depend on which way it clarifies.

  2. Why does continuous discovery become more important than intake over time?
  3. Name the five duties that survive whatever happens to specific regulatory timelines.

01A business unit has begun using a generative AI assistant embedded in an already-approved SaaS platform. Security was not consulted. What should the security manager do FIRST?
  • ABlock the feature at the network perimeter
  • BDetermine what data the feature processes and its business purpose
  • CEscalate the policy violation to the executive committee
  • DRequire the business unit to complete awareness training
B — Understand before acting. You cannot judge whether to block, approve with conditions, or escalate until you know the data and the purpose. This is the classic FIRST pattern.
02Which BEST reduces the risk of sensitive data leakage when deploying an LLM over confidential internal documents?
  • AFine-tuning the model on the documents so no external calls are needed
  • BEnforcing the requesting user's entitlements at the retrieval step
  • CA system prompt instructing the model to refuse confidential requests
  • DEncrypting the vector store at rest
B — Access control belongs where the data is retrieved. Fine-tuning makes leakage worse by embedding data in weights. A system prompt is not a security boundary. Encryption at rest does not stop an authorised query returning data the user should not see.
03An AI vendor silently updates the underlying model. The MOST significant risk is that:
  • ALicensing costs may increase
  • BPreviously validated behaviour and control effectiveness may no longer hold
  • CLatency may increase
  • DThe vendor may change sub-processors
B — Everything you validated — accuracy, bias testing, guardrail behaviour — was validated against a model that no longer exists. This is why version pinning and change notice are contract requirements.
04Who should accept the residual risk of an AI system used to screen job applicants?
  • AThe chief information security officer
  • BThe AI governance committee chair
  • CThe business executive accountable for hiring
  • DThe data science lead who built the model
C — Risk is owned by the business. Security advises and facilitates; it does not accept business risk on the business's behalf.

  • ATraining users to identify phishing emails
  • BScoping the agent's credentials and requiring approval for consequential actions
  • CA more sophisticated input content filter
  • DLogging all agent actions for later review
B — Indirect prompt injection cannot be reliably filtered, so limit what the agent can do rather than what it can read. Logging is detective; the question asks for the most effective mitigation.

  • BModel inversion
  • CAn evasion attack
  • DModel extraction

  • CEncrypting model weights at rest
  • DAdversarial training

  • BApprove it with enhanced logging
  • CPresent the limitation, its regulatory and business implications, and the options to the risk owner
  • DRequire the team to switch to a linear model
C — The security manager informs the risk owner's decision. Neither unilateral rejection nor prescribing the technical solution is the security manager's call.

  • APublic, because they are numeric
  • BAt the classification of the source documents
  • CInternal by default
  • DBased on the vector database's controls

  • AQuantify financial loss exposure
  • BEvaluate effects on individuals and society, including fairness, transparency and redress

Glossary

The vocabulary the exam assumes, defined the way it is used in this syllabus rather than the way a data scientist would define it.

AI as a service
Model capability consumed from a provider rather than hosted. The consumption mode that gives you the least control and is the most common.
AI readiness
Assessment of whether data, skills, infrastructure, governance and culture can support AI. Readiness gaps determine which failure mode you get first.
ANI, AGI, ASI
Narrow, general and super intelligence. Only the first exists; the other two are, respectively, undelivered and hypothetical.
AI charter
The formal document that makes an initiative governable: objectives, scope, stakeholders, governance structure, timeline, budget, risks, success metrics and signed sign-off.
AI lead
A named individual tracking the organisation's evolving relationship with AI, working across security, privacy, legal, procurement, risk and audit. Stops AI governance from being everyone's concern and nobody's job.
Ability, integrity, benevolence
The three questions zero trust asks of a model itself: can it do the task reliably, does it process data without manipulation, does it adhere to do-no-harm principles.
AI agency
A system's capacity to decide and act independently. Raises accountability, self-regulation, value-conflict and explanation questions that advisory systems do not.
AI in the loop
AI assists and augments a human decision while the person stays in control — the inverse of human-in-the-loop, where the workflow pauses for explicit approval.
Agent
A model given tools, memory and autonomy to take actions. Shifts the security question from what the system can say to what it can do.
AI bill of materials
An inventory of the models, datasets and components in a system. What lets you answer "are we affected" when something upstream is found compromised.
AI impact assessment
An assessment of a system's effects on individuals and society — fairness, transparency, oversight and redress — distinct from a security risk assessment.
Automation bias
The human tendency to over-trust machine output. The reason a reviewer's presence is not by itself evidence of oversight.
Black box problem
Results that cannot be explained because the reasoning is distributed across millions of weights. Managed by documenting what is and is not known about the opacity, not by eliminating it.
Appropriate risk
Risk worth taking to achieve an objective — as distinct from inappropriate risk, which brings no clear benefit, exceeds tolerance and threatens brand, trust or legal standing.
Backdoor
Poisoning that implants a hidden trigger, causing chosen behaviour only when the trigger appears. Invisible to ordinary evaluation.
Conformity assessment
The provider's demonstration that a high-risk system meets legal requirements, examining its quality management system and technical documentation. Repeated after substantial modification.
Consent specificity
Consent given for one purpose does not extend to another. Data collected to deliver a service does not thereby become available for training.
Concept drift
Degradation because the relationship the model learned has stopped holding. Distinct from data drift, where the inputs change distribution.
Confabulation
Fluent, confident and false output. A property of how generative models work, not a defect to be patched.
Deployer
An organisation using an AI system under its own authority. Becomes a provider on substantial modification, rebranding, or change of intended purpose.
Differential privacy
A mathematical bound on any single record's contribution to a model, limiting inversion and membership inference at a cost in accuracy.
Data lag
The gap between what a model learned during training and current reality. Retrieval mitigates it by supplying fresh context at query time instead of requiring retraining.
Data lake problem
Aggregating data from many sources into one store strips the access controls each source carried. A common way to lose a control you believed you still had.
Five Vs
Volume, velocity, variety, veracity and value — the characteristics of data feeding a training pipeline.
Embedding
A numeric vector representing content for retrieval. Not encryption — partially invertible, and classified at the sensitivity of its source.
Embedding tampering
Altering stored vectors to skew retrieval results without touching the model. A third tampering target alongside data poisoning and model tampering.
Evasion attack
A crafted input causing misclassification at inference. The model is intact; the input is adversarial.
Excessive agency
An agent holding more permission, autonomy or functionality than its task requires. The blast radius of a successful injection.
FAIR
Factor analysis of information risk — a method for expressing risk in monetary terms, so it can compete with the other numbers at a board table.
FRIA
Fundamental rights impact assessment. A legal obligation on the deployer of certain high-risk systems, completed before first use and filed with the regulator. Distinct from the provider's conformity assessment.
Development-production gap
The classic AI privacy breach: tightly controlled data copied into a looser development environment for model work. No attacker required.
Experimental risk posture
Accepting that an adaptive system can behave in ways nobody can fully account for, and bounding that uncertainty rather than treating it as a defect awaiting a fix.
Fine-tuning
Further training a base model on your own data, creating a new asset whose weights now carry that data irreversibly.
FRIA owner
The deployer. Worth memorising separately from the conformity assessment, which the provider owns.
Identify, never punish
The disclosure principle behind an accurate AI inventory. If admitting to an unapproved tool triggers discipline, the inventory records only what was already visible.
Gold image
A trusted baseline model or dataset held as a known-good reference for comparison and rollback, particularly before fine-tuning.
Generative adversarial network
Two models competing — one producing fakes, one detecting them — each improving the other. The mechanism behind many deepfakes, and why detection is structurally the losing side.
Guardrails
Input and output filtering and policy enforcement wrapped around a model. Compensating controls, never a substitute for architecture.
Hyperparameter
A setting chosen before training rather than learned from data. Part of the configuration recorded for reproducibility.
Human in command
A person decides whether the system is used at all and can shut it down. The strongest oversight mode.
Human in the loop
A person approves each action before it takes effect. For consequential or irreversible decisions.
Human on the loop
A person monitors and can intervene. For reversible, lower-impact decisions at volume.
Indirect prompt injection
Malicious instructions hidden in content the model reads on a user's behalf — a document, a web page, an email. The attacker never touches your interface.
Input contract
Documented specification of the data attributes a model requires, verified against production inputs. Guards against silent degradation when a source or format changes.
Incident threshold
The predefined point at which a wrong output stops being expected error and becomes an incident. Without one, AI incident detection has no line to cross.
Inference
Running a trained model to produce output. Where runtime attacks land.
Membership inference
Determining whether a particular record was in the training set — which can itself be the disclosure.
Least privilege for systems
The principle applied to models, pipelines and agents rather than only to people. Each needs its own minimum access, and none should inherit a human's.
MAESTRO
A threat modelling method built for AI, addressing multi-agent environments, adversarial machine learning and system autonomy — where STRIDE struggles.
Model card
Documentation of a model's intended use, performance, limitations and evaluation. A transparency and accountability artefact.
Model extraction
Repeated querying to reconstruct a functionally equivalent model. A volume attack, countered by volume controls.
Model inversion
Reconstructing training data from access to a model. Steals the data, where extraction steals the capability.
Nth party
Vendors beyond the fourth and fifth tiers of the supply chain, down to power utilities and data-centre facilities. Rarely mapped, occasionally the shared point of failure.
Overfitting
A model has memorised its training data including the noise. A reliability problem and a confidentiality one, since memorisation is what makes extraction and membership inference work.
Poisoning
Injecting malicious or corrupted data into a training corpus. A training-time attack, so runtime filtering cannot address it.
Problem families
Classification, regression, clustering, dimensionality reduction, anomaly detection, recommendation and sequential decision-making. If a proposal does not match one, question whether AI is the right tool.
Producer
In architectural terms, the party that designs, develops, tests and deploys an AI product — distinct from the provider who offers it and the customer who uses it.
Provenance
The recorded origin, rights and transformation history of a dataset or model. Must be captured at collection; cannot be reconstructed later.
Provider
An organisation that develops an AI system or places it on the market under its own name. Carries the heaviest regulatory obligations.
RAG
Retrieval-augmented generation. Documents are fetched at query time and placed in the model's input, keeping data outside the weights and access-controllable per user.
Reinforcement learning
Learning by trial and error against rewards. Carries a distinctive failure mode: optimising the reward given rather than the outcome intended.
Risk card
Documentation of a model's identified risks, their analysis and planned mitigations. The artefact most often missing from a documentation set.
Security for AI / AI for security
Protecting AI systems themselves, versus using AI to strengthen defence. A complete programme covers both directions.
Steering committee
Cross-functional body that keeps AI initiatives aligned and makes the calls on budget, scope, risk and ethics. Typically chaired by a CIO, CTO or chief data officer.
Secure by design
Building resistance to attack in from inception: owning customer security outcomes, radical transparency and accountability, and the leadership structures to deliver both.
Quality dimensions
Accuracy, completeness, consistency, timeliness, validity and uniqueness — the six-part answer to what "good data" means.
Red teaming
Adversarial testing of a system, covering not only security but harmful content, bias and safety failures.
Redress
A route by which an affected person can contest an outcome and have it reviewed. The element most often missing from a transparency answer.
Scenario planning
Deliberately imagining misuse and malfunction to surface risks that documentation of the known will not reveal.
Shared responsibility model
The split of duties between provider and deployer. Evaluation for your use case, oversight and redress never move to the provider, whatever you buy.
Token limit
The ceiling on how much text a model holds at once. Long documents get silently truncated, which is a correctness problem the user rarely sees.
Tokenization
Breaking data into pieces for a model to process, usually stored as binary files. Those files need the same access control and encryption as the source data.
Value alignment
Designing AI to behave consistently with human values, shaped by community feedback, ethical foundations, legal compliance and continuing stakeholder engagement. Differs by organisation and country.
Verification vs validation
Verification checks the model was built to specification; validation checks it serves its intended purpose. A model can pass the first and fail the second.
Trust but verify
The organising principle of an AI security programme: every output that matters is validated by something other than the model that produced it.
Shadow AI
Unapproved AI use, arising from staff tools, vendor features switched on in existing software, and models pulled from public repositories.
System prompt
Instruction text placed before user input to shape behaviour. Configuration, not a security boundary — leakable and arguable with.
Three attack surfaces
Development-time, runtime (ordinary infrastructure weakness), and through use. The middle one is underweighted and accounts for much real-world AI compromise.
Unbounded consumption
Resource or cost exhaustion through excessive inference. Sometimes called denial of wallet.
Underfitting
A model too simple to capture the pattern. The safer of the two fitting failures, because it fails visibly.
Vector store
The database holding embeddings for retrieval. Governed at the sensitivity of the source corpus, with entitlements enforced at query time.
